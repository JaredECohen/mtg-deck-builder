In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), a lifegain deck plays cards that gain life over and over, then adds payoff cards that reward each instance of gaining life with something that actually matters: +1/+1 counters, damage to the opponent, cards or an alternate way to win. The life itself is a side effect. The deck wins because its payoffs turn a stream of small life gains into a board that grows every turn or an opponent whose life total shrinks every time yours goes up.

That distinction is the whole archetype. A pile of cards that only gains life loses to almost anything. A deck where every life gain also grows a creature or drains an opponent is a real threat. This guide covers the pieces, how to fit them together, and the mistakes that make lifegain decks feel weak.

## What a lifegain deck is made of

Most lifegain decks are built from three kinds of cards. You need all three, and the balance between them decides how the deck plays.

| Role | What it does | Classic examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enablers | Gain life repeatedly, ideally in small, frequent chunks | Soul Warden, Soul's Attendant, creatures with lifelink |
| Payoffs | Reward you whenever you gain life | Ajani's Pridemate, Heliod, Sun-Crowned, Vito, Thorn of the Dusk Rose |
| Finishers | End the game, either through damage or an alternate win | Sanguine Bond, Felidar Sovereign, Aetherflux Reservoir |

### Enablers: many small gains beat one big one

Payoffs like Ajani's Pridemate trigger once each time you gain life, not once per point of life. Gaining 1 life three times puts three counters on Pridemate. Gaining 10 life once puts one counter on it. That's why the best enablers for this deck are the ones that trigger often, not the ones that gain the most.

Soul Warden is the classic example: whenever another creature enters the battlefield, you gain 1 life. That counts every creature, including your opponent's, so in a creature-heavy game it fires constantly. Soul's Attendant does the same job.

Lifelink is the other main enabler. A creature with lifelink makes you gain life equal to the damage it deals, so every attack or block also triggers your payoffs. Lifelink creatures are especially good because they're enablers and threats at once. They attack, they gain you life, and they push damage at the same time.

Spells that gain life as a bonus belong here too. Lightning Helix deals 3 damage to any target and gains you 3 life. A card like that is fine in any deck and better in this one, because it removes a creature or hits the opponent while also triggering your payoffs.

### Payoffs: where the deck gets its power

Payoff cards are the reason to play the archetype at all. They fall into a few groups.

**Growth payoffs** put +1/+1 counters on your creatures. Ajani's Pridemate is the template: a two-mana 2/2 that gets a +1/+1 counter whenever you gain life. Heliod, Sun-Crowned puts a +1/+1 counter on a creature or enchantment you control whenever you gain life, and can give another creature lifelink, so it's both an enabler and a payoff. Growth payoffs overlap heavily with [+1/+1 counters decks](/blog/plus-one-counters-deck-guide), and many lifegain lists are partly counters decks in disguise.

**Drain payoffs** make the opponent lose life whenever you gain it. Vito, Thorn of the Dusk Rose and Sanguine Bond both say, in effect, "whenever you gain life, target opponent loses that much life." These are the cards that fix the archetype's biggest problem, because every life gain becomes damage.

**Doubling effects** multiply your gains. Rhox Faithmender, for example, makes you gain twice as much life whenever you would gain life. Doublers are strong with drain payoffs, which care about the amount, and less exciting with growth payoffs, which only care about how many times you gained life.

### Finishers and alternate wins

Some decks finish by attacking with an oversized Pridemate. Others use a card that turns a huge life total directly into a win. Felidar Sovereign is a 4/6 with flying and lifelink that wins you the game at the beginning of your upkeep if you have 40 or more life. Aetherflux Reservoir lets you pay 50 life to deal 50 damage to any target.

These cards are exciting, but treat them as one route to winning, not the only one. A deck that can only win by reaching 40 or 50 life is slow, and slow decks need to be very good at defending themselves.

## Why lifegain alone doesn't win

The most common lifegain mistake is building a deck where most of the cards gain life and very few do anything else. It feels safe, because your life total is always high. In practice it loses, for four reasons.

1. **Life doesn't change the board.** Gaining 4 life doesn't kill an attacker, counter a spell or draw a card. If your opponent is building a bigger board than you, you're just delaying the loss by a turn.
2. **It doesn't stop most other ways to lose.** You still lose if you have to draw from an empty library, if you get ten poison counters, or, in Commander, if a single commander deals you 21 combat damage. None of those care about your life total.
3. **It costs you cards.** A card that only gains life is usually a card that does nothing else. Every one of those in your deck is a slot that isn't a threat or an answer, which is a real [card advantage](/blog/card-advantage-explained) cost.
4. **It gives the opponent time.** A combo deck doesn't care how much life you have if it can win in one turn. A control deck is happy to let you gain life while it sets up. Life only matters against decks trying to reduce it with damage.

Lifegain is at its best against aggressive decks. Gaining a few life each turn while your creatures block can completely blunt a fast red deck. The [guide to beating aggro](/blog/how-to-play-against-aggro) explains why buying time works so well in that matchup. Against anything else, the life is a bonus and your payoffs have to carry you.

## Combining lifegain with pressure

The strongest lifegain decks are aggressive or midrange decks that happen to gain life, not defensive decks that hope to outlast the opponent. Think of the life as fuel for your threats.

### Build around threats that grow

Ajani's Pridemate on turn two followed by enablers on turns three and four can be a 5/5 or bigger very quickly. Add a lifelink creature and every attack grows Pridemate again. Your opponent has to answer the payoff immediately or it gets out of hand.

This also creates a useful dilemma. If they spend removal on Pridemate, your Heliod or Vito is still coming. If they ignore the payoffs to race you, your lifelink and drains make racing very hard. A good lifegain deck runs enough payoffs that losing one isn't fatal.

### Use drain to close games

Drain payoffs turn your life gain into a clock the opponent can't block. Once Vito or Sanguine Bond is on the battlefield, Soul Warden triggers become a point of damage each, and a lifelink attacker effectively hits twice. Drain is what lets a lifegain deck win through a stalled board.

### Combos the archetype has produced

Lifegain has a few well-known two-card combos. Sanguine Bond with Exquisite Blood is the classic: Exquisite Blood says that whenever an opponent loses life, you gain that much life, and Sanguine Bond turns that life gain back into life loss for the opponent. Once either one triggers, they loop until the opponent is out of life.

Heliod, Sun-Crowned with Walking Ballista is another. Give Walking Ballista lifelink with Heliod, then remove a +1/+1 counter to deal 1 damage. You gain 1 life, Heliod puts the counter back, and you repeat as many times as you like.

You don't need to build around a combo to play lifegain, but these are worth knowing, both to play and to recognize from across the table. The [combo decks guide](/blog/mtg-combo-decks-explained) covers how to fit two-card combos into a deck without warping it.

## Building a lifegain deck

A practical starting point for a 60-card lifegain deck:

- **8-12 enablers**, favoring cheap creatures that trigger often and lifelink creatures that also attack.
- **8-10 payoffs**, split between growth and drain so you have more than one way to win.
- **4-8 interaction spells**, ideally ones that also gain life, like Lightning Helix.
- **A small number of finishers**, if any. Your payoffs often are the finishers.
- **A normal land count for a low curve.** Most lifegain decks are cheap and want to cast two spells a turn by the midgame.

Keep the curve low. Enablers are at their best early, when they can trigger many times over a game. A Soul Warden played on turn six gains far less over the game than one played on turn one.

White is the core color because it has the most enablers and growth payoffs. Black adds drain payoffs and lifelink. Green adds sturdy creatures and counter synergy. Red adds burn spells that gain life. Two colors is usually plenty.

### Lifegain in Commander

Lifegain is popular in Commander because the starting life total is 40 and there are several opponents, which means more creatures entering, more triggers and more targets for drain. Drain payoffs that hit each opponent are much stronger in a four-player game than "target opponent" effects.

The same rules apply, though. Make sure your deck has a plan beyond gaining life, and watch out for commander damage, which ignores your huge life total entirely. Oloro, Ageless Ascetic is a long-standing lifegain commander, and many players build white-black or white-green lifegain decks around whichever commander fits their collection.

## Common lifegain mistakes

1. **Too many cards that only gain life.** Each one should be doing something else as well, or triggering a payoff that does.
2. **Too few payoffs.** If your payoffs are four cards in sixty, most games you won't draw one, and the deck has no engine.
3. **Valuing big gains over frequent gains.** Payoffs count triggers. Many small gains beat one large one.
4. **Leaning entirely on an alternate win.** Felidar Sovereign is a nice top end, not a game plan.
5. **Forgetting the board.** If you aren't blocking well or pressuring the opponent, your life total is just a countdown that takes a little longer.

## The takeaway

Build lifegain as a synergy deck, not a defensive one. Play cheap enablers that gain life often, plenty of payoffs that turn each gain into counters or drain, and enough pressure that your opponent has to deal with your board rather than ignore it.
