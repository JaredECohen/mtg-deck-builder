Ninth Edition is the Magic: The Gathering core set released in July 2005, the last core set printed with white borders. Like every core set of its era, it was almost entirely reprints, chosen to teach the game and to anchor Standard alongside the Kamigawa and Ravnica blocks. It brought back trample and protection to the core set, adopted the word "Aura," and reprinted cards that still matter today, from Blood Moon and Phyrexian Arena to the three Urza's lands.

## What the set was for

Ninth Edition followed Eighth Edition, which had introduced the modern card frame, and it refined the core set rather than reinventing it. Its main jobs were to give new players a clean starting point and to supply Standard with staple effects that expansions did not always print.

A few changes stand out:

- **Trample and protection returned.** Starting with Sixth Edition, both keywords had been left out of core sets as too complicated for new players. Ninth put them back, with cards like Paladin en-Vec (first strike, protection from black and red).
- **"Aura" replaced "local enchantment."** The card type that attaches to a permanent got the name we still use, and Pacifism and Blanchwood Armor were now Enchantment — Aura cards.
- **Equipment entered the core set.** Mirrodin had introduced Equipment, and Ninth was the first core set to include it, with Loxodon Warhammer and Vulshok Morningstar.
- **Creature types were modernized.** Many reprints were updated to the race-plus-class model: Samite Healer became a Human Cleric, Clone became a Shapeshifter, and old one-off types like "Hell's-Caretaker" disappeared.
- **Some old staples left.** Birds of Paradise had been in every core set since Alpha and was left out of Ninth, so it was missing from Standard for the first time until Ravnica: City of Guilds reprinted it. Walls were dropped too.

Some reprints were chosen by public vote on the official Magic website, and many cards received new art. Ninth was also the first Magic set printed in Russian. The Russian cards were black-bordered, and their popularity helped push Wizards to print core sets with black borders from Tenth Edition on, which is why Ninth is the last white-bordered core set.

There is no storyline. The flavor is a sampler of Magic's history, from Serra Angel and Shivan Dragon to Mahamoti Djinn and Sengir Vampire.

## Mechanics

Ninth Edition introduced no new mechanics. It used the evergreen keywords of the day (flying, first strike, haste, trample, protection, regeneration and others) and a handful of returning ideas that core sets often lean on.

### Painlands

The set reprinted all ten painlands, such as Shivan Reef and Adarkar Wastes. Each taps for colorless for free or for one of two colors at the cost of 1 damage to you. With both allied and enemy pairs available, Standard decks of 2005-2007 could build reliable two- and three-color mana.

### Urza's lands

Urza's Tower, Urza's Mine and Urza's Power Plant tap for a single colorless mana on their own, but when you control all three, the Tower makes three and the other two make two each, for seven mana from three lands. Ninth's reprint put "Tron" into Standard and, later, made the lands legal in Modern.

### Lords and Equipment

The core set's tribal lords returned, including Elvish Champion, Goblin King and Lord of the Undead, along with Coat of Arms, which pumps every creature that shares a type with others. Loxodon Warhammer (+3/+0, trample and lifelink) was one of the most powerful Limited cards in the set. ## Limited

Ninth Edition was drafted and played as Sealed like other core sets of its era, but you won't find the kind of detailed archetype analysis that later sets got, so treat it as a classic "good stuff" core set. The cards were simple, two-color decks were the norm, and the best plan was to take removal and efficient creatures in whatever colors were flowing.

The removal was familiar: Volcanic Hammer and Shock in red, Dark Banishing in black, Pacifism in white. Bombs like Loxodon Warhammer, Serra Angel, Shivan Dragon and Mahamoti Djinn could swing a game on their own, and Hypnotic Specter was brutal if left unanswered. Blue had fliers and bounce, and green had big bodies like Spined Wurm and Craw Wurm. If you want to practice the fundamentals, a core set like this is ideal; our [how to play Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) guide covers the basics it was built to teach.

## The meta: how it played in Constructed

Ninth Edition became legal in Standard in August 2005, joining Mirrodin block and Kamigawa block. When Ravnica: City of Guilds arrived in October 2005, Mirrodin block rotated, and Ninth spent the rest of its life next to Kamigawa block, the Ravnica block, Coldsnap and finally Time Spiral block.

- **Tron decks** used the Urza's lands to cast big spells early, in versions like blue Urzatron and Izzet-colored "Izzetron."
- **Owling Mine** was built around Howling Mine, giving both players extra cards while punishing the opponent for drawing them.
- **Solar Flare** and other control decks used Wrath of God as a reset button.
- **Dragonstorm**, a combo deck from the Time Spiral era, used fast mana including Seething Song to cast a big Dragonstorm.

No Ninth Edition card was banned in Standard, and the set rotated when Tenth Edition arrived in July 2007.

Ninth matters to modern players because it's one of the oldest sets in Modern, which starts at Eighth Edition. That made several of its reprints Modern-legal: the Urza's lands (the core of Modern Tron), Blood Moon (a long-time weapon against greedy mana bases), and the painlands. Two Ninth Edition cards are banned in Modern: Seething Song since January 2013, and Summer Bloom since January 2016, when it was part of the Amulet combo deck. Our [Modern format guide](/blog/mtg-modern-format-guide) covers the format's ban list in context.

In Commander, Grave Pact, Phyrexian Arena, Seedborn Muse and Greater Good are all long-time favorites, and in Pauper, Soul Warden is a classic lifegain card. As of October 2026, no Ninth Edition card is banned in Legacy or Commander.

## Cards from Ninth Edition worth owning

These are reprints, so you can usually find them in later printings too, but they're worth having in some version:

- **Blood Moon**: a game-winning enchantment against multicolor mana bases in Modern and Legacy.
- **Urza's Tower, Mine and Power Plant**: the backbone of Modern Tron.
- **Phyrexian Arena**: steady card advantage for black Commander decks.
- **Grave Pact**: a Commander staple for sacrifice decks.
- **Seedborn Muse**: doubles your untaps in multiplayer games.
- **Wrath of God**: the classic four-mana board wipe.

## The takeaway

Ninth Edition is less a set to study than a snapshot of what "basic Magic" meant in 2005: painlands, classic creatures, a few new Equipment, and the first white-border goodbye. Its biggest influence today is through Modern, where its reprints made the Urza's lands and Blood Moon legal. If you want to play with its cards, a Modern Tron list is the most direct route.
