Antiquities is the second expansion ever printed for Magic: The Gathering, released in March 1994. Every card in it is an artifact, mentions artifacts, or produces colorless mana for them, and its flavor tells the backstory of the Brothers' War between Urza and Mishra on Dominaria. It's only 85 cards, but it gave the game Mishra's Workshop, Strip Mine, Mishra's Factory, Ashnod's Altar and the three Urza's lands that later became "Tron."

## The setting and flavor

Antiquities has no plot the way modern sets do. There was no novel at release, and the story lives in flavor text and in an article from the era, with Jeff Grubb's novel *The Brothers' War* fleshing it out in 1998. What the set has is one of Magic's foundational backstories.

Long before the game's present day, the Thran, an ancient and technologically advanced civilization, left their machines buried across the continent of Terisiare. Two brothers, Urza and Mishra, are apprentices of the archaeologist Tocasia. In the Caves of Koilos they find a powerstone, and when they disturb it, it splits in two. Urza takes one half, the Mightstone, and Mishra takes the other, the Weakstone. Each brother believes the other has the better half, and that rivalry grows into a war.

The Brothers' War consumes Terisiare for decades. Urza and his apprentice Tawnos build war machines and defenses; Mishra, aided by the artificer Ashnod and, behind the scenes, by agents of Phyrexia, builds his own engines of destruction. Both sides strip the land to fuel their factories, and the war devastates the forest of Argoth, home of the nature goddess Titania. The card names are a who's who of the conflict: Tawnos's Coffin, Ashnod's Altar, Mishra's Factory, Urza's Avenger, Argothian Pixies and Titania's Song.

The war ends with the Sylex Blast, when Urza uses an ancient artifact, the Golgothian Sylex, to destroy Mishra's forces at Argoth. The blast wipes out most of the continent and plunges Dominaria into a long Ice Age, which is the setting for the Ice Age set. Urza survives as a planeswalker, and his long quest against Phyrexia drives the Weatherlight Saga years later. Wizards revisited this era directly in 2022 with [The Brothers' War](/blog/brothers-war-mtg-set-guide) set.

## Mechanics

Antiquities predates keyword mechanics as we know them. Its identity is a theme rather than a rule.

### Artifacts everywhere

Most cards are colorless artifacts or artifact creatures, from tiny ones like Ornithopter (a 0/2 flier for `{0}`) to the huge Colossus of Sardia. The colored cards either support artifacts, like Sage of Lat-Nam (sacrifice an artifact to draw a card) and Priest of Yawgmoth (sacrifice an artifact for black mana equal to its mana value), or fight them. Green got artifact hate like Crumble and Gaea's Avenger, which grows with every artifact your opponents control, and white got Circle of Protection: Artifacts.

### Sacrifice engines

Many artifacts rewarded sacrificing other permanents. Ashnod's Altar turns a creature into two colorless mana, and Atog gets +2/+2 for each artifact you sacrifice to it. These cards were the seeds of combo strategies that still exist.

### Urza's lands

Urza's Tower, Urza's Mine and Urza's Power Plant each tap for one colorless mana alone, but with all three on the battlefield the Tower taps for three and the others for two each. They were designed to power big artifacts, and decades later they gave their name to Tron decks. Our [guide to reading a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) explains how land subtypes like "Urza's Tower" work.

### Product quirks

Antiquities boosters had only eight cards: six commons and two uncommons. The packs weren't fully random, so completing a set took more than one box. Mishra's Factory, Strip Mine and the Urza's lands each have four different artworks, the first time a nonbasic card had multiple art versions. Bronze Tablet is an ante card, and like every ante card it's banned in all sanctioned formats.

## Limited

Antiquities was never really drafted. In 1994, booster drafting as we know it wasn't a standard way to play, and eight-card packs built around artifacts didn't add up to a coherent Limited format anyway. People bought Antiquities packs to add artifacts to their Constructed decks, so there are no archetypes to describe honestly. If you open a pack at a retro event today, it's for the nostalgia, not the gameplay.

## The meta: how it played in Constructed

When Antiquities came out, there was only one Constructed format, the ancestor of today's Vintage. Its artifacts were powerful there immediately. Candelabra of Tawnos was restricted in May 1994 and Mishra's Workshop in June 1994, both later unrestricted in October 1997. Strip Mine has been restricted in Vintage since January 1998.

Type II, the forerunner of Standard, started in early 1995 with Revised and the most recent expansions, so Antiquities as a set was never part of it. Some of its cards still reached Type II through reprints. Strip Mine was reprinted in Fourth Edition and was restricted in Type II in October 1996, and Mishra's Factory and Triskelion were also in Fourth Edition. When Extended began in 1997, its banned list included Antiquities cards that hadn't been reprinted in a later set.

Today the set's power lives in eternal formats:

- **Vintage** has a whole archetype named "Shops" or Workshop decks, which use Mishra's Workshop to cast expensive artifacts and lock pieces on turn one.
- **Legacy** has banned Mishra's Workshop since the format was created in 2004, banned Strip Mine as well, and added Candelabra of Tawnos to its ban list in June 2026.
- **Modern** doesn't include Antiquities, but Ornithopter, Mishra's Factory, Hurkyl's Recall and the Urza's lands are Modern-legal through later reprints, and Modern Tron is named after the Urza's lands.
- **Pauper** banned Atog in January 2022, while Ornithopter and the Urza's lands are Pauper-legal.
- **Commander** players love Ashnod's Altar as a sacrifice and combo engine, and Strip Mine, Mishra's Workshop and Transmute Artifact all show up in artifact decks.

Our [Vintage format guide](/blog/mtg-vintage-format-guide) explains how restricted cards work in practice.

### The Reserved List

Antiquities has 18 cards on the Reserved List, Wizards' promise not to reprint certain early cards. They include Mishra's Workshop, Candelabra of Tawnos, Power Artifact, Transmute Artifact, Gate to Phyrexia, Su-Chi, Tawnos's Coffin and Argivian Archaeologist. Strip Mine, Mishra's Factory, Ashnod's Altar and the Urza's lands are not on the list, which is why they've been reprinted many times.

## Cards from Antiquities worth owning

- **Mishra's Workshop**: the defining land of Vintage artifact decks.
- **Strip Mine**: the most efficient land destruction ever printed, legal in Commander.
- **Mishra's Factory**: an artifact creature land for colorless and artifact decks.
- **The Urza's lands**: the original Tron.
- **Ashnod's Altar**: a Commander combo staple.
- **Ornithopter**: a free artifact creature for artifact synergies.

## The takeaway

Antiquities is tiny and old, but its ideas stuck: the Brothers' War is still central to Dominaria's history, and Workshop, Strip Mine and the Urza's lands still matter in competitive Magic. If you want to play its cards today in a format other than Vintage, Legacy is the most practical choice, using Mishra's Factory and the Urza's lands rather than the banned Workshop.
