In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), a planeswalker is a permanent that acts like a powerful ally on your side of the battlefield. It enters with a number of loyalty counters, and once per turn, on your turn, you can use one of its loyalty abilities, which add or remove loyalty counters as their cost. Opponents can attack it with creatures or hit it with damage, and each point of damage removes a loyalty counter. When it has no loyalty left, it goes to the graveyard.

This guide explains each of those rules in plain language, walks through an example turn by turn, and covers how to attack and defend planeswalkers. If you're still learning how to read card layouts, [how to read a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) is a good companion.

## What a planeswalker is

Planeswalker is a card type, like creature or artifact. Normally you cast a planeswalker the same way you cast a creature: during your main phase, when the stack is empty, by paying its mana cost. Once it resolves, it sits on the battlefield as a permanent.

A planeswalker card has two features you won't find on other cards:

- **Loyalty**, shown as a number in the bottom-right corner, where a creature would show power and toughness. This is how many loyalty counters it enters with.
- **Loyalty abilities**, listed in the text box. Each one starts with a cost in a loyalty symbol, such as +1, −2 or 0, followed by a colon and the effect.

Planeswalkers aren't creatures. They can't attack or block (unless some effect turns one into a creature), and they aren't affected by "destroy target creature" effects.

## Loyalty counters

When a planeswalker enters the battlefield, it gets loyalty counters equal to its printed loyalty. Its current loyalty is the number of loyalty counters on it right now, and that number goes up and down as the game goes on.

Loyalty works like a life total for that planeswalker:

- Using a plus ability adds counters.
- Using a minus ability removes counters.
- Damage dealt to the planeswalker removes counters.
- When a planeswalker has 0 loyalty, it's put into its owner's graveyard. This happens automatically, as a state-based action, the moment its loyalty reaches 0.

Unlike damage to a creature, lost loyalty doesn't heal at the end of the turn. Once a counter is gone, it's gone until something adds it back.

## Activating loyalty abilities

This is the part players most often get wrong, so here are the exact limits. You can activate a loyalty ability only:

1. **During your own turn.** Never on an opponent's turn.
2. **During one of your main phases.** Not during combat or your end step.
3. **When the stack is empty.** No spells or abilities can be waiting to resolve. This is the same timing as casting a sorcery.
4. **Once per turn for each planeswalker.** If you control two different planeswalkers, you can activate one ability of each, but never two abilities of the same planeswalker in one turn.

A few effects in the game change these limits, and the card will say so if it does. Without one of those, these four rules always apply.

### Paying the cost

The loyalty symbol is the ability's cost. A +1 ability's cost is to put one loyalty counter on the planeswalker. A −2 ability's cost is to remove two. A 0 ability has no loyalty cost.

You can't pay a minus cost you can't afford. A planeswalker with 2 loyalty can't activate a −3 ability. A planeswalker with exactly 3 loyalty can activate a −3 ability; it pays the cost, drops to 0 and goes to the graveyard, but the ability still resolves because it's already on the stack.

### The ability goes on the stack

Like other activated abilities, a loyalty ability uses the stack. You pay the cost right away, which means the counters are added or removed immediately, then the ability waits on the stack where your opponent can respond. If your opponent responds by damaging the planeswalker, it already has the loyalty from a plus ability, or has already lost the loyalty from a minus ability.

### No summoning sickness

Summoning sickness stops creatures from attacking or using abilities with the tap symbol on the turn they arrive. Loyalty abilities don't use the tap symbol, and planeswalkers aren't creatures, so you can activate a loyalty ability the same turn you cast the planeswalker. That's a big part of why they're strong: they do something right away.

## A worked example: Garruk Wildspeaker

Garruk Wildspeaker is a classic green planeswalker with a printed loyalty of 3 and three abilities:

- **+1:** Untap two target lands.
- **−1:** Create a 3/3 green Beast creature token.
- **−4:** Creatures you control get +3/+3 and gain trample until end of turn.

Here is how a few turns might go:

1. **Turn four:** you cast Garruk with four lands. It enters with 3 loyalty. You use the +1 to untap two lands, so Garruk goes to 4 loyalty and you have two mana to spend on something else.
2. **Your opponent's turn:** they attack Garruk with a 2/2. You don't block, so Garruk takes 2 damage and drops to 2 loyalty.
3. **Turn five:** you use the −1 to create a 3/3 Beast. Garruk drops to 1. The Beast can block next turn and protect Garruk.
4. **Later:** after several +1 activations, Garruk reaches 4 or more loyalty, and you use the −4 when you have enough creatures to turn the attack into a lethal one.

The rhythm is typical. Plus abilities build loyalty and usually give you a smaller benefit. Minus abilities cost loyalty for a bigger effect. The biggest minus ability, often called the **ultimate**, usually needs several turns of plus activations to reach.

Some ultimates give you an **emblem**: a permanent effect that lives in the command zone for the rest of the game. Emblems can't be destroyed or removed, so once you get one, its effect stays even if the planeswalker is later destroyed.

## How to attack a planeswalker

When you attack, you choose what each attacking creature is attacking: the opponent or a planeswalker that opponent controls. You can split your creatures, sending some at the player and some at a planeswalker.

Your opponent then declares blockers as normal. Blockers can block any attacking creature, regardless of whether it's attacking the player or a planeswalker. Any attacking creature that isn't blocked deals its combat damage to whatever it was attacking. If it was attacking a planeswalker, that damage removes that many loyalty counters.

Creatures with abilities like trample work against planeswalkers too. A trampling creature that's blocked while attacking a planeswalker can assign excess damage to that planeswalker.

If you want a full walkthrough of combat steps, see [how to play Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering).

### Damaging a planeswalker without attacking

Spells and abilities that deal damage to "any target" can target a planeswalker. Lightning Bolt, for example, deals 3 damage to any target, so it can remove 3 loyalty from a planeswalker. Spells that only say "target player" or "target creature" can't target a planeswalker.

Some removal also targets planeswalkers directly, such as cards that destroy or exile "target permanent" or "target planeswalker."

### Should you attack the player or the planeswalker?

That depends on the planeswalker and the game. Some guidelines:

- **Attack the planeswalker** when it's generating an advantage every turn or getting close to its ultimate. A planeswalker left alone for several turns usually wins the game.
- **Attack the player** when you can win the race. If you can deal lethal damage in two turns, a planeswalker that only makes a 3/3 every turn may not matter.
- **Use burn on a planeswalker** if it would otherwise survive combat or reach its ultimate next turn.

## How to protect your planeswalkers

Planeswalkers are strong, but they're also targets. To keep yours alive:

- **Cast them when your board is stable.** A planeswalker with no creatures to protect it is often easy to attack down.
- **Use the plus ability when it's under pressure.** Higher loyalty means it survives more damage.
- **Use abilities that protect themselves.** Planeswalkers that create blockers or remove creatures (like Garruk's Beast token) keep themselves alive.
- **Block for them.** Your creatures can block attackers going at your planeswalker just as they block attackers going at you.
- **Remove attackers first.** Clearing the board before casting the planeswalker gives it time to build loyalty.

## The legend rule and planeswalkers

Almost every planeswalker is legendary. The legend rule says that if you control two or more legendary permanents with the same name, you choose one to keep and put the others into your graveyard.

The rule only cares about the exact name and only applies to permanents controlled by the same player. So:

- You can't control two copies of Garruk Wildspeaker at once. If you cast a second one, you'll have to choose one to keep.
- You can control two different planeswalkers that share a character, such as two cards named differently that both feature Garruk.
- If you and your opponent each control a Garruk Wildspeaker, nothing happens. The legend rule only checks each player's own permanents.

## Planeswalkers in Commander

In Commander, planeswalkers work the same way. Players can choose which opponent or planeswalker each attacking creature goes after, which adds a lot of options in multiplayer games. Most planeswalkers can't be your commander; only ones whose card text says they can be your commander are allowed. If you're new to the format, start with [what Commander is](/blog/what-is-commander-edh).

## Common mistakes

1. **Activating two abilities of the same planeswalker in one turn.** Only one per planeswalker per turn.
2. **Activating on the opponent's turn.** Loyalty abilities are sorcery-speed.
3. **Forgetting the planeswalker loses loyalty from damage.** Damage doesn't wear off.
4. **Assuming planeswalkers can block.** They can't, unless they're turned into creatures.
5. **Not attacking an opponent's planeswalker.** Letting it reach its ultimate is often game-losing.

## The takeaway

A planeswalker enters with loyalty counters, uses one loyalty ability per turn during your main phase, and loses loyalty when it's damaged. Attack opponents' planeswalkers before they take over, and protect yours with creatures and smart use of plus abilities. Once these rules feel natural, planeswalkers become some of the most fun cards to build around.
