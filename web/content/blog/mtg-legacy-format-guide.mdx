Legacy is a Constructed Magic: The Gathering format where nearly every card from the game's history is legal, minus a ban list. Decks are 60 cards with up to four copies of each card, and the result is one of the most powerful and interactive formats in Magic: cheap spells, efficient answers and games decided by tight decisions. Its main barrier is cost, largely because some of its key cards sit on the Reserved List and are never reprinted.

If you want the quick comparison of every format, start with [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained). This guide goes deeper on Legacy itself: what's in it, how it's managed and why its players are so devoted to it.

## What Legacy is

Legacy is an **eternal** format. Unlike Standard, which rotates old sets out as new ones arrive, Legacy keeps every set it has ever included. New cards join every time a tournament-legal set is released, and nothing leaves except through the ban list.

The format grew out of an older one called Type 1.5 in the mid-2000s. Type 1.5 shared its card pool with Vintage (then called Type 1) but banned the cards Vintage only restricted. Legacy was given its own, separately managed ban list, which let it develop its own identity.

### Deck-building rules

- At least 60 cards in the main deck.
- No more than four copies of any card, except basic lands and cards that say otherwise.
- A sideboard of up to 15 cards for best-of-three matches.
- Cards must be legal in Legacy, which means from a set or product that's legal in the format and not on its banned list.

These are the same construction rules as Modern and Pioneer. The difference is entirely in which cards you may use.

## The card pool

Legacy's card pool is huge. It includes cards from Magic's earliest sets alongside cards printed this year. That depth creates a format where the strongest cards at every mana cost tend to be extremely efficient.

Some long-standing staples show what that means in practice:

- **Brainstorm** and **Ponder**: one-mana blue spells that let you dig through your deck and fix your draws.
- **Force of Will**: a counterspell you can cast without paying its mana cost by exiling a blue card from your hand and paying 1 life. It lets blue decks interact on turn one even while tapped out.
- **Swords to Plowshares**: one white mana to exile a creature.
- **Lightning Bolt**: three damage to any target for one red mana.
- **Wasteland**: a land that can destroy a nonbasic land.
- **Daze**: counters a spell unless its controller pays one more mana, and you can cast it by returning an Island you control to your hand instead of paying its cost.

These examples have been fixtures of the format for many years, but the banned list does change. Always check the official Legacy banned list before you build.

### Why the card pool shapes games

When a one-mana spell can exile your best creature, and a free counterspell can stop your key play, games revolve around mana efficiency. Spells that cost three or more mana need to be very powerful to see play, because cheap answers are everywhere. Many decks run fewer lands than they would in other formats and lean on cheap card selection to hit what they need.

At the same time, the format has plenty of room for fast combo, graveyard strategies and decks that attack the mana. That breadth is why you'll see aggressive tempo decks, slow control decks, combo decks and "prison" decks that try to lock the opponent out with cards like Chalice of the Void or Blood Moon.

## How the ban list works

Every eternal format needs a way to remove cards that make the format unhealthy. Legacy handles this entirely through banning: a banned card can't be in your deck or sideboard at all.

That differs from Vintage, Legacy's closest relative. Vintage uses a **restricted list** for many of its most powerful cards, allowing one copy rather than none. Legacy has no restricted list, so cards that are too strong for four copies are simply banned. This is the main reason Legacy feels lower-powered than Vintage despite their huge overlap.

### Why cards get banned

Cards tend to get banned in Legacy for the same reasons as in other formats:

- **They make games too fast.** A deck that can reliably win before the opponent can interact gets in the way of the format being fun to play.
- **They dominate the format.** If one deck or card shows up in a large share of top decks, variety suffers.
- **They're too efficient for the cost.** Fast mana, free spells and card draw that's far above the curve can warp everything around them.

Because the format is eternal, bans are a big deal. They can make a deck someone spent years assembling unplayable, so Legacy's ban list tends to change cautiously.

Since the list does change over time, this guide doesn't reproduce it. Check the official banned and restricted announcement page from Wizards of the Coast for the current version.

## The Reserved List and its effect on cost

The **Reserved List** is a list of older cards that Wizards of the Coast has committed not to reprint. The policy dates to the game's early years, when collectors worried that reprints of old cards would undermine their value. The cards on it are almost entirely from Magic's first several years.

Several important Legacy cards are on the Reserved List. The most famous examples are the original dual lands, such as Underground Sea and Volcanic Island: lands with two basic land types and no drawback. Many Legacy decks want them, and their supply is fixed.

This creates the format's main problem. Most cards in Magic get cheaper as reprints add copies to the market. Reserved List cards can't be reprinted, so as more people want to play Legacy, demand rises while supply stays the same, and prices tend to go up. That keeps the cost of entry for many Legacy decks high compared with formats like Pioneer or Modern.

A few things soften this:

- **Not every staple is on the Reserved List.** Cards like Force of Will, Brainstorm and Wasteland have been reprinted. Only some key cards are locked.
- **Some decks don't need Reserved List cards.** Mono-colored decks that use basic lands, and decks built around cheaper creatures, can avoid the dual lands entirely.
- **Online play.** The Reserved List policy concerns physical printings, and many Reserved List cards exist on Magic Online, where Legacy is popular.
- **Proxy-friendly local events.** Some stores and groups run casual events that allow stand-in cards. Sanctioned tournaments don't, so always ask the organizer.

If cost is the main obstacle, [Pauper](/blog/what-is-pauper-mtg) is another eternal format with a huge card pool, but restricted to commons, which keeps decks affordable.

## What Legacy games feel like

### Turn one matters

Because so many important cards cost one mana, the first turn of a Legacy game often sets the tone. A blue player might cast Brainstorm. A white player might deploy a creature that taxes the opponent's spells. An aggressive deck might play a one-drop threat and back it up with free counterspells.

### Cheap interaction everywhere

Most decks play a lot of interaction, and much of it costs one or even zero mana. This means games are full of decisions: when to cast a spell into possible counter-magic, when to hold up a response and when to commit. It's a format where reading what your opponent might have matters enormously.

### The mana itself is a target

Cards like Wasteland punish greedy mana bases, and cards like Blood Moon can shut down decks relying on nonbasic lands. Building a mana base in Legacy means balancing access to multiple colors against vulnerability to land destruction.

### Sideboards are essential

With so many different strategies in the format, sideboarding carries a lot of weight. Graveyard hate, cards that slow down combo, and answers to specific permanents can swing post-board games. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers the general approach.

## Why players love Legacy

Despite the cost, Legacy has a devoted following. A few reasons come up again and again:

- **Decks last.** Because the format is eternal and bans tend to be cautious, a deck you build can stay playable for years. Many players have played the same deck for a long time and know it inside out.
- **Skill rewards.** Cheap card selection, free spells and lots of interaction mean small decisions add up. Many experienced players consider it one of the most skill-intensive formats there is.
- **Variety.** Legacy supports a wide range of strategies at once: aggro, tempo, control, combo, prison and graveyard decks.
- **History.** It's one of the few places you can play with cards from Magic's earliest years alongside new ones. For players who love the game's history, that matters.
- **Mastery pays.** Because decks change slowly, the time you spend learning your deck and its matchups keeps paying off.

## Getting started with Legacy

1. **Watch or read about the format first.** Get familiar with its common strategies so you can recognize them across the table.
2. **Pick a deck that fits your budget and play style.** Some strategies need Reserved List lands. Others can use basic lands and cheaper cards.
3. **Try it online or at proxy-friendly events** before making a big investment.
4. **Learn the format's key cards.** Knowing what Force of Will, Daze or Wasteland can do saves you from walking into them.
5. **Plan to play your deck a lot.** Legacy rewards depth with one deck more than breadth across many.

## The takeaway

Legacy is Magic with nearly all of its history available: a 60-card eternal format with a ban list, no restricted list, cheap and powerful interaction, and decks that last for years. The Reserved List keeps some of its key cards scarce and expensive, but online play, cheaper strategies and local proxy events lower the barrier. If you love tight decisions and want a deck you can master over years, Legacy is worth exploring.
