Your first Magic: The Gathering tournament will almost certainly be a small event at a local game store: a handful of rounds, each a best-of-three match against one opponent, with players paired by record as the night goes on. You bring a legal deck (and sometimes a written decklist), play each match to a result, report it, and get paired again. Judges or store staff are there to answer rules questions, and calling one over is normal, not rude.

This guide walks through the whole experience in order: preparing, registering your deck, how rounds and pairings work, how to behave at the table, when to call a judge and how final standings are decided. Details vary by store and by event level, so treat this as the general shape and listen to the organizer's announcements on the day.

## Choosing your first event

Most players start at a casual weekly event at their local store. These run at the most relaxed level of rules enforcement. Mistakes are usually fixed with a quick correction and a reminder rather than a penalty, and the focus is on fun and learning.

Larger events run at stricter levels, where the official tournament rules are applied more formally and penalties are standardized. You don't need to know those documents in detail to play your first event. You should know they exist: Wizards publishes the Magic Tournament Rules, which cover how events run, and separate documents that guide judges at the higher levels.

Two event types are especially friendly to first-timers:

- **Constructed events** where you bring a deck you built at home, in a format like Standard, Pioneer or Modern.
- **Limited events** like a prerelease or a booster draft, where everyone builds a deck on the spot from new packs. Everyone starts on equal footing. The [prerelease guide](/blog/mtg-sealed-prerelease-guide) covers that experience specifically.

## What to bring

- **A legal deck.** For Constructed, that's at least 60 cards in the main deck with no more than four copies of any card except basic lands, plus an optional sideboard of up to 15 cards. Check that every card is legal in the format.
- **Card sleeves.** Use matching, opaque sleeves in good condition. Worn or marked sleeves can make cards identifiable, which is a problem at any event.
- **Something to track life totals.** Pen and paper is the most reliable. Many players use dice for counters and tokens too.
- **Tokens and markers** if your deck creates tokens or uses counters.
- **Whatever the store needs for registration.** Many events require an account with Wizards' event system. Ask the store in advance.

## Decklists

A decklist is a written record of exactly what's in your main deck and sideboard. Casual store events often don't require one. Larger events always do, and some stores collect them even at small events.

The rules are simple:

- **Your deck must match your list exactly**, card for card, at the start of every game.
- **List every card with its quantity and full name.** Write "4 Lightning Bolt", not "4 Bolt".
- **Count carefully.** The most common decklist error is a main deck of 59 or 61 cards, or a sideboard that doesn't add up.
- **Return your sideboard cards** to the sideboard after each match, so your deck matches the list again for the next round.

If you want to understand how lists are laid out and what other players' lists tell you, see [how to read an MTG decklist](/blog/how-to-read-a-decklist).

## How rounds and matches work

### Swiss rounds

Most tournaments use a system called Swiss pairings. In each round you're paired against someone with a similar record. After round one, winners tend to play winners and players who lost play each other. Nobody is knocked out during the Swiss rounds. You keep playing every round unless you choose to drop.

The number of rounds depends on how many players show up. A small store event might have three or four rounds. Larger events run more.

### Matches and games

Each round is a match, usually best-of-three: the first player to win two games wins the match. The [best-of-one vs best-of-three guide](/blog/best-of-one-vs-best-of-three) explains why that structure changes how decks are built.

A match usually goes like this:

1. **Find your table** and sit down with your opponent.
2. **Shuffle your deck,** then present it to your opponent, who may shuffle or cut it. You do the same for them.
3. **Decide who plays first** in game one, usually with a die roll or coin flip.
4. **Draw opening hands and mulligan** if needed.
5. **Play game one.**
6. **Sideboard if you want.** After game one, both players may swap cards between their main deck and sideboard. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers how to plan those swaps.
7. **The loser of the previous game chooses** whether to play first or draw first in the next game.

### Time limits

Rounds have a time limit, commonly around 50 minutes. If time runs out before the match ends, the round goes into extra turns: the current turn finishes and then a small, fixed number of additional turns are played. If nobody has won the match by then, the match can end in a draw. The organizer will announce the exact procedure.

The practical lesson: play at a reasonable pace. You never need to rush a decision that matters, but make routine plays promptly.

### Match points

Each match result is worth points:

| Result | Match points |
| --- | --- |
| Win | 3 |
| Draw | 1 |
| Loss | 0 |

Standings are ordered by match points. After the Swiss rounds, larger events often cut to the top players for single-elimination playoffs. Small store events usually just end after the last Swiss round.

## Sportsmanship at the table

Tournament Magic is competitive, but it's still a game played with people from your local community. Good habits:

- **Communicate clearly.** Announce what you're casting, what it targets, and when you're moving to combat or passing the turn.
- **Track life totals out loud.** Say the new total after damage, and keep a written record. If your numbers and your opponent's don't match, sort it out right away.
- **Keep your play area tidy.** Lands, creatures and other permanents should be easy to see. Keep your graveyard and exile zones clearly separate.
- **Don't touch your opponent's cards** without asking, apart from shuffling their deck when it's presented.
- **Win and lose gracefully.** Shake hands, say "good games" and move on. Long complaints about bad luck make everyone's night worse.
- **Help new players.** If your opponent is also new, being patient costs you nothing.

## Calling a judge

Judges are there to keep games fair and answer rules questions. Calling one is never an accusation. Good players call judges all the time.

To call a judge, raise your hand and say "Judge!" clearly. Stop playing until the judge arrives, and don't move any cards in the meantime.

Call a judge when:

- **You and your opponent disagree** about how a rule or card works.
- **Someone made a mistake** that changed the game state, like drawing an extra card or forgetting a mandatory trigger.
- **You want a rules question answered privately.** You can ask to speak with a judge away from the table if you don't want your opponent to hear the question.
- **Something feels off.** If an opponent's behavior seems wrong, a judge can watch the match.

When the judge arrives, each player explains what happened. Be honest and stick to facts. The judge decides how to fix it, and at stricter events may apply a penalty. At most casual events, the result is usually just a correction and a reminder.

## How tiebreakers work

With Swiss rounds, several players often finish with the same number of match points. Tiebreakers decide who ranks higher. The standard tiebreakers, in order, are:

1. **Opponents' match-win percentage:** how well your opponents did overall. Beating strong opponents counts for more.
2. **Your game-win percentage:** the share of individual games you won across your matches.
3. **Opponents' game-win percentage:** how well your opponents did in individual games.

You don't need to calculate these yourself. The tournament software does it. The point is simply that how your opponents perform after playing you matters, which is why two players with the same record can end up a few places apart.

### Intentional draws and dropping

In the last rounds, two players sometimes agree to an intentional draw, each taking one match point, if that guarantees both a spot in the playoffs. This is allowed, but you can't offer anything in exchange for a result, and you can't decide a match with a coin flip or die roll. If you want to leave the event early, tell the organizer you're dropping so you aren't paired again.

## Getting the most from your first event

- **Play a deck you know.** A familiar deck you've practiced with beats a stronger deck you've never played.
- **Expect to lose some matches.** Almost everyone does at their first event. Pay attention to what beat you.
- **Ask your opponents questions after the match.** Most players are happy to talk about what they'd have done differently.
- **Take notes between rounds.** Write down what decks you faced and what decisions you were unsure about.

## The takeaway

Your first tournament is mostly a structured evening of best-of-three matches. Bring a legal deck in good sleeves, register an accurate list if one is required, play at a reasonable pace, and communicate clearly. When something goes wrong, call a judge without hesitation. Standings come from match points, then tiebreakers, so play every round out. Most of all, go to learn: the players you meet and the matches you lose will teach you more than any single win.
