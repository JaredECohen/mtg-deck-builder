The best way to teach someone Magic: The Gathering is to hand them a simple deck, explain only what they need for the next decision, and let them play real turns as soon as possible. Rules make sense once there's a card in front of the player that uses them. Long explanations before the first game mostly get forgotten.

This guide is for the teacher. If the person you're teaching wants to read ahead, the [beginner's guide to playing Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) covers the rules, and [your first game of Magic](/blog/your-first-game-of-magic) walks through a full sample game turn by turn.

## Pick simple decks

The deck you hand a new player matters more than anything you say. A good teaching deck has:

- **One or two colors.** Fewer colors means fewer mana problems and fewer things to keep track of.
- **Mostly creatures.** Creatures are the easiest card type to understand. You cast them, they attack and block, and the result is visible on the table.
- **Simple keywords.** Flying, trample, vigilance, lifelink and first strike are easy to explain in a sentence. Avoid cards with long paragraphs of text.
- **A handful of instants and sorceries.** A couple of straightforward removal spells and a pump spell or two are enough to introduce non-creature cards.
- **A sensible land count.** For a 60-card teaching deck, about 24 lands keeps both players casting spells, which is more fun than mana trouble.

Build two decks of similar power, and give the new player the more straightforward one. A deck full of big green creatures is easier to pilot than a deck full of tricks. Preconstructed decks sold for beginners are designed with this in mind and are a reasonable shortcut. If you're building your own, keep the cards old and plain rather than flashy and complicated.

Don't use your tournament deck or your favorite combo deck. Losing to something the new player can't understand teaches them nothing, and winning with a deck they could never build themselves doesn't help either.

## Teach in this order

You don't need to explain the whole game before you start. Explain enough to take the first turn, then add rules as they come up.

1. **How you win.** Each player starts at 20 life. Reduce your opponent to 0 and you win. Everything else is a way to make that happen or stop it.
2. **Lands and mana.** You can play one land per turn. Tap a land for mana, and spend mana to cast spells. The symbols in a card's top-right corner show its cost.
3. **Creatures.** The two numbers in the bottom-right corner are power and toughness. Power is the damage it deals, toughness is how much damage it can take.
4. **Attacking.** Tap your creatures to attack. If they aren't blocked, they deal damage to the opponent.
5. **Blocking.** The defending player chooses which of their untapped creatures block. Blocked creatures deal damage to each other instead.
6. **The turn structure.** Untap, draw, main phase, combat, second main phase, end. You'll naturally walk through this every turn, so a short explanation is enough.
7. **Instants and sorceries.** Sorceries are cast on your own turn in a main phase. Instants can be cast at almost any time, including during combat.
8. **Responses and the stack.** Only once the player has seen an instant cast in a real game. Before then it's too abstract.

A useful rule of thumb: introduce a new rule only when a card on the table needs it. When the player draws a creature with flying, explain flying. When you cast an instant in their combat step, explain instants.

### Play the first game with open hands

For the very first game, play with both hands face up. You can point out options ("you could cast this now, or keep the mana for that") and explain why you're making your own choices. Nobody cares who wins this game. It's a guided tour.

From the second game on, play with hidden hands but keep talking through your turns. Saying "I'm attacking with this because you have no flyers" teaches more than any rules summary.

## Let them make the choices

The hardest part of teaching is not playing the other person's turn for them.

When it's their turn, ask questions instead of giving instructions. "What do you want to do?" and "What do you think happens if you attack with that?" get the player thinking about the game. Telling them "play the Forest, cast the Grizzly Bears, attack with the Elf" gets them through the turn but teaches them to wait for directions.

Some guidelines:

- **Offer options, not answers.** "You could block with either creature. One survives, the other trades" is better than "block with the 3/3."
- **Let mistakes happen, then explain.** If they attack into a bigger creature, let it happen, then talk about it afterward. A lesson they experienced sticks better than a warning.
- **Allow take-backs early.** In the first few games, letting a player undo a misclick or a misunderstood rule keeps things friendly. Phase this out as they improve.
- **Don't flood them with tips.** Pick one or two lessons per game. Correcting every small inefficiency is exhausting for a new player.

It also helps to lose sometimes, honestly or not. A new player who wins a close game is far more likely to want a second one.

## Common sticking points

These are the rules new players trip over most often. Expect to explain each one at least twice.

- **Summoning sickness.** A creature can't attack or use abilities with the tap symbol until you've controlled it continuously since the start of your turn. New players almost always try to attack with a creature they just cast. The [summoning sickness guide](/blog/summoning-sickness-explained) covers the details, including haste.
- **Who chooses blocks.** The attacker chooses which creatures attack, but the defender chooses which creatures block and which attacker each blocks.
- **Damage wears off.** Damage that doesn't destroy a creature is removed at the end of the turn. A 2/4 that took 3 damage is back to full health next turn.
- **Tapped creatures can't block.** Attacking with everything leaves you open on the opponent's turn, unless your creatures have vigilance.
- **One land per turn.** Not one land per main phase, and not as many as you like.
- **Colored mana.** A card that costs `{1}{G}` needs at least one green mana, not just two mana of any kind.
- **"Dies" means goes to the graveyard.** Players often confuse dying, being exiled and being returned to hand.
- **Instant timing.** New players often don't realize they can cast an instant during the opponent's turn, especially during combat after blockers are declared.

The [beginner mistakes guide](/blog/mtg-beginner-mistakes) covers the strategic errors new players make once the rules are settled, such as cutting lands or attacking into bad trades.

## How long to teach

Keep the first session short. Two or three games is plenty. Magic has a lot of moving parts, and a new player's attention runs out before yours does. End while they still want to play another game.

Between sessions, let them look through the cards on their own. Reading a card and asking "what does this do?" is a great sign, and answering those questions is often more effective than any structured lesson.

## Next steps for a new player

Once someone understands a basic game, there are several good ways to keep them going.

- **Swap decks.** Let them play the other teaching deck. Seeing the game from the opposite side teaches a lot.
- **Let them build.** Hand them a pile of commons and basic lands and help them build a 60-card deck. Building teaches the mana curve and land counts in a way that reading never does.
- **Try a digital version.** MTG Arena includes a tutorial and enforces the rules automatically, which lets a new player practice without someone checking every step.
- **Go to a prerelease.** Prereleases are friendly sealed events where everyone builds a fresh deck from new cards, which puts experienced players and newcomers on more even footing.
- **Visit a local game store.** Stores host casual events where new players are welcome. The [local game store guide](/blog/how-to-find-a-local-game-store-mtg) covers what to expect.
- **Try Commander when they're ready.** Many groups play Commander. It's a lot to take in at first, so it often works best after a few regular games.

## The takeaway

Teach with simple two-color creature decks, start from the win condition and build up one rule at a time, and play the first game with open hands. Ask questions instead of giving orders, let small mistakes happen, and expect summoning sickness and blocking to come up more than once. Keep the first session short, then give the new player a way to keep playing on their own.
