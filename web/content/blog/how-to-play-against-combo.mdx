To beat a combo deck in Magic: The Gathering (MTG), figure out what combo they're playing as early as possible, then either disrupt the one or two pieces it can't function without or kill them before their combo turn arrives. Interaction against combo is all about timing: the right answer at the wrong moment often does nothing, while a single well-timed removal spell or counterspell can win the game.

If you want the background on how combo decks are built and why they work, read [combo decks in MTG explained](/blog/mtg-combo-decks-explained) first. This guide is about the matchup from the other side of the table.

## Step one: identify the combo

You can't disrupt a plan you don't understand. Against an unknown deck, gather clues from the first few turns:

- **Lands and colors.** The colors narrow the possibilities a lot.
- **Cheap card selection.** A turn-one or turn-two Ponder, Preordain or similar spell suggests a deck that's digging for specific cards.
- **Lack of pressure.** An opponent who isn't attacking or developing a normal board is usually setting something up.
- **Odd cards.** A creature or enchantment that looks weak on its own is often a combo piece. Read it. You're always allowed to ask to read an opponent's card on the battlefield, and you should.

Once you have a guess, ask yourself three questions: what are the pieces, what turn can they realistically win, and which piece is the hardest for them to replace? The answers tell you what to save your interaction for.

If a player demonstrates a loop you don't understand, ask them to walk through it. In tournament play, a judge can help, and the player performing a loop has to show how it works before shortcutting it.

## Disruption timing: hit the window

Most combos have a short window when they're vulnerable. Before the pieces come together, your answer stops them. After, it often doesn't.

Consider the classic Splinter Twin and Deceiver Exarch combo. If you kill Deceiver Exarch in response to Splinter Twin being cast, the enchantment has no legal target and doesn't resolve. But once Splinter Twin is already attached, the opponent can respond to your removal by activating the copy ability, and each copy untaps the original. They make as many hasty creatures as they want before your removal even resolves. The same removal spell is excellent at one moment and useless a moment later.

General principles:

- **Answer the piece before the combo is live.** Kill the creature on your turn or theirs before the other piece arrives, rather than holding removal for "when they try it."
- **Respond to the enabling spell.** When the second piece is a spell that targets the first, removing the target in response is the cleanest answer.
- **Go after the piece they can't replace.** If they play four copies of one half and one copy of the other, the rarer piece is the better target.
- **Don't waste interaction on setup.** Countering a card-selection spell when they have more in hand rarely matters. Save your counter for the spell that actually wins.

### Discard

Discard spells like Thoughtseize and Duress are strong against combo for two reasons. They take a key piece before it can be cast, and they show you the opponent's whole hand. That information tells you exactly what to hold your other answers for. Discard is best early, before the opponent has had time to draw redundant pieces or set up protection.

### Counterspells

Against combo, a counterspell is most valuable when the opponent has to cast the key spell into it. If you're holding Counterspell, think about which spell in their deck you must stop and keep the mana open for it. Be ready for them to bait you with a less important spell first.

Counterspells that stop abilities are useful too. Stifle counters an activated or triggered ability, which can break a combo that relies on a trigger.

## Graveyard hate

Many combo decks use the graveyard: they put a creature there and bring it back, or they recast spells from it. Graveyard hate comes in two styles.

**Permanent hate** stays on the battlefield and stops graveyard strategies continuously. Rest in Peace and Leyline of the Void both exile cards that would go to the graveyard. These shut a graveyard deck down if they land early.

**One-shot hate** exiles cards at a chosen moment. Relic of Progenitus and Tormod's Crypt can exile graveyards at instant speed. The key is timing: if the opponent casts a spell targeting a creature card in their graveyard, exile that graveyard in response, and the spell has nothing to return. Using it too early just gives them time to refill.

Surgical Extraction is a different kind of answer: it exiles a card from a graveyard and also searches out the other copies of that card from the owner's hand, graveyard and library. Against a combo that relies on one card, it can remove that card from the game entirely.

## Rule-setting cards

Some permanents change what a combo deck is allowed to do:

- **Spell limits.** Rule of Law makes each player unable to cast more than one spell each turn, which is crippling for decks that chain many spells.
- **Taxes.** Thalia, Guardian of Thraben makes noncreature spells cost `{1}` more, slowing down decks full of cheap spells while also attacking.
- **Ability shutdown.** Pithing Needle names a card, and activated abilities of sources with that name can't be activated unless they're mana abilities. It stops combo pieces that work through activated abilities.

These cards are strong because the combo deck has to answer them before it can win, which costs time and cards.

## Racing: when your best defense is speed

You don't always need to interact. Many combo decks spend their early turns setting up and have little ability to block or remove creatures. A fast aggressive start can kill them before they're ready.

Racing works best when:

- **Your clock is faster than their combo turn.** If they typically win around turn four or five and you can deal 20 by then, you're the favorite.
- **Pressure forces them to go off early.** A combo player facing lethal next turn has to try to win with whatever they have, often without the protection they wanted. That's when your single piece of interaction matters most.
- **You combine both plans.** The strongest approach is pressure backed by one or two pieces of disruption. The opponent can't wait to build a perfect turn, and when they try to go off early, you have an answer.

What doesn't work is a slow, reactive deck with no clock. Given enough time, a combo deck finds all its pieces and its protection.

## Holding interaction without falling behind

Holding up mana for an answer costs you development. Against combo, it's often worth it, but be deliberate.

- **Know their go turn.** Spend your early turns developing, then start holding interaction as they reach the mana they need to win.
- **Prefer cheap answers.** A one-mana answer lets you develop and protect yourself on the same turn.
- **Watch for protection.** If they cast discard or a counterspell first, they may be clearing the way. That's a sign the combo turn is coming.
- **Don't tap out at the wrong time.** Casting a big sorcery-speed threat on the turn they're likely to win can give them the window they need.

## Sideboarding for combo

Best-of-three is where combo matchups are often won. The [sideboarding guide](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers the mechanics; here are the specifics for combo.

**Bring in:** discard, cheap counterspells, graveyard hate against graveyard combos, rule-setting permanents, and anything that answers their specific key piece.

**Take out:** removal for creatures they don't play, lifegain, and slow cards that don't pressure or disrupt.

**Don't overload on narrow hate.** A sideboard card that only stops one plan can be blanked if the opponent has a backup plan or brings in a different approach for games two and three. Keep your deck functional and add enough hate to matter, not so much that you lose your own game plan.

**Mulligan with the matchup in mind.** After sideboarding, a hand with an early clock and one piece of interaction is often better than a slow hand with lots of removal.

## The takeaway

Identify the combo early, know its pieces and its usual win turn, and save your interaction for the moment it actually stops them. Use discard to see their hand, counter the spell that matters, exile graveyards in response rather than in advance, and put pressure on them so they can't wait for a perfect turn. Combo decks are powerful, but they're fragile when you know exactly where to hit.
