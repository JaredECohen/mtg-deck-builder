In Magic, your life total, the cards in your hand and the mana you can spend each turn are all resources, and good players trade them for each other on purpose. Life isn't a score to protect. It's a budget you can spend to gain cards, mana or time, as long as you don't spend the last point. Seeing every decision as a trade between resources makes many hard choices simpler.

Two of these resources have their own deep dives. [Card advantage](/blog/card-advantage-explained) covers how to count and gain cards, and [tempo](/blog/tempo-explained-mtg) covers time and mana efficiency. This guide is about how the three resources fit together and how to decide which one to spend.

## The three resources

- **Life.** You start with 20 (40 in Commander). It's the only resource that ends the game when it runs out.
- **Cards.** The cards in your hand and on the battlefield are your options. More cards means more ways to respond to what your opponent does.
- **Mana.** The mana you can produce each turn limits how much you can do. Unused mana is wasted, since it doesn't carry over to your next turn.

Time, in the sense of how many turns you have left, sits underneath all three. That's what tempo measures.

None of these is valuable on its own. They're only worth what they help you do. A full grip of cards is useless if you die before casting them, and 20 life is useless if your opponent has a board you can never beat.

## Life is a resource, not a score

The most important idea in this guide: **only your last point of life matters.** Being at 1 life and being at 20 life are equally alive. Going from 20 to 14 often changes nothing about the outcome of a game, while the card or tempo you gained in exchange might decide it.

New players tend to treat every point of damage as a loss. They chump block (block with a creature that will die without killing the attacker) early, use removal on the first small creature they see, and refuse to pay life for anything. That conservatism often loses games, because it spends cards and tempo to protect a resource they didn't need yet.

Plenty of cards make this trade explicit:

- **Shocklands** let you pay 2 life to have the land enter untapped.
- **Fetchlands** cost 1 life to find the right land.
- **Phyrexian Arena** draws you an extra card each turn and costs 1 life each turn.
- **Painlands** deal 1 damage to you when you use them for colored mana.

Each of these turns life into something else: tempo, color consistency or cards. They're good when the life doesn't matter and bad when it does.

## When to spend life freely

Life is cheapest when your opponent can't easily take the rest of it.

- **Against slow decks.** A control deck with few threats can't punish you for being at 12. Take hits, pay life for lands and card draw, and use your cards on things that actually threaten you.
- **When you're the beatdown.** If you're the aggressor, racing matters more than preserving life. Taking a hit to keep your attacker swinging is often correct. The [beatdown guide](/blog/whos-the-beatdown-mtg) explains how to tell which role you're in.
- **Early in the game, when the threat is small.** A 2/2 hitting you a few times is often less dangerous than wasting your premium removal on it, as long as you have a plan for it later.
- **When it buys a big card advantage.** Taking 3 damage to set up a block that kills two creatures is usually a great trade.

## When to protect your life total

Life gets expensive when your opponent has ways to finish you without combat.

- **Against burn and reach.** A deck full of spells like Lightning Bolt can deal the last few points directly. Against those decks, every point counts, and paying life for lands or taking avoidable hits is a real cost. The [guide to playing against aggro](/blog/how-to-play-against-aggro) covers this in detail.
- **When the opponent is ahead on board.** If they have more damage on the battlefield than you, your life is the clock on how long you have to turn things around.
- **When you're the control player.** If your plan is to win the long game, you need the life to get there.

A useful habit: before paying life or taking a hit, ask "how could my opponent deal the rest?" Count their visible damage, then add a reasonable guess for what's in their hand. If the answer is "not soon," spend the life.

## Trading resources

Almost every play in Magic exchanges one resource for another. Thinking about the trade explicitly makes it easier to tell when it's good.

| You spend | You get | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Life | Cards | Phyrexian Arena, or not blocking so you keep your creature |
| Life | Mana or tempo | Paying for a shockland to cast a spell on curve |
| Cards | Life | Chump blocking, or using removal on an attacker |
| Cards | Tempo | Cheap removal on a creature that cost more mana |
| Mana | Cards | Draw spells, or activated abilities that draw |
| Tempo | Cards | Tapping out for a card draw spell instead of a threat |

The point isn't to always win every trade. It's to make the trades that move you toward your plan. An aggro deck happily trades cards for damage, because damage is how it wins. A control deck happily trades life for cards, because cards are how it wins.

### Exchanges should match your role

The same trade can be good for one deck and bad for another. Chump blocking with a creature to save 4 life is usually wrong for an aggressive deck that needed that creature to keep attacking. For a control deck a turn from casting a board wipe, the same chump block might be exactly what keeps it alive long enough to stabilize.

## Managing the cards in your hand

Cards in hand are options. How you spend them matters as much as how many you have.

### Don't empty your hand without a reason

Playing every card as soon as you can is often right for aggro, which wants to deal as much damage as possible before the opponent sets up. For most other decks, holding a card or two back protects you from overcommitting. If your opponent might have a board wipe like Wrath of God, putting your last three creatures onto the battlefield risks losing everything at once. Holding one back means you can rebuild.

### Keep information hidden

A card in your hand is something your opponent has to respect. Holding up mana with a card in hand forces them to play around a trick or a counterspell, whether or not you actually have it. Once your hand is empty, they know you have nothing and can play freely.

### Spend cards on the right targets

Removal and counterspells are limited. Using them on the first thing you see is a common way to run out before the threat that actually beats you arrives. Ask whether a creature will matter in three turns before you spend a card on it.

### Watch your hand size

At the end of your turn, if you have more than seven cards in hand, you discard down to seven. If you're about to discard, it's usually better to spend the extra cards first, even on marginal plays, since a discarded card is a wasted resource.

## Using all your mana

Mana is the resource most easily wasted, because it disappears every turn whether you use it or not. Some practical habits:

- **Plan your turn around your mana.** If you have four mana, a two-drop plus another two-drop often beats a single three-drop, as long as both cards are useful.
- **Hold mana with purpose.** Keeping mana open for an instant is good when you have one, or when representing one changes your opponent's play. Holding mana with nothing to cast is just waste.
- **Have mana sinks.** Cards with activated abilities let you turn spare mana into value in long games.

## The takeaway

Life, cards and mana are all resources, and the right play usually trades one for another. Spend life freely when your opponent can't easily reach your last point, and guard it against burn and decks that are ahead on board. Match your trades to your role: aggro converts cards into damage, control converts life into cards. Keep a card or two back when a board wipe is likely, spend removal on what really matters, and use your mana every turn.
