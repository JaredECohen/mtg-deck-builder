A ramp deck in MTG spends its early turns gaining extra mana, then uses that mana to cast big, game-winning spells turns ahead of schedule. Instead of playing a four-drop on turn four, a ramp deck tries to play a six-drop on turn four. If the payoffs are powerful enough, the opponent can't keep up.

This guide covers the three kinds of ramp, how to choose payoffs, how to balance the two halves of the deck, and the weaknesses you need to plan for. For how ramp fits alongside aggro, control, midrange, combo and tempo, see [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained).

## The ramp plan in one sentence

Use cheap cards to get ahead on mana, then use that mana on threats bigger than anything the opponent can handle at that point in the game.

A ramp deck has two parts that must work together:

1. **The ramp**: cards that give you extra mana, now or permanently.
2. **The payoffs**: expensive cards that are worth ramping into.

Ramp without payoffs is a pile of mana with nothing to spend it on. Payoffs without ramp are a hand full of cards you can't cast until turn seven. The deck only works when both halves show up together.

## The three kinds of ramp

### Mana creatures

Mana creatures, often called **mana dorks**, are creatures that tap for mana. **Llanowar Elves** and **Elvish Mystic** cost one green mana and tap for one green. **Birds of Paradise** taps for one mana of any color, which also helps fix a multicolor deck.

**Strengths:** They're the fastest ramp in the game. A turn-one Llanowar Elves lets you cast a three-mana spell on turn two.

**Weaknesses:** They have summoning sickness, so they can't tap for mana the turn they arrive unless something gives them haste. ([Summoning sickness explained](/blog/summoning-sickness-explained) covers the rule.) They're also creatures, so cheap removal and board wipes kill them. Losing your only mana creature to a one-mana removal spell can leave you a turn behind.

### Mana rocks

Mana rocks are artifacts that tap for mana. **Mind Stone** costs two and taps for one colorless mana, and you can later pay one and sacrifice it to draw a card. **Arcane Signet** costs two and taps for one mana of any color in your commander's color identity, so it's a Commander card. **Sol Ring**, a Commander staple, costs one and taps for two colorless mana.

**Strengths:** Rocks aren't creatures, so they don't have summoning sickness and they survive creature removal and creature board wipes. They work in any color.

**Weaknesses:** Most rocks cost two or more, so they arrive later than mana creatures. They're vulnerable to artifact removal, and many make only colorless mana, which doesn't help you cast colored spells.

### Land ramp

Land ramp puts extra lands onto the battlefield, usually from your library. **Rampant Growth** puts a basic land onto the battlefield tapped for two mana. **Cultivate** and **Kodama's Reach** each find two basic lands: one onto the battlefield tapped, the other into your hand. **Sakura-Tribe Elder** can be sacrificed to fetch a basic land onto the battlefield tapped, often after it has blocked. **Wood Elves** fetches a Forest onto the battlefield (untapped) when it enters. **Explore** lets you play an additional land this turn and draws you a card.

**Strengths:** Lands are the hardest permanents for most decks to remove, so land ramp is sticky. Fetching basic lands of any type also fixes your colors.

**Weaknesses:** Land ramp is usually slower than a turn-one mana creature. Most spells that fetch lands are sorceries that do nothing to the board the turn you cast them.

### Which kind to use

Most ramp decks mix types. Green has the most ramp of every kind, which is why it's the classic ramp color. Non-green decks lean on mana rocks, or on lands that produce extra mana in specific circumstances.

## Payoffs: what you're ramping into

The best payoffs share three traits:

- **They affect the board immediately.** A payoff that only does something next turn gives the opponent a turn to answer it.
- **They're hard to answer cleanly.** A threat that leaves value behind when it dies, or that protects itself, punishes the opponent's removal.
- **They swing the game alone.** You won't cast many payoffs per game, so each one needs to matter.

Long-standing examples:

- **Primeval Titan** is a 6/6 trampler that fetches two lands onto the battlefield whenever it enters or attacks, ramping you further.
- **Wurmcoil Engine** is a 6/6 artifact creature with deathtouch and lifelink that leaves two 3/3 tokens when it dies, so removal doesn't fully answer it.
- **Inferno Titan** deals 3 damage divided as you choose among targets when it enters or attacks, often killing small creatures on the way in.
- **Craterhoof Behemoth** gives all your creatures trample and a big boost for one turn, turning a board of small creatures into a lethal attack.
- **Karn Liberated** is a seven-mana planeswalker that exiles permanents and cards from hand.

Spells with X in their cost are also natural payoffs, because every extra mana you produce makes them bigger.

## Balancing ramp and threats

Getting the ratio right is the hardest part of building ramp. Too much ramp and you draw mana with nothing to cast. Too few payoffs and the ramp is wasted. Too few ramp pieces and your hand is full of expensive cards you can't cast.

### A starting point for 60-card decks

These are rough guidelines, not rules. Adjust them through playtesting:

| Part of the deck | Rough count |
| --- | --- |
| Lands | 22 to 25 |
| Ramp pieces | 8 to 12 |
| Payoffs | 6 to 10 |
| Interaction and early defense | The remaining slots |

Ramp decks often run as many lands as any other deck, or more, because they need to hit land drops and want extra mana late. The ramp pieces count as additional sources of mana, but they're not a replacement for lands.

### Use your curve

Look at your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained). A ramp deck's curve has a peak at one to three mana (the ramp), a gap in the middle, and another cluster at five, six or more (the payoffs). Make sure your ramp actually connects to your payoffs: a deck with one-mana elves and seven-mana threats has a gap the elves alone can't close.

### Don't forget defense

Ramp decks spend their first few turns not affecting the board much. Against an aggressive deck, that can be fatal. Include some early defense: cheap removal, creatures that block well, or ramp that doubles as defense, like Sakura-Tribe Elder blocking before you sacrifice it.

## How to pilot a ramp deck

### Mulligan for ramp and payoffs together

A hand with lands and ramp but no payoff can be fine if you have time to draw one. A hand with payoffs and no ramp is often too slow. A hand with a turn-one or turn-two ramp piece, lands and one payoff is ideal.

### Sequence your ramp carefully

- **Play mana creatures early** so they can tap the next turn.
- **Cast land ramp on turns when you have nothing better to do.** A turn-two Rampant Growth is a fine turn; a turn-five Rampant Growth instead of a threat usually isn't.
- **Count your mana ahead of time.** Know which turn you'll cast your payoff and which ramp you need to get there.

### Pick the right payoff for the moment

If you have more than one payoff in hand, choose the one that matters most right now. Against a wide board, the payoff that clears creatures is best. Against a slow deck, the payoff that's hardest to answer may be better.

### Protect your mana

Against decks with cheap creature removal, consider holding a mana creature until you can use it alongside another play, or diversifying with rocks and land ramp so one removal spell doesn't stall you.

## Common weaknesses

- **Fast aggro.** Ramp decks spend early turns developing mana. A fast aggressive deck can deal lethal damage before the payoffs arrive.
- **Cheap removal on mana creatures.** Losing a turn-one elf to a one-mana removal spell costs a whole turn of development.
- **Counterspells.** A ramp deck invests several turns to cast one big spell. Losing it to a two-mana counterspell is a huge tempo swing.
- **Drawing the wrong half.** Too much ramp with no payoff, or payoffs with no ramp, is the most common way ramp decks lose to themselves.
- **Removal that answers big threats cleanly.** Exile effects in particular deal with payoffs that rely on death triggers.

Plan for these. Include early defense against aggro, run a mix of ramp types, and choose payoffs that are hard to answer with a single card.

## Ramp in Commander

Ramp is even more important in Commander, where games are longer, everyone starts at 40 life and decks tend to lean on expensive, high-impact spells. Mana rocks like Sol Ring and Arcane Signet, and land ramp like Cultivate, are common across many Commander decks. [What is Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) explains how the format works.

## The takeaway

A ramp deck works when its two halves meet: enough ramp to get ahead on mana, and enough payoffs worth casting with it. Mix mana creatures, rocks and land ramp, choose payoffs that affect the board immediately and are hard to answer, and keep some early defense so you live long enough to cast them.
