The Magic: The Gathering (MTG) color pie is the system that decides what each of Magic's five colors can and can't do. White, blue, black, red and green each have their own strengths, weaknesses and personality, and no color can do everything. That's the point: by choosing colors for your deck, you choose which tools you get and which problems you'll struggle to answer.

This guide walks through each color, the ten two-color pairs and their guild names, and how the pie shapes your deckbuilding. If you're brand new, [how to read a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) explains where a card's color and mana cost appear.

## The five colors and the wheel

The five colors are white, blue, black, red and green, almost always listed in that order. Their mana symbols are a sun for white, a water drop for blue, a skull for black, a fireball for red and a tree for green. In text they're abbreviated with single letters: W for white, U for blue, B for black, R for red and G for green. Blue gets U because B was already taken by black.

The colors sit in a circle in that order:

- Each color's two neighbors on the wheel are its **allies**. They share some values and tools.
- The two colors across from it are its **enemies**. Their values clash.

So white's allies are blue and green, and its enemies are black and red. Blue's allies are white and black; its enemies are red and green. The pattern continues around the circle.

Cards that aren't any color, like most artifacts and lands, are **colorless**. Any deck can play them.

## White: order, community and protection

White is the color of rules, structure and the group over the individual.

**What it does well:**

- **Small, efficient creatures** and lots of them. White is strong at going wide with tokens.
- **Removal that exiles or neutralizes** a threat, like Swords to Plowshares and Oblivion Ring.
- **Board wipes.** Wrath of God, which destroys all creatures, is a classic white card.
- **Life gain and protection** for its own creatures.
- **Taxing and rules effects** that make the opponent's spells or attacks cost more.

**Where it's weak:** White has historically had little raw card draw, so it can run out of gas in long games. Its creatures are often individually small, so bigger creatures can outclass them one-on-one.

## Blue: knowledge, control and trickery

Blue is the color of thought, patience and manipulation.

**What it does well:**

- **Card draw and card selection.** Brainstorm, Ponder and Opt help you see more of your deck.
- **Counterspells.** Counterspell is the namesake of a whole category, and blue owns it.
- **Bounce.** Returning a permanent to its owner's hand, like Unsummon.
- **Flying creatures** and other evasion.
- **Taking control** of opposing permanents, as with Control Magic.

**Where it's weak:** Blue has very few ways to permanently remove a permanent once it has resolved. Its creatures tend to be small for their cost, and it can struggle against fast threats if it doesn't draw its counters.

## Black: ambition, power at any cost

Black is the color of ambition and willingness to pay any price, including your own life.

**What it does well:**

- **Creature removal.** Doom Blade and many other cards kill creatures outright.
- **Card draw that costs life**, like Night's Whisper and Phyrexian Arena.
- **Tutors**, which search your library for a specific card. Demonic Tutor is the classic example.
- **Discard**, like Duress and Thoughtseize, which strips cards from the opponent's hand.
- **Reanimation**, returning creatures from graveyards to the battlefield.
- **Sacrifice effects** that turn your own creatures into resources.

**Where it's weak:** Black has trouble removing artifacts and enchantments. Its powerful effects often cost life or creatures, which can hurt against aggressive decks.

## Red: freedom, emotion and speed

Red is the color of impulse, passion and acting now.

**What it does well:**

- **Direct damage.** Lightning Bolt hits a creature or player, which makes red good both at removing small creatures and at finishing games.
- **Haste and aggression.** Red creatures attack quickly.
- **Artifact destruction**, like Shatter.
- **Temporary effects**, like Act of Treason, which steals a creature for a turn.
- **Short-term card access**, often letting you exile cards from your library and play them briefly.

**Where it's weak:** Red struggles to remove enchantments and large creatures. It tends to run out of steam in long games, and much of its power is spent quickly.

## Green: nature, growth and size

Green is the color of nature, instinct and accepting things as they are.

**What it does well:**

- **Big creatures** for their cost.
- **Ramp**, which means extra mana: mana creatures like Llanowar Elves and land-fetching spells like Rampant Growth.
- **Artifact and enchantment removal**, like Naturalize.
- **Fight effects**, where your creature deals damage to an opposing creature.
- **Creatures with reach**, which can block fliers.

**Where it's weak:** Green has little direct removal outside fighting, few counterspells and limited ways to handle fliers beyond reach. It relies heavily on creatures, which leaves it exposed to board wipes.

## Strengths and weaknesses at a glance

| Color | Strong at | Weak at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White | Wide boards, exile removal, board wipes, life gain | Raw card draw |
| Blue | Card draw, counters, bounce, flying | Permanent removal, big creatures |
| Black | Creature removal, tutors, discard, reanimation | Artifacts and enchantments |
| Red | Direct damage, haste, artifact removal | Enchantments, long games |
| Green | Big creatures, ramp, artifact and enchantment removal | Fliers, direct removal, counters |

## The ten color pairs and their guild names

Two-color pairs are often called by the names of the ten guilds from the Ravnica setting. These names are used across the whole game community, not just in Ravnica sets.

### Allied pairs

| Pair | Guild | Typical identity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White-blue | Azorius | Control, fliers, rules and taxes |
| Blue-black | Dimir | Control, card draw, milling, trickery |
| Black-red | Rakdos | Aggression, sacrifice, chaos |
| Red-green | Gruul | Big, aggressive creatures |
| Green-white | Selesnya | Go-wide tokens, counters, community |

### Enemy pairs

| Pair | Guild | Typical identity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White-black | Orzhov | Life drain, tokens, attrition |
| Blue-red | Izzet | Spells matter, tempo, card draw |
| Black-green | Golgari | Graveyard value, sacrifice |
| Red-white | Boros | Fast aggro, combat |
| Green-blue | Simic | Ramp, +1/+1 counters, card draw |

These identities are tendencies, not rules. Many Izzet decks are about casting lots of instants and sorceries, but an Izzet deck can be anything that uses blue and red cards.

## Three-color names

Three-color combinations have names too, from two other sets:

- **Shards** (from the Alara block) are a color plus its two allies: Bant (green-white-blue), Esper (white-blue-black), Grixis (blue-black-red), Jund (black-red-green) and Naya (red-green-white).
- **Wedges** (from the Tarkir block) are a color plus its two enemies: Abzan (white-black-green), Jeskai (blue-red-white), Sultai (black-green-blue), Mardu (red-white-black) and Temur (green-blue-red).

## Why the color pie matters for deckbuilding

### It shapes what your deck can answer

Every color has gaps. A mono-green deck can struggle to remove a big flying creature. A mono-black deck may have no clean way to handle an enchantment. Adding a second color is often about covering a weakness, like adding white to green for flexible removal, or adding red to blue for burn.

### It shapes your game plan

Colors push you toward certain archetypes. Red and white lean aggressive. Blue lends itself to control and tempo. Green supports ramp and big creatures. Black supports removal-heavy and graveyard plans. [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) covers these plans in more depth.

### It shapes your mana

Each color you add makes your mana less consistent. A two-color deck needs enough lands of each color to cast its spells on time. A three-color deck needs more fixing. That's why many strong decks are one or two colors, and why color choice is a balance between power and consistency.

### It defines Commander decks

In Commander, your commander's **color identity** decides which cards you can play. A Golgari commander lets you play black and green cards, plus colorless ones, and nothing else. Learning the color pie helps you pick a commander whose colors give you the tools you want. See [what is Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) for how the format works.

## Common beginner questions

**Can a card be more than one color?** Yes. Multicolored cards have more than one color in their mana cost, and they usually have a gold frame.

**Are artifacts a color?** Most artifacts are colorless and can go in any deck. Some colored artifacts exist, and their color comes from their mana cost.

**Do lands have a color?** Lands are colorless, even though they produce colored mana.

**Is the color pie strict?** It's a design guideline, and it shifts slowly over time. Occasionally a card does something outside its color's usual role, but the broad strengths and weaknesses above have held for a long time.

## The takeaway

The color pie gives each color clear strengths and clear gaps. Pick colors for what they do, use a second color to cover the first color's weaknesses, and keep your mana in mind as you add more colors.
