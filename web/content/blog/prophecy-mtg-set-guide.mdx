Prophecy is the Magic: The Gathering expansion released in June 2000, the third and final set of the Masques block. It tells the story of the Prophecy War, a Keldon invasion of the continent of Jamuraa on Dominaria, and its signature mechanic is rhystic magic: cheap spells that fizzle if an opponent pays a small tax. Head designer Mark Rosewater has called it the second-worst designed set in Magic's history, yet it also gave the game Rhystic Study, one of Commander's most-played cards.

## The story

Prophecy is set a short time before the Phyrexian invasion of Dominaria that the Invasion block would tell. A faction in Keld, the warlike northern nation, led by **Latulla, Keldon Overseer** and Warlord Druik, convinces the Keldons that the mineral-rich coast of northern Jamuraa was once their ancestral land. Latulla especially wants a magical mineral called "Hero's Blood," which she believes she needs to wake the legendary Witch-Kings of Keld, as an old Keldon prophecy foretells.

The Keldons raid the Jamuraan coast, burn villages and set up a colony. The city-states of the region form the **Kipamu League**, but it is disorganized and loses ground. Latulla is aided by **Greel, Mind Raker**, a mutant whose life-draining powers spread fear. The planeswalker Teferi sends **Barrin**, the master wizard of Tolaria, to organize the defense, and he fights alongside **Mageta the Lion**, the nature mage **Jolrael, Empress of Beasts**, and his wife **Rayne**, an artificer.

The turning point comes in Keld itself. Latulla tries to wake the Witch-Kings, but the Keldon council condemns her, and in ritual combat she is defeated and exiled. Her forces return to Jamuraa without support. In the final battle, Rayne discovers that Greel is spreading a Phyrexian plague; Greel kills her, and Barrin destroys him. Barrin then finds Latulla trapped under her overturned war barge and kills her.

The League wins, but the cost is high. Rayne's death leaves Barrin bitter toward Urza's grand plan against Phyrexia, and Teferi, angry at Urza's indifference to the destruction, decides to protect his homeland on his own terms. Both threads feed straight into the Invasion block. The novel is *Prophecy* by Vance Moore, published in July 2000.

## Mechanics

Prophecy had no new keywords. Its themes, as described on the MTG Wiki, were rhystic magic, land sacrifice, rewards for tapping out, and anti-Rebel cards to punish the Rebel decks from Mercadian Masques.

### Rhystic magic

A rhystic card is cheap for what it does, but its effect is weakened or stopped entirely if a player pays a small amount of mana, usually `{1}` or `{2}`. **Rhystic Study** is a three-mana enchantment that draws you a card whenever an opponent casts a spell unless they pay `{1}`. **Rhystic Lightning** deals 4 damage, or only 2 if the target's controller pays `{2}`. **Rhystic Tutor** finds any card unless any player pays `{2}`.

The catch is that rhystic cards are at their best against an opponent who has tapped out; against someone holding a little mana back, they often do much less. Rosewater's own criticism was that the set was built too heavily for competitive players, and many of its cards ended up narrow. Rhystic Study is the exception, because in multiplayer Commander paying `{1}` on every spell adds up fast.

### Avatars

Each color got a rare Avatar that costs eight mana but costs six less under a condition. **Avatar of Woe** is a 6/5 with fear that taps to destroy a creature, and it costs `{B}{B}` if there are ten or more creature cards in all graveyards. **Avatar of Hope** is a 4/9 flier that can block any number of creatures, cheap if you're at 3 life or less. **Avatar of Might** is an 8/8 trampler, cheap if an opponent controls at least four more creatures than you.

### Legendary Spellshapers

Spellshapers are creatures that discard cards to produce spell effects. Prophecy's five legendary Spellshapers each cost five mana and discard two cards for a big effect. **Mageta the Lion** destroys every other creature, **Latulla, Keldon Overseer** deals X damage to any target, and **Jolrael, Empress of Beasts** turns a player's lands into 3/3 creatures.

### Land sacrifice and tapped-out rewards

As a counterweight to rhystic, many cards reward you for tapping all your lands or for sacrificing them. **Squirrel Wrangler** sacrifices lands to make and pump Squirrels; **Keldon Firebombers** makes everyone sacrifice all but three lands; **Chimeric Idol** taps your lands to become a 3/3. The "Wind" cycle, like **Plague Wind**, costs nine mana for a one-sided board wipe.

## Limited

Prophecy was drafted with Mercadian Masques and Nemesis in the block format of the time, which opened packs in release order, so Prophecy came last. Draft formats around 2000 were less mapped out than today, and there were no two-color archetypes designed into the set the way modern sets are built. The MTG Wiki notes Prophecy even had relatively few playable cards in Limited.

What worked were efficient creatures, evasion and removal. Rhystic cards were risky because opponents in Limited often had spare mana, although **Rhystic Lightning** still traded well. The Spellshapers and Avatars were game-winning rares when they came together. Blue tempo with **Spiketail Hatchling**, **Withdraw** and fliers, and black removal, were reliable plans. ## The meta: how it played in Constructed

Prophecy joined a Standard of Urza's block, Sixth Edition and Masques block in July 2000, and it stayed through Invasion until the November 2001 rotation. The MTG Wiki's Standard timeline lists Replenish, Tinker, Napster, Angry Hermit, Rebels and Fires among the period's decks, and Prophecy was a minor ingredient in most of them. The Wiki sums up its tournament impact as insubstantial, while naming Avatar of Woe, Chimeric Idol, Foil, Mageta the Lion and Spiketail Hatchling as its potent cards. Most of those cards found homes as role-players rather than as the reason a deck existed.

No Prophecy card has ever been banned in Standard, and none is banned or restricted today.

As of October 2026:

- **Commander**: Rhystic Study is on the official Commander Game Changers list, which affects deck brackets (see [Commander brackets explained](/blog/commander-brackets-explained)). Plague Wind, Rhystic Tutor, Jolrael and Avatar of Woe also see play.
- **Legacy and Vintage**: Prophecy cards are fringe here; Avatar of Woe is a possible reanimation target, since a creature that taps to destroy creatures is strong when it arrives early.
- **Pauper**: Rhystic Study and Spiketail Hatchling are Pauper-legal commons.
- **Modern**: Prophecy predates Eighth Edition and isn't Modern-legal; cards like Avatar of Woe are legal there only through later reprints.

For how these formats differ, read [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) or our [Commander basics](/blog/what-is-commander-edh).

## Cards from Prophecy worth owning

- **Rhystic Study**: the card-draw engine almost every blue Commander deck wants.
- **Avatar of Woe**: a cheap, repeatable removal machine in graveyard-heavy games.
- **Plague Wind**: a one-sided wrath for big Commander decks.
- **Rhystic Tutor**: a cheap tutor, if a risky one.
- **Spiketail Hatchling**: a fun Pauper tempo card.
- **Mageta the Lion**: a repeatable board wipe on a creature.

Prophecy is a lesson in design more than a set full of staples: rhystic magic sounded great and played poorly. But one of its commons became a pillar of Commander, so the set still turns up at most tables.
