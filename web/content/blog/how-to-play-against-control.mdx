To beat a control deck, present more must-answer threats than it has answers, commit them one or two at a time so a single board wipe never wrecks you, and cast your important spells when the control player is tapped out or forced to make a bad choice. Control wins by trading one card for one card until its expensive card-advantage spells take over, so your job is to make every trade cost them something and to close the game before their late game arrives.

This guide assumes you know what control is and how it's built. If you want that background first, the [deck archetypes guide](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) covers it. Here we focus on the matchup itself: what to put in your deck, how to sequence your turns, and the habits that separate players who beat control from players who get ground out.

## What the control player is trying to do

Understanding their plan tells you where to push. A typical control deck has four kinds of cards:

- **Counterspells** like Counterspell and Mana Leak, which stop a spell on the stack.
- **Spot removal** that kills or exiles one creature or permanent.
- **Board wipes** (sweepers) like Wrath of God and Supreme Verdict, which destroy every creature at once.
- **Card advantage and finishers**: spells that draw several cards, planeswalkers, and a small number of hard-to-answer win conditions.

The control player wants to reach the late game with more cards in hand than you and a pile of lands on the battlefield. Every time they answer one of your threats with one of their cards, the trade is even. Every time they wipe two or three of your creatures with one card, they gain ground. Once they resolve a big draw spell or a planeswalker that you can't pressure, they are usually winning.

That gives you three goals: never give them a cheap two-for-one, never let them spend their mana efficiently, and keep the clock running so they can't afford to wait.

## Build for threat density

Your deck choices matter before the game starts. Against control, the quality that matters most is how many real threats you have, not how strong each individual threat is.

A control deck might run eight or ten answers that can handle a creature. If your deck has twelve creatures, they can trade with almost all of them and still have cards left over. If your deck has twenty threats, including creatures, planeswalkers, creature lands and recursive cards, they can't answer everything.

Ways to raise threat density:

- **Play threats that aren't creatures.** Planeswalkers dodge creature sweepers entirely. Lands that turn into creatures, like Celestial Colonnade or Mutavault, survive sorcery-speed board wipes because they are only creatures when you activate them.
- **Play threats that leave something behind.** A creature with an enters-the-battlefield ability has already generated value by the time it's countered by removal. A card that makes several bodies forces an answer for each body or a sweeper.
- **Cut cards that do nothing against control.** Removal spells and lifegain are weak when the opponent has only a few creatures. In best-of-three, [sideboarding](/blog/sideboarding-101) is your chance to swap those dead cards for more threats or disruption.
- **Value card advantage of your own.** Cards that draw extra cards or come back from the graveyard keep you from running out of gas in a long game.

## How to play around counterspells

Counterspells only work if the control player has untapped mana. That simple fact drives most of the matchup.

### Make them use their mana first

If the control player passes their turn with three lands untapped, they are almost certainly holding something: a counterspell, an instant-speed removal spell, or an instant-speed draw spell. You don't have to cast your best spell into that open mana. Cast a lesser threat first. If they counter it, your important spell resolves next turn or later in the same turn. If they don't counter it, you have a threat on the battlefield and they still have to deal with it.

### Cast two spells in one turn

Most counterspells stop one spell. If you can cast two relevant spells in the same turn, the control player can only answer one of them with a single counter. This is why cheap threats are valuable against control: on turn five, two three-drops often beat one five-drop.

### Watch for "unless" counters

Mana Leak reads "Counter target spell unless its controller pays `{3}`." Soft counters like this are beaten by leaving extra mana open. If you can wait one turn and cast your threat with three extra mana available, Mana Leak becomes a dead card. Read the counterspells in your opponent's likely list so you know which ones can be paid around.

### Use discard

Hand disruption like Duress or Thoughtseize lets you look at the control player's hand and take the answer that would stop your best threat. It's one of the cleanest tools against counterspells, because it removes the counter before it can ever be cast, and it gives you perfect information about what they're holding.

### Use uncounterable effects

Some cards can't be countered, and some help other spells resolve. Cavern of Souls is the classic example: you name a creature type as it enters, and creature spells of that type you cast using its mana can't be countered. Cards like this are strong against counter-heavy decks and are common sideboard picks.

## How to play around board wipes

Board wipes are control's main way to get ahead on cards. A Wrath of God that kills four of your creatures is a four-for-one, and few decks recover from that.

The rule is simple: **commit only as much to the battlefield as you need to pressure them.** If two creatures put the control player on a fast clock, keep the third and fourth in your hand. If they wipe the board, you rebuild immediately with the cards you held. If they don't, you've still been attacking every turn.

A few refinements:

- **Count their mana.** Most sweepers cost four or more. Before they reach that mana, you can usually be more aggressive. Once they can cast a sweeper, slow down your deployment.
- **Notice when they're waiting.** A control player who takes small amounts of damage for several turns without using removal is often holding a sweeper and waiting for you to overcommit. Don't oblige.
- **Diversify your threat types.** Sweepers that hit creatures don't touch planeswalkers, creature lands, or enchantments that generate value. Spreading your threats across card types means no single answer handles everything.
- **Rebuild with haste.** A creature with haste deals damage the turn it arrives, which partly undoes the tempo a sweeper bought them.

## Play threats at instant speed

Control decks are built to act on your turn. They hold up counterspells and instant-speed answers, then use leftover mana at the end of your turn to draw cards. Flash creatures and other instant-speed threats turn that rhythm against them.

The classic play is to cast a flash creature at the end of the control player's turn, often called "on their end step." That creates a problem for them:

- If they counter it, they've spent mana on their own turn and have less available for your turn. Your main-phase spell is more likely to resolve.
- If they don't counter it, your creature untaps with you and attacks immediately, and it dodged any sorcery-speed sweeper they might have cast that turn.

Cards like Vendilion Clique, Snapcaster Mage and other creatures with flash work well here. Instant-speed effects of other kinds, like activating a creature land at the end of their turn, give the same benefit. The underlying idea is to act when the control player has the fewest options, not when it's convenient for you.

## Win the card advantage war, or end the game before it starts

Every game against control is a race between your clock and their card advantage. There are two ways to come out ahead.

**Pressure early.** If the control player spends their first several turns answering your threats one by one, they never get the spare mana to cast their big draw spells. Each turn they spend answering is a turn they aren't pulling ahead. Aggressive starts are a real strategy against control, as long as you hold back enough to survive a sweeper.

**Out-value them late.** If your deck is slower, plan to win the long game. That means playing cards that generate more than one card's worth of value, keeping your hand stocked, and not throwing threats into obvious counterspells just to use your mana. Planeswalkers and card-draw engines are especially strong because control decks often have fewer clean answers to them.

The worst position is the middle: a deck that doesn't pressure early and runs out of cards late. Know which plan your deck is on before the game begins.

## Patience is a weapon

Players lose to control by getting impatient. They cast a big spell into four open mana because they had nothing else to do, or they dump their hand to "make something happen." Control decks are built to punish exactly that.

Some practical habits:

- **It's fine to pass your turn without casting anything.** If your only play walks into an obvious counterspell, holding it can be correct. The control player then has to decide whether to tap mana on their own turn, which opens a window for you.
- **Make your land drops anyway.** More mana lets you cast two spells in one turn or pay for soft counters later.
- **Count the cards that matter.** Track how many counterspells and sweepers they've used. If they've already cast both copies of a sweeper you've seen, you can commit harder.
- **Don't over-discard to hand size.** Holding threats is fine, but if you're forced to discard at the end of your turn, you held too many. Deploy one more.
- **Respect the finish.** Control finishers often arrive late and close quickly. When they start tapping out for big threats, that's your window to resolve your best spells.

## Mulligans and sideboarding for the matchup

Against control, a hand with lands and several threats is better than a hand with removal and lifegain. Removal spells may have no targets for many turns. When you evaluate an opening hand, count threats first. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) covers the general process.

After game one, look at what you can cut. Spot removal, sweepers of your own, and narrow anti-aggro cards often come out. Additional threats, discard, uncounterable effects, and cards that punish an opponent for holding mana open come in. Avoid going so heavy on narrow anti-control cards that your deck stops working if they bring in a different plan.

## The short version

Bring more threats than they have answers, deploy them in small waves so sweepers stay inefficient, and cast your best spells when the control player is tapped out or has to choose between two threats. Use flash threats on their end step, take their counterspells with discard, and keep the clock running so their card advantage arrives too late. Above all, be patient: control decks want you to make the first mistake.
