In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), instant speed means you can do something any time you have priority: on your turn or your opponent's turn, during combat, or in response to another spell. Instants are cast at instant speed, and so are spells with the flash keyword and most activated abilities. Sorcery speed is the opposite. Sorceries, creatures, artifacts, enchantments and planeswalkers can normally only be cast during your own main phase, when nothing else is waiting to resolve.

That timing difference shapes a huge amount of Magic strategy. This guide covers exactly when you can act at instant speed, how flash works, why experienced players leave mana untapped on their opponent's turn, and which things are locked to sorcery speed.

## Instant speed vs. sorcery speed

Two terms you'll need first:

- **Priority** is the permission to cast a spell or activate an ability. Players get priority repeatedly during each turn, and the game only moves forward when both players pass without doing anything.
- **The stack** is where spells and abilities wait to resolve. When you cast something, it goes on the stack, and players can respond to it before it resolves. The [stack guide](/blog/how-the-stack-works-mtg) explains how responses resolve in order.

With those in mind, here are the two timing rules.

| | Instant speed | Sorcery speed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When | Any time you have priority | Your main phase, with the stack empty |
| Whose turn | Either player's | Only yours |
| In response to a spell | Yes | No |
| Card types that normally use it | Instants, cards with flash | Sorceries, creatures, artifacts, enchantments, planeswalkers, battles |
| Abilities | Most activated abilities | Abilities that say "activate only as a sorcery," loyalty abilities, equip |

Lands are a special case. Playing a land isn't casting a spell, but it follows similar timing: only during your main phase, with the stack empty, and normally only once per turn.

## When you can cast an instant

Because instants can be cast whenever you have priority, you have far more windows than you might expect. Some of the most useful:

- **In response to a spell or ability.** If your opponent casts removal on your creature, you can respond with an instant before the removal resolves. Giant Growth gives a creature +3/+3 until end of turn, which can save it from damage-based removal.
- **During combat.** After blockers are declared, you can cast an instant to change how the fight goes. That's the basis of combat tricks, covered in depth in the [combat tricks guide](/blog/combat-tricks-guide).
- **During your opponent's upkeep or draw step.** Removing a creature before your opponent reaches their main phase can stop them from using it that turn.
- **At the end of your opponent's turn.** This is the most common window of all, covered below.
- **During your own turn,** at any point you have priority, including your main phase.

The [turn structure guide](/blog/mtg-turn-structure-phases) walks through each phase and step where players get priority.

### Instants on your own turn

New players sometimes think instants are only for the opponent's turn. They aren't. Lightning Bolt deals 3 damage to any target, and casting it on your own turn to clear a blocker before combat is perfectly normal. The advantage of an instant is the choice, not a requirement to wait.

That said, the choice is valuable. If a spell could be cast now or later with the same result, waiting usually gives you more information and gives your opponent less.

## Flash: instant speed for other card types

Flash is a keyword that lets you cast a spell any time you could cast an instant. It's most often found on creatures, but any permanent spell can have it.

A creature with flash can be cast on your opponent's turn, in response to their spell, or during combat. That opens up plays a normal creature can't make:

- **Ambush blocks.** Ambush Viper is a 2/1 with flash and deathtouch. Cast it after your opponent declares attackers and it can block and destroy an attacker that thought it was safe.
- **Dodging sorcery-speed removal.** A creature that enters on your opponent's turn can't be hit by their sorcery-speed removal until their next turn.
- **Keeping mana flexible.** If you leave mana open for a counterspell and your opponent doesn't cast anything worth countering, you can spend the mana on a flash creature instead.

Snapcaster Mage is a well-known example. It has flash, and when it enters, it gives an instant or sorcery card in your graveyard flashback until end of turn, letting you cast that card again from the graveyard.

### Flash creatures and summoning sickness

A creature cast at the end of your opponent's turn will be ready to attack on your turn. Summoning sickness only cares whether you've controlled the creature continuously since your most recent turn began, and a creature cast before your turn starts meets that condition. The [summoning sickness guide](/blog/summoning-sickness-explained) covers the rule in full.

## Holding up mana

"Holding up mana" means passing your turn with lands untapped so you can act at instant speed on your opponent's turn. It's one of the clearest signs of an experienced player.

When you pass with two blue mana open, your opponent has to consider that you might have Counterspell, which counters target spell for `{U}{U}`. They might play a weaker spell first to test you, wait a turn, or play around a counter you don't actually have. Either way, your open mana is affecting their decisions without you spending a card.

The cost is that you didn't use that mana on your own turn. If you hold up mana and never use it, you've wasted a turn's worth of development. That's why instant-speed decks like to have several options at the same mana cost. If no counterspell target appears, they can use the mana on a draw spell or a flash creature at the end of the opponent's turn instead.

### Bluffing

Untapped mana is information. Your opponent can see it but not your hand, so holding up mana can be a bluff. A deck that plays a lot of instants gets this benefit for free, because opponents have to respect the possibility even when you have nothing.

## End-of-turn plays

The end step of your opponent's turn is the ideal moment for many instant-speed plays. Your opponent has already used their main phases, so they've committed their mana and their plays for the turn. If you act now:

- **You've seen everything they did** before choosing what to do.
- **You untap right away,** so the mana you spent comes back immediately.
- **They have little mana left to respond** with, since they've usually spent most of it.

Common end-of-turn plays include card draw at instant speed, flash creatures, and removal on a creature that would be dangerous on their next turn. Think Twice, for example, is an instant that draws a card and can be cast again from the graveyard with flashback, and casting it at the end of the opponent's turn uses mana you held up but didn't need.

### A note on "until end of turn"

Effects that last "until end of turn" end during the cleanup step, which comes after the end step. Damage marked on creatures is also removed in cleanup. So if you give a creature +3/+3 during the end step, it still has the bonus for the rest of that end step. Players don't normally receive priority during cleanup, so the end step is the last regular chance to act in a turn.

## What sorcery speed restricts

Some things can only be done at sorcery speed, even if they aren't spells.

- **Sorceries and most permanent spells**, as covered above.
- **Planeswalker loyalty abilities.** You can activate one loyalty ability per planeswalker per turn, only during your main phase with the stack empty.
- **Equip.** Moving an Equipment onto a creature is a sorcery-speed ability.
- **Abilities that say "activate only as a sorcery."** The card tells you.

On the other hand, most activated abilities without that restriction can be activated at instant speed, and mana abilities like tapping a land for mana can be activated whenever you need to pay a cost. Triggered abilities aren't cast at all. They happen automatically when their condition is met.

## Common instant-speed mistakes

1. **Casting instants in your main phase for no reason.** If the result is the same, waiting keeps your options open.
2. **Holding up mana every turn and never using it.** Have a backup plan for the mana if nothing worth answering appears.
3. **Forgetting the beginning of combat.** If you want to tap down or remove a potential attacker, the beginning of combat step is often better than the main phase.
4. **Thinking flash creatures can't attack.** Cast on your opponent's turn, they can attack on yours.
5. **Responding too early.** You can wait until a spell is actually on the stack before deciding whether to answer it.

## The takeaway

Instant speed means acting whenever you have priority, on either turn and in response to anything. Instants and flash cards give you that freedom, sorcery speed limits most other spells to your own main phase, and the best timing is usually as late as you can wait without losing value, often at the end of your opponent's turn.
