Brawl is a commander-style singleton format: you build a deck around one legendary creature or planeswalker, include only one copy of each card other than basic lands, and play only cards that share your commander's colors. It's played mostly on MTG Arena, where it's one of the most popular ways to play outside of ranked Constructed. Brawl takes the core idea of Commander and adapts it for faster, usually one-on-one games with a narrower card pool.

If you already know Commander, you'll recognize most of Brawl immediately. This guide explains how Brawl works, the variants you'll run into, how to build a deck and what makes it different from its bigger sibling.

## What Brawl is

Brawl was introduced by Wizards of the Coast as a lighter cousin of Commander. The basic structure is the same:

- **You choose a commander.** It starts the game in the command zone, a special zone outside the battlefield, and you can cast it from there.
- **Your deck is singleton.** Apart from basic lands, you can run only one copy of any card.
- **Color identity limits your deck.** Every card must fit within your commander's color identity, which counts the colored mana symbols in its cost and rules text.
- **Your commander comes back.** If your commander would go to the graveyard or exile, you can return it to the command zone and cast it again later, paying an extra cost each time.

If any of those terms are new, [what is Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) explains the command zone, color identity and commander tax in detail. Brawl uses the same ideas, so this guide won't repeat them at length.

### Planeswalkers can lead

One of Brawl's signature differences is that a legendary planeswalker can be your commander, not just a legendary creature. In Commander, a planeswalker can only lead the deck if its card specifically says it can be your commander. In Brawl, any legendary planeswalker is eligible. That opens up decks built around a walker's abilities, which play quite differently from creature-led decks.

## Where Brawl is played

Brawl lives mainly on MTG Arena. Arena offers Brawl as a regular queue alongside its other formats, and it's a common way for players to use cards from their collection that don't make it into competitive sixty-card decks.

Brawl was originally designed with paper play in mind too, and you can still play it at a kitchen table with friends using whatever rules your group agrees on. In practice, though, nearly all organized Brawl play happens digitally, and Arena's implementation is what most players mean by "Brawl."

## The Brawl variants

There isn't just one version of Brawl. The variants differ in two main ways: which cards are allowed, and how big the deck is.

### Standard Brawl

The original version of Brawl uses only cards legal in Standard. Because Standard rotates, so does Standard Brawl's card pool, and decks that were legal one year may lose cards the next. Standard Brawl decks are smaller than Commander decks, which makes them more consistent: you see your best cards more often.

If you want to understand how that rotating card pool works, the [Standard format guide](/blog/mtg-standard-format-guide) covers rotation and why it exists.

### Brawl with a wider card pool

Arena also offers a version of Brawl that draws on a much larger pool of cards available on the platform, not just Standard. This version uses a larger deck size closer to Commander's, so it feels more like a traditional singleton commander game. Arena has renamed and reorganized its Brawl queues over the years, so check the format description in the client for the exact card pool and rules of the queue you're joining.

### What stays the same

Across variants, the core rules hold: one commander, singleton, color identity, and the command zone. The differences are mostly about scale. A smaller deck and a narrower pool make games more predictable. A bigger deck and a wider pool make them more varied.

Because exact deck sizes, starting life totals and banned cards can change, this guide describes them in relative terms. The official source for current numbers is the format description in MTG Arena and Wizards' announcements.

## How Brawl differs from Commander

Brawl and Commander share a lot, but the differences change how games feel.

| | Commander | Brawl |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main platform | Paper, at game stores and kitchen tables | MTG Arena |
| Typical game | Multiplayer, often four players | Usually one-on-one |
| Commanders | Legendary creatures, plus cards that say they can be commanders | Legendary creatures and any legendary planeswalker |
| Card pool | Nearly all of Magic's history | Narrower, depending on the variant |
| Starting life | Higher | Lower than Commander |
| Commander damage | A loss condition | Not a loss condition |
| Banned list | Its own list | Its own separate list |

A few of these deserve more explanation.

### One-on-one games

Commander is built around multiplayer politics: deals, threat assessment and shifting alliances. Brawl on Arena is mostly played one-on-one, so those dynamics disappear. Your job is to beat one opponent, which makes Brawl play more like a traditional duel. Cards that are great in multiplayer, like effects that hit "each opponent," lose value because there's only one opponent to hit.

### No commander damage rule

In Commander, a player who takes a certain amount of combat damage from a single commander loses the game. Brawl doesn't use that rule. You still win by reducing your opponent's life to zero, decking them or using an alternate win condition, but a big commander doesn't get a separate shortcut to victory.

### Lower life, faster games

Brawl games start with a lower life total than Commander, and decks are often tuned for one opponent. Combined with a smaller or more focused card pool, games tend to end faster. Aggressive strategies are better in Brawl than in Commander, because they don't have to deal damage to three separate players.

### A different banned list

Brawl maintains its own list of banned cards, separate from Commander's. A card that's fine in Commander may be banned in Brawl, and the reverse can also be true. Always check the current list for the variant you play.

## Building a Brawl deck

The process of building a Brawl deck looks a lot like building a Commander deck, scaled to the format. If you've never built a singleton commander deck, [how to build your first Commander deck](/blog/how-to-build-your-first-commander-deck) walks through the steps, and most of them carry straight over.

### Start with the commander

Your commander defines your colors and usually your plan. Pick one with an ability you want to use every game, because you'll have access to it from the start. A commander that draws cards, makes tokens or generates value each turn tends to be stronger than a big body with no ongoing effect.

Planeswalker commanders are worth considering. Because you can cast them from the command zone repeatedly, a planeswalker that protects itself, for example by making creature tokens, can be very hard to deal with.

### Balance your categories

Like Commander, Brawl rewards a balanced deck. A typical build includes:

- **Lands.** Enough to hit your land drops reliably. Brawl's smaller decks don't change the need for a solid mana base.
- **Ramp and fixing.** Fewer pieces than in Commander, since games are shorter and you're facing one opponent.
- **Interaction.** Removal that handles creatures and planeswalkers, since your opponent's commander will keep coming back.
- **Card advantage.** Ways to draw extra cards so you don't run out of resources.
- **Threats.** Cards that win the game or take over the board.

### Tune for one opponent

Because most Brawl games are one-on-one, prefer cards that are efficient against a single opponent. Cheap removal and creatures that pressure your opponent early are more valuable than slow, expensive effects designed for long multiplayer games. Board wipes are still useful, but you'll need fewer of them.

### Plan for the opposing commander

Every opponent has a commander they can recast. Removal that exiles or otherwise keeps it off the battlefield only works temporarily, because they can return it to the command zone. The real answer is pressure: make them spend mana recasting their commander instead of advancing their game plan, and the tax adds up.

## Who should play Brawl

Brawl is a good fit if:

- **You love Commander but want faster games.** Brawl games usually end much sooner than a four-player Commander game.
- **You play mostly on Arena.** It's the main place to play commander-style Magic digitally with a ready queue.
- **You want to use cards from your collection.** Singleton decks make use of cards that don't fit into four-of Constructed decks.
- **You want to learn commander-style deckbuilding.** The format teaches the same skills on a smaller scale.

If you're curious how Brawl fits alongside every other way to play, [the formats overview](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) compares them side by side.

## The takeaway

Brawl is Commander's quicker, mostly one-on-one cousin: one commander, singleton, color identity, and the command zone, with planeswalkers allowed to lead. The variants change the card pool and deck size, so check the current rules for the queue you play. Build around a commander you want every game, tune for a single opponent, and lean on efficient interaction and pressure.
