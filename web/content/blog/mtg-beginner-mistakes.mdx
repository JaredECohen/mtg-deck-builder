The most common Magic: The Gathering (MTG) beginner mistakes fall into two groups: deckbuilding errors (the wrong number of lands, too many colors, no plan for the mana curve) and play errors (bad attacks, poorly timed spells, and removal spent on the wrong creature). Almost every new player makes most of them, and each one has a simple fix.

Here are the ten that cost new players the most games, why they hurt, and what to do instead. If you are still learning the basic rules, start with our guide on [how to play Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) first.

## 1. Playing Too Few (or Too Many) Lands

New players love spells and resent lands, so they cut lands to fit in more cool cards. The result is games where you sit with a hand full of four-drops and two lands on the battlefield. The opposite error happens too, usually after a few games of being stuck on lands, and leads to drawing land after land with nothing to cast.

**Why it hurts:** You can only play one land per turn, so every missed land drop delays your whole game plan. Flooding out is less dramatic but just as deadly.

**The fix:** Start from proven numbers and adjust from there. Around 24 lands in a 60-card deck, around 17 in a 40-card Limited deck, and roughly 36 to 38 in a 100-card Commander deck (counting cards that make mana). Lower-curve aggressive decks can go a little lower, and decks full of expensive spells a little higher.

## 2. Playing Too Many Colors

Every color has great cards, so it is tempting to play all of them. A five-color pile of your favorite cards looks amazing on paper.

**Why it hurts:** Each color needs its own lands. The more colors you add, the more often you draw a hand with the wrong ones: three Forests and a hand full of blue and red spells.

**The fix:** Build your first decks in one or two colors. Pick a main color for most of your spells and, if you want, a second color for a few key cards. Add a third color only once you understand how dual lands and mana fixing work.

## 3. Ignoring the Mana Curve

A deck where most spells cost five or more does nothing for the first several turns. A deck of all one-drops runs out of gas quickly. Many beginner decks are lopsided toward expensive cards because big creatures are exciting.

**Why it hurts:** The player who uses their mana efficiently every turn usually wins. If you have nothing to cast on turns two and three, you fall behind a deck that does.

**The fix:** Count your spells by mana value and make sure you have plenty of plays in the two- and three-mana range. Our guide to the [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) shows what a healthy spread looks like for different kinds of decks.

## 4. Building a Deck of 60 One-Ofs

When you are new, you may own one copy each of dozens of cards, so your deck becomes a long list of singles: one of this, one of that, every card different.

**Why it hurts:** A deck of one-ofs plays differently every game, and rarely the way you want. If your best card is a single copy, you will see it in only a small fraction of your games. You can't build a reliable strategy on cards you rarely draw.

**The fix:** In 60-card formats, play multiple copies of your most important cards. Four copies of your best cards and two or three of supporting ones is a strong default. (Commander is the exception: it is a singleton format by design, and it makes up for it with lots of cards that do similar jobs.)

## 5. Attacking Into Obvious Blocks

You have a 2/2 and your opponent has an untapped 3/3. You attack anyway, because attacking feels proactive. The 3/3 blocks, and your creature dies for nothing.

**Why it hurts:** Giving away creatures for free is one of the fastest ways to lose. You lose a card, your opponent loses nothing, and their board is now stronger than yours.

**The fix:** Before you attack, look at each potential blocker and ask: "If they block, what happens?" Attack when the block is good for you, when your opponent can't afford to block, or when you have a trick that changes the math. Otherwise, keep your creature home. Remember that attacking creatures tap, so they won't be able to block on your opponent's turn either.

## 6. Casting Everything in Your First Main Phase

Beginners often play their land and cast every spell in hand before combat, then attack. Sometimes that's correct, but often it gives away information and options for nothing.

**Why it hurts:** If you cast a creature before combat that can't attack this turn anyway (because of summoning sickness), you have shown your opponent your play before they decide how to block. And casting a combat trick or pump spell before attackers are declared lets your opponent block around it.

**The fix:** As a default, attack first, then cast creatures and most sorceries in your second main phase. Cast things before combat only when they affect the attack: a creature with haste, a removal spell that clears a blocker, or an effect that boosts your attackers. Hold instant-speed tricks until blockers are declared, when they do the most damage.

## 7. Not Reading Your Opponent's Cards

It is easy to focus on your own plan and treat your opponent's cards as obstacles you'll deal with later. Then their creature with deathtouch kills your best attacker, or a triggered ability you never noticed wins them the game.

**Why it hurts:** You can't make good attacks, blocks, or removal choices without knowing what your opponent's permanents do.

**The fix:** Read every card your opponent plays, the moment they play it. It is always fine to ask to see a card or to ask what it does. Pay special attention to keywords like flying, first strike, and deathtouch, and to any ability that triggers each turn. If you're not sure what a card does, our guide on [how to read a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) explains where everything is on the card.

## 8. Wasting Removal on Small Threats

You draw your only removal spell and immediately use it on the first creature your opponent plays, a modest 2/2. Three turns later they cast a huge flier and you have no answer.

**Why it hurts:** Most decks have only a handful of removal spells, and some threats can only be handled by removal. Using it early on something your creatures could have handled leaves you defenseless against the cards that actually beat you.

**The fix:** Before using removal, ask whether this creature is a real threat or just a speed bump. Save premium removal for creatures that would win the game, that generate value every turn, or that you can't handle any other way. That said, don't hold removal so long that you lose to an early threat. If a creature is dealing you serious damage and nothing else can stop it, it is a fine target.

## 9. Forgetting Summoning Sickness and Triggers

Two related slip-ups. First, planning an attack with a creature you cast this turn, only to remember it has summoning sickness. Second, forgetting your own triggered abilities, such as an "at the beginning of your upkeep" effect, and simply missing the value.

**Why it hurts:** Miscounting your attackers can lead you to make a bad play earlier in the turn. Missed triggers are free value thrown away. In tournament play, your opponent generally isn't required to point out triggers you forget.

**The fix:** Remember that creatures can't attack or use tap abilities until they have been under your control since the start of your turn, unless they have haste. For triggers, slow down at the start of each turn and scan your permanents. Many players physically tap the table or say "upkeep" out loud as a habit. On MTG Arena the game tracks triggers for you, which is a good way to learn which cards have them.

## 10. Tilting and Not Learning From Losses

You lose a close game, get frustrated, and immediately shuffle up while still annoyed. Or you blame the loss on bad luck and move on without thinking about it.

**Why it hurts:** Frustration, often called "tilt," makes you play faster and worse. And while luck decides some games, assuming it decided all of them means you never find the mistakes you made.

**The fix:** After every loss, take a minute and ask two questions: "Where did the game turn?" and "Was there a different play I could have made?" Often the answer is a mulligan decision, a bad attack, or a removal spell spent too early. (Our [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) can help with the first one.) If you're tilted, take a short break before the next game. Losing is part of Magic for everyone, and each loss you learn from makes you better.

## The Short Version

Most beginner mistakes come down to two habits: building decks that can't reliably cast their spells, and making plays without thinking through what the opponent can do. Fix your land count, stick to one or two colors, build a sensible curve with multiple copies of your best cards, and then slow down during games. Read every card, think before attacking, and save removal for the threats that matter.
