To evaluate an MTG card, compare what it does to what it costs, then ask how much it changes the game in the situations you will actually face. A good card is efficient for its mana, either gains you cards or saves you time, affects the board, and stays useful in many situations. In Limited, where you build from a pool you draft or open, this skill decides most of your wins before the first game starts.

This guide covers the core principles of card evaluation, the classic BREAD pick heuristic for draft, how to judge creatures and removal, why some cards are worse than they look, and how Constructed evaluation differs.

## The five things every card is judged on

Every card can be looked at through the same five questions. No card scores well on all of them, but strong cards score well on several.

### Mana efficiency

How much does the card do for its cost? The baseline in Limited is roughly a two-mana 2/2 like Grizzly Bears or a four-mana 3/3 like Hill Giant. Cards that give you more than that for the same mana are efficient; cards that give you less need a good reason to make your deck.

Mana efficiency isn't just about creatures. Lightning Bolt deals 3 damage for one mana, which is extremely efficient. A spell that deals 3 damage for four mana does the same job but leaves you much less mana for the rest of your turn.

### Card advantage

Card advantage means ending up with more useful cards than your opponent. Some cards generate it directly: Divination draws two cards. Others do it through the board: Mulldrifter is a flying creature that also draws two cards when it enters, so it replaces itself and leaves a body behind.

A removal spell that kills one creature is a one-for-one trade, which is fine. A card that kills two creatures, or a creature that blocks and kills several attackers over a game, is a two-for-one or better. Over a long game, the player with more cards usually wins.

### Tempo

Tempo is about time and mana. A play gains tempo if it costs you less mana than it costs your opponent, or if it lets you develop your board while they fall behind. Bouncing a five-mana creature with a cheap spell gains tempo even though it doesn't trade cards, because your opponent has to spend that turn's mana recasting it.

Tempo matters most for aggressive decks. If you are ahead on the board, cards that keep your opponent off balance can be worth more than cards that draw extra cards.

### Board impact

How much does the card change the battlefield right now? A creature that blocks well, removes a threat, or forces your opponent to react has high board impact. A card that does nothing until a later turn, or only works in a specific situation, has low impact even if its eventual payoff is large.

Limited is mostly a game of creature combat, so board impact is often the deciding factor.

### Flexibility

A flexible card is good in many situations. Removal that hits any creature is more flexible than removal that only hits fliers. A creature with a useful activated ability is more flexible than a vanilla creature of the same size. Prodigal Sorcerer, which taps to deal 1 damage to any target, is a classic Limited all-star largely because it is useful in nearly every game state.

Flexible cards are rarely dead in hand, which is a big deal when you can't sideboard much and don't know what you will face.

## BREAD: a classic Limited pick heuristic

BREAD is one of the oldest teaching tools for draft. It gives a rough order for which kind of card to take when you're unsure. The common version goes:

| Letter | Stands for | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B | Bombs | Cards that win the game on their own if unanswered |
| R | Removal | Cards that kill or neutralize opposing creatures |
| E | Evasion | Creatures that are hard to block, like fliers |
| A | Aggressive creatures (Aggro) | Efficient creatures that pressure your opponent |
| D | Duds | Everything else: filler you take only when nothing above is left |

Different teachers describe the letters slightly differently. Some versions explain the D as "Duds" and others treat it as "the rest of your deck," but the idea is the same.

### How to use BREAD today

BREAD is a good starting point, especially for your first few drafts. It reminds you that a game-winning rare is worth more than a filler creature, and that cheap removal is almost always a strong pick.

Experienced Limited players treat it as a guideline rather than a rule. A few common refinements:

- **Not all removal beats all creatures.** An expensive, conditional removal spell can be worse than an excellent two-drop, especially in a fast format.
- **Your deck matters more as the draft goes on.** Early picks are about raw power. Later, a card in your colors that fills a gap in your curve can beat a stronger card you won't play.
- **Every format is different.** Some sets reward aggressive decks and some reward slow, grindy ones. Pay attention to which commons are performing as you play the set.

## Judging creatures

Creatures are the backbone of most Limited decks, so evaluating them well pays off quickly.

### Stats vs mana value

Start by comparing a creature's power and toughness to its mana value. A creature that is bigger than the baseline for its cost is efficient. A creature that is smaller needs an ability to make up for it.

Look at how stats match the format, too. A 2/1 trades with a 1/1 or dies to anything with 2 power. A 2/3 blocks well against those same creatures. A big creature like Shivan Dragon, a 5/5 flier that can pump its power with red mana, dominates boards in a way that a pile of small creatures can't.

### Keywords

Keywords can add a lot of value:

- **Flying** is the most valuable common keyword in Limited. Air Elemental, a 4/4 flier for five mana, has long been a strong pick because few creatures can block it.
- **Deathtouch** lets a small creature trade with anything, which makes it a great blocker.
- **First strike** and **double strike** win combat against creatures of similar size.
- **Vigilance** lets a creature attack without leaving you open.
- **Lifelink** and **trample** are useful but usually less impactful than evasion or deathtouch.

### Abilities that generate value

Creatures that draw a card when they enter, deal damage, or create extra bodies are often better than their raw stats suggest. They provide card advantage or board impact on top of being a creature. Mulldrifter and Prodigal Sorcerer are old examples of creatures valued far beyond their size.

## Removal tiers

Not all removal is equal. A rough way to rank it:

| Tier | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Premium | Cheap, unconditional, instant speed | Lightning Bolt (for small creatures), Doom Blade |
| Solid | Unconditional but slower or more expensive, or cheap with a small drawback | Pacifism, Murder |
| Conditional | Only hits some creatures or needs setup | Removal that only hits fliers or tapped creatures |
| Situational | Damage-based removal that only kills small creatures, or slow effects | A sorcery that deals 2 damage |

A few notes. Pacifism keeps a creature from attacking or blocking but leaves it on the battlefield, so abilities still work and it can be undone by enchantment removal. Doom Blade can't hit black creatures, which matters if your opponent is playing black. Damage-based removal is great early and worse late, when creatures get bigger.

Even situational removal is often playable in Limited, since you rarely have enough answers. The question is how high to pick it.

## Why combat tricks and narrow cards are worse than they look

New players often overvalue combat tricks like Giant Growth. In the right moment, a trick wins a combat and kills a creature, which feels great. The problem is the moments when it doesn't work:

- **They need a target.** If you have no creatures, the trick does nothing.
- **They can be blown out.** If your opponent removes your creature in response, you lose two cards to their one.
- **They don't affect the board permanently.** After the turn ends, your creature is back to its normal size.

Tricks are best in aggressive decks that are already attacking, and a couple of them can be fine. Taking several over solid creatures is a common reason decks run out of threats.

Narrow cards have the same issue. A card that's great against fliers but does nothing otherwise, or a creature that only works with one specific other card, will often be stuck in your hand. In Limited, you usually can't predict your opponent's deck, so cards that are always useful beat cards that are sometimes excellent.

## Constructed evaluation is different

In Constructed formats, you build from the full card pool and can play up to four copies of a card. That shifts priorities:

- **Raw power and efficiency matter more.** A Limited-playable four-mana 3/3 almost never makes a Constructed deck, because better options exist at every cost.
- **Cheap cards gain value.** Constructed games are faster and more efficient, so one-mana spells like Lightning Bolt or Thoughtseize are measured against what the best decks are doing on turns one and two.
- **Synergy can trump individual power.** A card that's weak alone can be excellent in a deck built around it, like lords in a tribal deck or combo pieces.
- **Narrow cards become sideboard cards.** Since you can sideboard in best-of-three, cards that are excellent against one deck type can still be valuable.

A Limited bomb isn't necessarily good in Constructed, and a Constructed staple can be mediocre in Limited. Always evaluate a card for the format you're playing.

## Common evaluation mistakes

**Overvaluing rarity.** Rares and mythics aren't automatically better than commons. Many are built for Constructed or Commander and do little in Limited.

**Ignoring your curve.** A pile of strong five-drops is weak if you have nothing to play before turn five. Use the [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) to keep your deck balanced.

**Judging cards in a vacuum.** A card's value depends on your deck and the format. A great aggressive creature is worse in a slow, controlling deck.

**Falling for "win more" cards.** Some cards are only good when you're already winning. A card that makes your already-winning board bigger doesn't help when you're behind, which is when you need help most.

**Not updating opinions.** First impressions of a set are often wrong. Adjust when a card keeps surprising you.

## The short version

Evaluate cards by asking what they cost, what they do, and how often they'll be useful. Use BREAD as a starting pick order, value cheap removal and evasive creatures highly, be skeptical of combat tricks and narrow cards, and remember that Constructed rewards efficiency and synergy differently from Limited. Above all, keep learning from your games and update your opinions.
