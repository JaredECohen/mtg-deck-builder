To play Magic: The Gathering, each player brings a deck, starts at 20 life, and takes turns playing lands for mana, casting spells, and attacking with creatures until one player's life total hits zero. Every turn follows the same fixed sequence of steps, and once you know that sequence and a handful of core rules (one land per turn, summoning sickness, how blocking works, and the stack), you can play almost any game of Magic.

## What Magic Is and How You Win

Magic is a strategy card game for two or more players, each with their own deck. Some cards are lands, which produce mana, the resource you spend to play everything else. The rest are spells: creatures, one-shot effects, and lasting upgrades.

You win by making your opponent lose. There are three common ways that happens:

- **Life reaches 0.** Each player starts at 20 life in most formats (Commander uses 40). Creatures attacking, burn spells, and other effects lower it. A player at 0 or less life loses.
- **Drawing from an empty library.** Your deck is called your library. If you are required to draw a card and your library has no cards left, you lose the game. This is often called "decking."
- **Poison counters.** A few cards, such as creatures with the infect or toxic keywords, give players poison counters instead of dealing normal damage. A player with 10 or more poison counters loses.

A few rare cards simply say "you win the game." For a first game, focus on getting your opponent from 20 to 0.

## The Five Colors and Mana

Every spell in Magic belongs to one or more of five colors, and each color has a matching basic land that produces its mana:

| Color | Basic land | What the color tends to do |
|---|---|---|
| White | Plains | Small creatures that work together, life gain, removing many things at once |
| Blue | Island | Drawing cards, countering spells, flying creatures, tricks |
| Black | Swamp | Destroying creatures, bringing things back from the graveyard, paying life for power |
| Red | Mountain | Direct damage, fast aggressive creatures, haste |
| Green | Forest | Big creatures, extra mana, fighting |

To get mana, you **tap** a land: turn it sideways to show it has been used this turn. A Forest taps for one green mana, an Island for one blue mana, and so on. You spend that mana right away to pay for spells. Mana you don't use empties out of your mana pool at the end of each step and phase, so you can't save it for later in the turn.

Most decks use one or two colors, because more colors make it harder to draw the right lands.

## The Card Types

Every card has a type, printed in the middle of the card on the type line. The type tells you when you can play it and what it does once it resolves. (Our guide on [how to read a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) breaks down every part of the card face.)

- **Land.** Not a spell. You play it, it stays on the table, and you tap it for mana. You can play only one land per turn.
- **Creature.** A permanent that can attack and block. Its two numbers in the bottom corner are power (damage it deals) and toughness (damage it can take before dying), written as 2/2 or 4/4.
- **Instant.** A one-shot effect you can cast at almost any time, even during your opponent's turn. After it resolves, it goes to your graveyard. Lightning Bolt is the classic example.
- **Sorcery.** A one-shot effect like an instant, but you can only cast it during your own main phase when nothing else is happening.
- **Artifact.** A permanent object, often colorless. Some are equipment, some make mana, and some are creatures too.
- **Enchantment.** A lasting magical effect. Auras attach to a single permanent; others affect the whole game.
- **Planeswalker.** A powerful ally that enters with loyalty counters. Once per turn, on your turn, you can activate one of its loyalty abilities, which add or remove loyalty. Opponents can attack planeswalkers to reduce their loyalty, and a planeswalker with 0 loyalty is put into the graveyard.
- **Battle.** A newer, less common type. A battle enters with defense counters, and creatures attack it to remove them. Defeating it usually gives you a reward.

Creatures, artifacts, enchantments, planeswalkers, battles, and lands are all **permanents**: they stay on the battlefield after you play them. Instants and sorceries are not permanents. They do their thing and go to the graveyard.

## The Zones of the Game

Cards move between areas called zones. Knowing them makes card text much easier to follow.

- **Library.** Your deck, face down. You draw from the top.
- **Hand.** Cards you have drawn but not played. Opponents know how many you hold, not what they are.
- **Battlefield.** Where permanents live. Both players share it.
- **Graveyard.** Your discard pile, face up. Instants and sorceries go here after resolving, destroyed creatures go here, and discarded cards go here.
- **Exile.** A zone outside the game. Cards that are exiled are usually gone for good, which is why exiling a creature is often stronger than destroying it.
- **The stack.** Where spells and abilities wait to resolve. This is the zone that lets players respond to each other, and it gets its own section below.

Commander adds one more, the **command zone**, where each player's commander starts the game.

## Turn Structure, Step by Step

Every turn has the same phases in the same order. Only the active player (the player whose turn it is) can play lands and cast sorcery-speed spells, but both players can cast instants during most steps.

### Beginning phase

1. **Untap step.** Untap all your tapped permanents. Your lands and creatures are ready again. Nobody can cast spells during this step.
2. **Upkeep step.** Nothing happens by default, but many cards say "at the beginning of your upkeep," and those abilities trigger here. Players can cast instants.
3. **Draw step.** Draw one card from the top of your library.

### First main phase

This is where most of the action happens. You may play one land (if you haven't played one this turn) and cast creatures, sorceries, artifacts, enchantments, planeswalkers, and battles. You can also cast instants here.

### Combat phase

Combat has five steps, covered in detail in the combat section below:

1. **Beginning of combat**
2. **Declare attackers**
3. **Declare blockers**
4. **Combat damage**
5. **End of combat**

You don't have to attack.

### Second main phase

Exactly like the first main phase. If you didn't play a land earlier, you can play it now. Many experienced players hold creatures and sorceries until this phase so they can see how combat went first.

### Ending phase

1. **End step.** Abilities that say "at the beginning of the end step" trigger. Players can still cast instants, which makes this a popular time for an opponent to cast instant-speed card draw or flash creatures.
2. **Cleanup step.** If you have more than seven cards in hand, discard down to seven. Then all damage on creatures is removed, and effects that last "until end of turn" end.

Then the next player's turn begins with their untap step.

## Lands: One Per Turn

The single most important resource rule in Magic: **you may play only one land per turn, and only on your own turn.** You do it during either main phase, when the stack is empty. Playing a land doesn't use the stack, so opponents can't respond to it.

That is why decks run so many lands: a typical 60-card deck plays somewhere around 24 lands, and a 40-card Limited deck around 17. Too few and you get stuck unable to cast your spells. Too many and you run out of things to do. Our guide to the [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) shows how to match your land count to your spells.

## Casting Spells and Paying Costs

A spell's mana cost is printed in its top-right corner. It has two kinds of symbols:

- **Colored symbols** must be paid with mana of that color. A green symbol needs green mana.
- **Generic mana**, shown as a number in a gray circle, can be paid with mana of any color (or colorless mana).

So a cost of "two generic plus one red" can be paid with a Mountain plus any two other lands, but it needs at least that one Mountain (or another source of red).

To cast a spell, you announce it, choose any targets it needs, and pay the cost by tapping lands. The spell goes onto the stack. If nobody responds, it resolves: a permanent enters the battlefield, or an instant or sorcery does what it says and goes to the graveyard.

**Timing matters.** Most spells can only be cast at "sorcery speed": during your own main phase, when the stack is empty. Instants, and cards with the flash keyword, can be cast at "instant speed": any time you have priority, including during your opponent's turn or in the middle of combat.

## Tapping and Summoning Sickness

Tapping means turning a card sideways to show it has been used. Lands tap for mana, and creatures tap when they attack. Some cards have abilities with the tap symbol in their cost, which means you tap the card to use the ability. A tapped creature can't block, and a tapped land can't produce more mana until your next untap step.

**Summoning sickness** is the rule that stops a creature from attacking the turn it arrives. A creature can't attack, and can't use abilities that have the tap symbol in their cost, unless you have controlled it continuously since the start of your most recent turn. In plain terms: a creature you cast this turn has to wait until your next turn to attack.

Summoning sickness doesn't stop a creature from blocking. A creature you cast on your turn can block on your opponent's next turn. The **haste** keyword ignores summoning sickness entirely, which is why red, the fastest color, has so much of it.

## Combat in Detail

Here is what happens in each combat step.

### Declare attackers

The active player chooses which untapped creatures will attack and what each one attacks: the opposing player, or one of that player's planeswalkers or battles. Attacking creatures become tapped. (Creatures with **vigilance** attack without tapping, so they can still block next turn.)

### Declare blockers

The defending player chooses which of their untapped creatures will block, and which attacker each one blocks. The rules here:

- Each blocking creature can block only one attacker.
- Several creatures can team up to block the same attacker.
- You don't have to block at all.
- An attacking creature that isn't blocked deals its damage to whatever it attacked.

### Combat damage

All combat damage is dealt at the same time. Each creature deals damage equal to its power:

- An unblocked attacker deals its damage to the player (or planeswalker or battle) it attacked.
- A blocked attacker deals damage to the creature or creatures blocking it. If more than one creature blocked it, the attacker's controller divides its damage among them.
- A blocking creature deals its damage to the attacker it blocked.

A creature with damage equal to or greater than its toughness is destroyed. If a 3/3 blocks a 2/2, the 2/2 dies and the 3/3 survives; its damage wears off in the cleanup step.

### End of combat

Combat ends. Effects that last "until end of combat" wear off, and you move to the second main phase.

### Common combat keywords

Know these keywords from day one:

- **Flying.** Can only be blocked by creatures with flying or reach. A flier can still block creatures without flying.
- **Reach.** Can block creatures with flying, but doesn't have flying itself.
- **First strike.** Deals its combat damage before creatures without first strike. If a 2/2 first striker blocks a 2/2 without first strike, the first striker kills it before it can strike back. **Double strike** deals damage in both the first-strike step and the regular step.
- **Trample.** If a trampler is blocked, it only has to assign lethal damage to its blockers, and the rest goes to the player it attacked. A 6/6 trampler blocked by a 2/2 can deal 2 to the blocker and 4 to the player.
- **Deathtouch.** Any amount of damage from this creature destroys the creature it hits.
- **Lifelink.** Damage this creature deals also gains you that much life.

## The Stack and Responding at Instant Speed

The stack is the part of Magic that confuses new players most, but the idea is simple: **when someone casts a spell, the other player gets a chance to respond before it happens, and the last thing added is the first thing to resolve.**

A spell or ability doesn't happen immediately. It goes onto the stack, and then each player in turn gets priority: the chance to add something else or pass. When both players pass in a row without adding anything, the top item on the stack resolves. Then players get another chance to respond before the next item resolves.

### A worked example

You attack with a Grizzly Bears, a 2/2 creature. Your opponent blocks with a 3/3. You don't want your Bears to die, so you cast Giant Growth, an instant that gives a creature +3/+3 until end of turn. If it resolves, your Bears become 5/5 and win the fight.

Giant Growth goes on the stack. Your opponent now has priority and responds by casting Lightning Bolt, an instant that deals 3 damage to any target, aimed at your Bears. Lightning Bolt goes on top of Giant Growth.

The stack now holds Lightning Bolt on top and Giant Growth underneath.

You both pass, so the top item resolves first. Lightning Bolt deals 3 damage to your 2/2 Bears, and they die. Then Giant Growth tries to resolve, but its only target is gone, so it does nothing and goes to your graveyard. Your opponent's 3/3 survives untouched.

Reverse the order (Bolt first, Giant Growth in response) and Giant Growth resolves first, so your 5/5 Bears survive the 3 damage.

The lesson: whoever acts last gets their effect first, which is why holding an instant until your opponent commits is so powerful.

### Abilities use the stack too

Activated abilities (anything with a cost and a colon, like "tap: deal 1 damage") and triggered abilities (anything starting with "when," "whenever," or "at") also go on the stack and can be responded to. The main things that don't use the stack are playing a land and mana abilities, such as tapping a land for mana.

## Starting a Game

1. **Each player shuffles their deck** thoroughly. In a casual game you can also cut or shuffle your opponent's deck.
2. **Set life totals** to 20 (40 in Commander). Use a die, a notepad, or a phone app.
3. **Decide who goes first.** Flip a coin or roll a die. In a match of several games, the loser of the previous game usually chooses who starts the next one.
4. **Draw an opening hand of seven cards.**
5. **Decide whether to mulligan.**

### The mulligan

If your opening hand is bad, typically too few lands (zero or one) or too many (six or seven), you can **mulligan**. Magic uses the London mulligan: shuffle your hand back into your library and draw a fresh seven cards. Then put one card from that hand on the bottom of your library for each mulligan you have taken. Mulligan once and you keep six cards; mulligan twice and you keep five. You can keep mulliganing as long as you like, but each time costs you a card.

Players decide in turn order, starting with the player going first. Our [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) covers what makes a hand worth keeping.

### The first turn

In a two-player game, **the player who goes first skips the draw step of their first turn.** Going first is an advantage, because you get to play your lands and creatures before your opponent does, and skipping that one draw balances it slightly.

From there, take turns following the turn structure above until someone wins.

## How to Learn Magic: A Practical Path

Reading the rules gets you started, but playing is how they stick. A path that works for most people:

1. **Start with preconstructed decks.** Starter decks and beginner boxes are ready to play and roughly balanced, so you can learn the rules before learning deckbuilding.
2. **Try MTG Arena.** The free digital version of Magic has a tutorial and enforces the rules for you, a fast way to learn the turn structure and the stack.
3. **Play with someone who knows the game.** A patient friend or a local game store's casual night can answer "can I do this now?" questions on the spot.
4. **Pick a format.** Once the basics are comfortable, choose a format, which decides which cards are legal and how decks are built. Commander is the most popular casual format, while Standard and Pioneer are popular constructed formats. Our [formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) guide compares them, and [what is Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) covers the multiplayer favorite.
5. **Learn from your mistakes.** Nearly every new player makes the same handful of errors. Our list of [common beginner mistakes](/blog/mtg-beginner-mistakes) will save you a few lost games.

When you are ready to build a deck of your own, the [Deck Lab builder](/) can put together a complete, legal list from a short description of what you want to play.

## The Short Version

Start at 20 life. Each turn: untap, upkeep, draw, main phase, combat, second main phase, end. Play one land per turn, wait a turn before new creatures attack, and remember that the last spell cast resolves first. Play a few games with preconstructed decks and everything else in Magic builds on that foundation.
