A mill deck wins by emptying the opponent's library instead of reducing their life total to 0. "Milling" means putting cards from the top of a library into the graveyard. Once a player's library is empty, they lose the next time they would draw a card, so a mill deck only has to remove every card and then wait for a draw.

The word comes from Millstone, an old artifact that milled two cards at a time. Today, milling is both a win condition and a tool other decks use to fill their own graveyard. This guide covers how decking works, the two sides of milling and what makes mill strong or weak. For how mill sits among other strategies, see the [deck archetypes overview](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained).

## How decking works

Running out of cards is called being "decked." The rule is specific, and the details matter.

- **You don't lose when your library is empty.** You lose when you're required to draw a card and can't.
- **The loss happens as a state-based action.** If you attempted to draw from an empty library since the last time the game checked, you lose the next time it checks, which happens almost immediately.
- **Every draw counts.** Your normal draw step is the most common way to get decked, but a card that makes you draw two or three cards does it too. If you have to draw two and have one card left, you lose.

So an opponent with zero cards in their library is still in the game until they have to draw. In most games that's their next draw step, which gives them one more turn of actions with whatever is already in their hand and on the battlefield.

### What milling is

Milling moves cards from the top of a library directly to the graveyard. Modern cards use the keyword "mill," as in "target player mills three cards." Milled cards are not drawn, so milling a player doesn't make them lose on its own. It just shrinks their library until a draw finishes them.

## The two sides of milling

Milling has two very different uses.

### Opponent mill: milling as a win condition

This is the classic mill deck. Your whole plan is to empty the opponent's library. A 60-card deck has 53 cards in the library after the opening seven, minus one draw per turn. That's a lot of cards, so mill decks need either big mill effects or many repeated small ones.

Long-standing mill cards include:

- **Glimpse the Unthinkable:** a two-mana sorcery in blue and black that makes a player mill ten cards.
- **Hedron Crab:** a one-mana creature that makes a player mill three cards whenever you play a land.
- **Traumatize:** a five-mana sorcery that mills half of a player's library, rounded down.
- **Mesmeric Orb:** an artifact that makes each player mill a card whenever a permanent they control becomes untapped. It hits you too, so it pairs best with a deck that wins fast.

There's also the famous combo of **Painter's Servant** and **Grindstone**. Painter's Servant makes every card the same color, and Grindstone keeps repeating its mill as long as the two milled cards share a color. Together they mill an opponent's entire library in one activation.

### Self-mill: milling yourself on purpose

Self-mill puts your own cards into your graveyard to use them from there. It's not a way to win directly but a resource engine for graveyard strategies.

- **Reanimator** mills big creatures and brings them back cheaply. The [Reanimator guide](/blog/reanimator-deck-guide) covers that plan.
- **Flashback and similar mechanics** let you cast cards from the graveyard, so milling them is like drawing them.
- **Dredge** lets you replace a draw by milling cards and returning the dredge card to your hand. Golgari Grave-Troll and Life from the Loam are classic dredge cards.
- **Cards that count your graveyard,** like creatures that grow with each card there, get bigger as you mill.

Self-mill creatures like Stitcher's Supplier and Satyr Wayfinder are common in these decks. They put cards into your graveyard and leave a body behind to block or sacrifice.

The risk of self-mill is decking yourself. It rarely happens with moderate self-mill, but decks that mill large chunks of their own library need to watch the count, especially in long games.

## How a mill deck plays

Most opponent-mill decks are built like control decks. They mill a few cards a turn while using removal, counterspells and blockers to survive. The [control deck guide](/blog/mtg-control-deck-guide) covers that style of play.

A typical game:

1. **Early:** play cheap repeatable mill like Hedron Crab, and use removal to slow the opponent's creatures.
2. **Middle:** keep surviving while repeated mill adds up. Every card you mill is a card they never draw.
3. **Late:** finish with a big mill spell, then hold on for one more turn until they have to draw.

Track their library size as the game goes. Knowing exactly how many cards are left tells you how many more turns you need to survive and when a big spell will finish the job.

### Mill in Commander

In Commander, each player starts with a 99-card library, so milling someone out takes far more cards than in a 60-card format. Mill decks in Commander lean on big effects and repeated triggers, and often need a way to hit several opponents at once. Self-mill is extremely popular there, since graveyard strategies thrive in long games.

## Strengths of mill

- **It ignores life total.** Lifegain decks and big blockers don't stop it. An opponent at 40 life can still lose to an empty library.
- **It sidesteps creature combat.** Mill doesn't need to attack, so a board full of blockers is no protection.
- **It's hard to interact with.** Many decks have no way to put cards back into their library, so once cards are milled, they're gone.
- **Big spells end games suddenly.** A large mill spell can cut a library from small to empty in one turn.

## Weaknesses of mill

Mill also has real problems, and understanding them is the key to playing it well.

### It helps graveyard decks

Milling an opponent who uses the graveyard can be like giving them free cards. A Reanimator player is happy to see a big creature milled. Flashback, delve and dredge cards all get better. Know what the opponent's deck does before you mill them heavily.

### It doesn't affect the board

Milling doesn't stop the opponent's creatures. Against fast aggressive decks, a mill deck can mill plenty of cards and still lose to damage. You need enough defense to survive, which is why mill usually pairs with control. The guide on [how to beat aggro](/blog/how-to-play-against-aggro) covers the defensive tools that matter most here.

### Shuffle effects

Some cards put the graveyard back into the library. Emrakul, the Aeons Torn shuffles its owner's graveyard into their library when it's put into a graveyard from anywhere, so milling it undoes your work. Elixir of Immortality lets a player shuffle their graveyard back into their library, and Gaea's Blessing triggers a shuffle when it's milled.

### Slow and card-hungry

Mill effects that aren't huge take many turns to empty a library, and each mill card you play is one that doesn't block or remove a threat. A mill deck that draws its mill cards without its defense often falls behind.

## The takeaway

Mill decks win by emptying the opponent's library, and a player loses only when they have to draw from an empty library, not the moment it's empty. Opponent mill is a slow, control-style win condition that ignores life totals but doesn't affect the board, while self-mill fuels graveyard strategies. Know which kind you're playing, protect yourself from creature decks, and watch out for opponents who want their cards in the graveyard.
