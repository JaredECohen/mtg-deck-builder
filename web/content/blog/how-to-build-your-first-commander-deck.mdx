To build a Magic: The Gathering Commander deck, start with a legendary creature you're excited to play, let its colors and abilities decide your plan, then fill the 99 other cards with a reliable template: about 36 to 38 lands, around 10 ramp cards, around 10 card draw sources, 8 to 10 targeted removal spells, 2 to 4 board wipes, and the rest spent on synergy and ways to win. After that, play games, note what's not working and swap cards.

This guide walks through each step, with a worked example you can follow along. If you're still learning the format's basic rules (100-card singleton decks, 40 starting life, the command zone), read [what Commander is](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) first.

## Step 1: Pick a commander you actually like

Your **commander** is the legendary creature that starts the game in the command zone and leads your deck. It also defines the deck's **color identity**: every card in your deck must use only the colors that appear in your commander's mana cost and rules text. A mono-red commander means a mono-red deck. A commander with white, blue and black mana symbols opens up all three of those colors.

A few things that matter when choosing:

- **Pick something you find fun.** You'll play this deck many times. A commander whose ability you enjoy using matters more than its raw power.
- **Look at what the ability asks for.** A commander that rewards casting instants wants a deck full of them. One that cares about creature types wants that tribe. The ability is your deck's first clue.
- **Consider how many colors.** One or two colors means a simpler mana base and cheaper lands. Three or more colors gives more card options but needs more mana fixing.

Remember the singleton rule: other than basic lands, each card can appear only once in your deck. That shapes how you build. You'll be relying on several cards that do similar jobs rather than four copies of the best one.

## Step 2: Decide what the deck is trying to do

Before you list any cards, write one sentence describing how the deck wins. "Make a huge army of tokens and win with a big attack." "Stall the game with removal, then win with one huge spell." "Keep recasting my commander and win with commander damage."

That sentence tells you which cards to prioritize and which to cut. Commander games are long and multiplayer, so most plans fall into a few broad shapes:

- **Go wide**: make many creatures or tokens and win through combat or effects that reward a big board.
- **Voltron**: suit up your commander with Equipment and Auras and win through commander damage (21 combat damage from a single commander).
- **Value engine**: generate steady card or mana advantage and win late with overwhelming resources.
- **Combo**: assemble a specific card combination that wins outright.
- **Spellslinger**: cast lots of instants and sorceries and reward each one.

You don't need to choose one perfectly. Most decks blend two. But a clear primary plan keeps the 99 focused.

## Step 3: Use a starting template for card counts

New Commander deck builders most often fail in the same way: too many cool synergy cards, not enough lands, ramp, card draw or answers. A template fixes that before it happens.

| Category | Starting count | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lands | 36-38 | Lets you cast spells every turn |
| Ramp | ~10 | Gets you ahead on mana |
| Card draw | ~10 | Keeps your hand from running dry |
| Targeted removal | 8-10 | Answers one problem permanent |
| Board wipes | 2-4 | Resets the board when you're behind |
| Synergy and win conditions | The rest (~30) | Your deck's actual plan |

This is a flexible starting point, not a rulebook. A deck with a low mana curve and a lot of cheap ramp can trim a land or two. A deck with a commander that costs six or seven mana might want 38 lands plus extra ramp. A deck whose commander draws cards every turn can cut back on other draw. Start here, then adjust based on how games actually go.

Many cards count for two categories. A creature that draws a card when it enters is both card draw and a body. A removal spell that also ramps you is doubly valuable. Count them in whichever slot they fill most reliably.

## Step 4: Fill the template with staples

Some cards appear in a huge number of Commander decks because they're good in almost any deck of the right colors. They're a sensible base for your first build.

**Mana rocks.** Artifacts that produce mana are the most flexible ramp, since every color can play them.

- **Sol Ring** costs one mana and taps for two colorless. It's in a huge share of Commander decks because nothing else ramps so much for so little.
- **Arcane Signet** costs two and taps for one mana of any color in your commander's color identity.
- **Mind Stone** and similar two-mana rocks are cheap, reliable ramp in any deck.

**Lands.** **Command Tower** taps for any color in your commander's color identity, which makes it an easy include for any deck with two or more colors. Beyond that, lands that enter untapped and produce more than one of your colors improve consistency. In a one- or two-color deck, basic lands are perfectly good.

**Ramp by color.** Green has the deepest pool: cards like **Cultivate** and **Rampant Growth** fetch basic lands onto the battlefield, and mana creatures like **Llanowar Elves** accelerate you on turn one. Other colors lean more on artifacts.

**Card draw.** Look for repeatable sources that fit your plan, like an enchantment or creature that draws a card each time something you care about happens. One-shot draw spells are fine, but repeatable engines are what keep you ahead in long multiplayer games.

**Removal.** Prioritize flexible removal that hits more than one card type. **Swords to Plowshares** in white, **Beast Within** in green and **Chaos Warp** in red all answer a wide range of problems. In multiplayer, instant-speed answers are especially useful because you can wait to see which threat actually matters.

**Board wipes.** Cards like **Wrath of God** in white or **Blasphemous Act** in red reset the board. You don't need many, but a deck with none has no way back from falling far behind.

## Step 5: Watch your mana curve

Commander decks can play more expensive cards than 60-card decks because games run longer, but a top-heavy deck still sits there doing nothing for the first few turns. A healthy first build usually has a solid base of two- and three-mana cards, a moderate number of fours and fives, and only a handful of cards at six or more.

The **mana curve** is the number of cards at each mana value. It's worth counting yours before you finalize the list. If you have twenty cards at five or more mana and only a few at two, cut some expensive cards for cheaper ones, or add more ramp. The [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) shows how to count and read a curve step by step.

## Step 6: Make sure you can actually win

It's easy to build a deck that does lots of things and never ends the game. Commander games are multiplayer, so you usually need to defeat three opponents, not one. Check that your deck has several clear ways to close out a game:

- A big combat finish: an effect that pumps your whole team or gives it evasion.
- A damage or life-loss engine that hits every opponent at once.
- A commander that wins through commander damage, with ways to protect it.
- A combo, if you and your group enjoy that style.

Aim for at least three or four distinct cards that can turn a strong position into a win. More is better, since you'll only draw some of them in any game.

## A worked example: Krenko, Mob Boss

Let's apply the steps to **Krenko, Mob Boss**, a mono-red legendary Goblin with a well-known ability: tap it to create a number of 1/1 red Goblin tokens equal to the number of Goblins you control. Since Krenko is a Goblin itself, it always makes at least one, and the number grows quickly once you have a few.

**Commander and identity.** Krenko is mono-red, so every card in the deck must be red or colorless. That makes the mana base simple: mostly Mountains.

**The plan.** "Make an army of Goblins with Krenko, then win with effects that reward a big board." Krenko needs to tap to work, so the deck wants ways to give it haste or protect it from removal.

**Applying the template.**

- **Lands (about 36).** Mostly Mountains, plus a few utility lands. Command Tower works in a mono-color deck but does the same job as a Mountain, so it's optional here.
- **Ramp (about 10).** Red has fewer land-ramp spells than green, so this deck leans on artifacts: Sol Ring, Arcane Signet, Mind Stone and other two-mana rocks.
- **Card draw (about 10).** Red often draws through "impulse draw", cards that exile the top of your library and let you play those cards for a limited time. Effects that draw cards when creatures attack or enter also fit well.
- **Removal (8-10) and wipes (2-4).** Chaos Warp, cheap damage spells, and Blasphemous Act, which is easier to cast when there are many creatures on the battlefield.
- **Synergy and win conditions (about 30).** Other Goblins that make more Goblins or boost the team. Equipment such as **Lightning Greaves** or **Swiftfoot Boots** to give Krenko haste and protection. Payoffs like **Impact Tremors**, which deals damage to each opponent whenever a creature enters under your control, and **Goblin Bombardment**, which turns a pile of tokens into direct damage.

That gives a clear deck: early ramp and Goblins, Krenko on turn four or earlier with haste, a board that grows exponentially, and several cards that convert a huge board into a win against the whole table.

## Step 7: Build on a budget

Commander can be expensive, but it doesn't have to be. Some ways to keep costs down:

- **Start from a preconstructed deck.** Official Commander precons are built to play right out of the box and are an excellent base to upgrade over time.
- **Play fewer colors.** Mono-color and two-color decks need fewer expensive multicolor lands.
- **Use basic lands generously.** They're free, and a mostly basic mana base is fine at most tables.
- **Prioritize function over flash.** A budget removal spell that answers the same threats as a pricey one does the same job.
- **Upgrade slowly.** Replace a few cards at a time based on what actually underperformed.

The [budget decks guide](/blog/budget-mtg-decks-under-50) covers more general money-saving habits.

## Step 8: Playtest and iterate

No first draft is right. Play games, then look at what happened:

- **Did you miss land drops or flood?** Adjust your land and ramp counts.
- **Did you run out of cards?** Add more card draw.
- **Did a single opposing permanent run away with the game?** Add more flexible removal.
- **Were cards stuck in your hand all game?** They were too expensive or didn't fit the plan. Cut them.

Keep a short list of cards that never did anything and cards you wished you'd drawn. After five or ten games, those lists tell you exactly what to swap.

## Step 9: Talk about power level

Commander is a social format, and decks range from casual, theme-focused builds to highly tuned competitive lists. The best games happen when everyone at the table is playing at a similar level. Before a game with new players, describe your deck honestly: how fast it can win, whether it has infinite combos, and how much interaction it plays.

The official Commander brackets give everyone a shared vocabulary for this conversation. The [Commander brackets guide](/blog/commander-brackets-explained) explains how they work and how to place your deck.

## Your first deck, in short

Pick a commander you love, write one sentence about how the deck wins, fill the template (36-38 lands, ~10 ramp, ~10 draw, 8-10 removal, 2-4 wipes, the rest synergy), check the curve, make sure you have several ways to win, then play and adjust.
