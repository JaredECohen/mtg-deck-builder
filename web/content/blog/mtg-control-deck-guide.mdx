In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), a control deck wins by answering everything that matters until the opponent runs out of resources, then finishing with a small number of hard-to-handle threats. You trade cards one for one, look for chances to trade one for two, and keep making land drops so your late game is stronger than theirs. Playing control well is mostly about choosing what to answer, when to spend your mana, and when to stop reacting and start winning.

If you want the short version of where control sits among the other strategies, the [deck archetypes overview](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) covers it. This guide is the deep dive: how a control game actually unfolds and the decisions that separate a good control player from someone who just holds up counterspells.

## The control game plan

Control games tend to run in three stages.

1. **Survive the early turns.** Your opponent plays threats faster than you, so your first job is to stay alive with a healthy life total. Cheap removal and early blockers do this work.
2. **Stabilize.** At some point the board is clear or under control, your opponent's hand is small, and you have more lands and more cards. That moment is called stabilizing, and it's the turning point of the game.
3. **Convert.** Once you're ahead on resources, deploy a threat the opponent can't easily answer and protect it until the game ends.

The mistake most new control players make is treating the whole game as stage one. They keep answering forever and never move to stage three, which gives the opponent extra draws to find the one card that breaks the game open. Control doesn't win by not losing. It wins by building an advantage and then cashing it in.

### Why control works

Control relies on a simple fact: every card in the opponent's deck has to be answered or ignored, and most cards in a focused deck do roughly one thing. If your answers are efficient and you have more of them than they have threats, they eventually run dry. Meanwhile, the [card advantage](/blog/card-advantage-explained) you generate from draw spells and board wipes means you keep drawing gas while they topdeck one card a turn.

## The card types in a control deck

Most control decks are built from the same handful of building blocks. The exact cards change from format to format, but the roles stay stable.

| Role | What it does | Classic examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Counterspells | Stop a spell before it resolves | Counterspell, Absorb |
| Spot removal | Kill one threat that's already on the battlefield | Swords to Plowshares, Path to Exile, Fatal Push |
| Board wipes | Destroy many creatures at once | Wrath of God, Supreme Verdict |
| Card draw and selection | Refill your hand, find the right answer | Opt, Fact or Fiction, Sphinx's Revelation |
| Win conditions | End the game once you've stabilized | Teferi, Hero of Dominaria, Celestial Colonnade |
| Lands | Hit every land drop and cast expensive spells | Usually 25 or more in a 60-card deck |

### Counterspells versus removal

Counterspells and removal both answer threats, but they work differently. A counterspell has to be cast while the threat is on the stack, which means you must leave mana open on the opponent's turn and guess correctly about what they'll cast. Removal can be used after the fact, at a time of your choosing, but it can't stop things like a creature's enters-the-battlefield trigger or a sorcery that already did its damage.

A good control deck runs both. Counterspells handle the cards that removal can't touch, like planeswalkers and big sorceries. Removal handles the threats that slipped through or resolved while you were tapped out.

### Board wipes

A board wipe is the card that most often swings a control game. When the opponent commits three creatures and you destroy all of them with one card, you've traded one card for three. That's the cleanest card advantage in the game. The cost is that board wipes are usually sorceries, so you cast them on your own turn and take a hit first.

### Lands matter more than you think

Control wants to make a land drop every turn for a long time. Missing land drops is how control loses to slower decks, because you can't hold up a counterspell and cast a draw spell in the same turn without enough mana. Control decks typically run more lands than other archetypes, and your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) can afford to go higher because the deck expects long games.

## Choosing win conditions

A control deck usually runs only a few cards that actually win the game. That's on purpose. Every slot spent on a threat is a slot not spent on an answer, and you only need one threat to stick.

Good control finishers share some traits:

- **They're hard to answer.** Planeswalkers dodge creature removal. Creature-lands like Celestial Colonnade dodge sorcery-speed removal and board wipes because they're only creatures when you animate them.
- **They protect themselves or generate value.** Teferi, Hero of Dominaria draws you a card and untaps lands so you can keep counter mana open. A threat that helps you stay in control is worth more than a bigger body.
- **They end the game reasonably quickly.** A finisher that takes ten turns to win gives the opponent ten more draws.

Don't overload on win conditions. If your deck has fifteen threats, it's a midrange deck with too many counterspells. Most control lists live in the range of a handful of finishers backed by a lot of interaction.

## Managing threats: what to answer and what to ignore

The central skill in control is threat assessment. You have a limited number of answers, so you can't spend one on everything.

Ask three questions about each card your opponent plays:

1. **Can it beat me if I ignore it?** A 1/1 with no abilities probably can't on its own. A planeswalker that makes a token every turn probably can.
2. **Can my next card answer it better?** If you have a board wipe in hand, let the small creatures come and wipe them together instead of spending spot removal on each one.
3. **What else might they have?** If the opponent has a known big threat still in their deck, don't spend your last counterspell on a mid-sized creature.

### Pick your counterspell targets

Holding up a counterspell creates a kind of standoff. The opponent knows you have mana open and may bait you with a weaker spell first. Save hard counters for the cards that would beat you if they resolved: the planeswalker, the card-advantage engine, the big finisher. Let the cards you can handle with removal resolve and deal with them later.

### Life is a resource, even for control

You don't need to stay at 20. Taking a few hits early so you can cast a board wipe on a fuller board is often the right play. What matters is that you stabilize at a life total the opponent can't close out with burn or a single attack.

## When to tap out

Tapping out means spending all your mana on your own turn, leaving nothing for instant-speed answers. Control players are often scared to do it, but refusing to ever tap out is its own mistake.

Tap out when:

- **The board wipe is worth it.** If the opponent has committed several creatures, the wipe is better than holding up a counterspell for a spell they may not cast.
- **You're deploying a threat they can't handle quickly.** Landing your finisher when the opponent has few cards in hand is how you convert your advantage.
- **The opponent is low on cards.** If they have one card in hand, the odds that it's the one spell that matters are lower. Use your mana.

Hold mana up when:

- **The opponent has a full grip and a known big threat.** This is the moment counterspells were built for.
- **You have instant-speed card draw.** Cards like Fact or Fiction let you pass with counter mana open and still use it at the end of the opponent's turn if they do nothing. That flexibility is one of control's biggest advantages.

A useful habit is to plan your mana before your turn begins. Ask, "If they cast their best card next turn, can I answer it?" If yes, you can spend the rest. If no, decide whether the play you're making is worth the risk.

## Patience, and knowing when to stop being patient

Control rewards patience. You'll often pass turns doing nothing visible, holding up mana and letting the opponent act first. That's fine. Most of your opponents' mistakes come from feeling pressure to do something into open mana.

But patience has limits. Every turn the game goes on, the opponent draws a card that could be the answer to your plan. Once you've stabilized and have a meaningful lead in cards, start pressuring. The best control players switch from defense to offense the moment it's safe, not three turns later. This idea, knowing whether you're the one who needs to attack, comes up in every matchup. It's the same skill a [midrange deck](/blog/mtg-midrange-deck-guide) uses when it changes roles.

## Common control mistakes

1. **Answering everything.** Spending premium removal on a weak threat leaves you without an answer for the strong one.
2. **Never tapping out.** Holding up counterspells every turn while the opponent plays around them wastes your mana and your time.
3. **Too few lands.** Control that misses land drops can't do two things in a turn, and that's how it falls behind.
4. **Too many win conditions.** Threats you can't use early dilute your answers. Keep the finisher count low.
5. **Waiting too long to win.** Every extra turn gives the opponent another draw. Close once you're safe.
6. **Ignoring the clock.** In tournament play, long control games risk going to time. Play at a steady pace and make routine decisions quickly.
7. **Keeping answer-light hands.** Control needs interaction early. A hand of lands and late-game cards often loses to a fast start. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) has worked control examples.

After the first game, control usually gets better. You know what the opponent is playing, so you can swap narrow answers in and dead cards out. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers how to plan those changes.

## The takeaway

Control is a plan with an ending: survive, stabilize, then convert. Run plenty of lands, a mix of counterspells and removal, a board wipe or two, card draw to stay ahead, and a small number of threats that are hard to answer. Spend answers on the cards that actually beat you, tap out when it gains you more than it risks, and start winning as soon as the board is safe.
