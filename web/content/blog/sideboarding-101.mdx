A sideboard is a set of extra cards, up to 15 in Constructed best-of-three, that you can swap into your deck between games of a match. It lets you fix your worst matchups: you lose game one to a graveyard deck, bring in graveyard hate, and play game two with a deck built to beat theirs. Good sideboarding is one of the clearest skill gaps between casual and competitive players.

This guide covers the rules, what to put in a sideboard, how to plan swaps, and the most common ways players hurt their own decks by sideboarding badly.

## What a sideboard is

In Constructed formats like Standard, Pioneer, Modern and Legacy, your sideboard is a separate pile of up to 15 cards that sits next to your main deck. The cards count toward the format's copy limit together with your main deck, so if you run three copies of a card in the main deck, you can have at most one more in the sideboard.

In Limited (draft and sealed), there's no fixed 15. Every card in your pool that isn't in your main deck is effectively your sideboard, along with basic lands. That gives you a lot of flexibility: you can change colors, swap in situational cards, or adjust your land count between games.

Commander is the exception. It's usually played as single, multiplayer games rather than matches, so a sideboard isn't part of how most groups play it.

## When you can sideboard

You sideboard between games of a match, not during a game and not before game one. Game one is played with your main deck as registered. After each game, both players can swap cards between their deck and sideboard before the next game starts.

Two rules keep this fair:

- **Your deck must stay legal.** In a 60-card format, your deck still needs at least 60 cards after you sideboard. The usual practice is to swap one card in for one card out so the count stays the same.
- **Reset after the match.** When a new match starts, you go back to your registered main deck and sideboard.

### Best-of-one vs best-of-three

In best-of-three, the first player to win two games wins the match. That's where sideboards matter, since games two and three are played with the decks adjusted to the matchup.

Best-of-one is a single game. It's common on MTG Arena for quick queues, and since there's no second game, there's no sideboarding between games. Decks built for best-of-one tend to put flexible answers in the main deck because they can't bring them in later. Decks built for best-of-three can keep the main deck focused and save the narrow cards for the sideboard.

## Building a sideboard: cards by job

Think of your sideboard as a toolbox. Each card should have a specific job against a specific kind of deck. A useful way to build one is to list the archetypes you expect to face (see [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained)) and ask which ones your main deck struggles against.

Here are the most common jobs and long-standing examples of each. Check which ones are legal in your format.

| Job | What it beats | Example cards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Graveyard hate | Decks that reuse their graveyard | Rest in Peace, Leyline of the Void, Tormod's Crypt, Relic of Progenitus |
| Artifact and enchantment removal | Artifact-heavy decks, enchantment auras, key permanents | Naturalize, Disenchant, Wear // Tear |
| Extra creature removal | Aggro and creature-heavy decks | Pyroclasm, Fatal Push, Path to Exile |
| Counterspells and discard | Combo and control | Duress, Negate, Thoughtseize, Counterspell |
| Lifegain and protection | Burn and aggressive red decks | Kor Firewalker, Celestial Purge |
| Shutting off activated abilities | Planeswalkers and key artifacts | Pithing Needle |

### Graveyard hate

Some decks treat the graveyard as a second hand: they discard creatures to return them, cast spells from it, or count cards in it. Against these decks, a single graveyard hate card can be game-winning.

Rest in Peace exiles every graveyard when it enters and keeps cards from going there afterward. Leyline of the Void exiles any card that would go to an opponent's graveyard instead, and it can start the game on the battlefield if it's in your opening hand. Tormod's Crypt and Relic of Progenitus are cheap colorless artifacts that any deck can play.

### Artifact and enchantment removal

Naturalize and Disenchant are the classic answers: destroy target artifact or enchantment. They're narrow in the main deck, since many opponents have no good targets, but excellent against decks that rely on artifacts or enchantments.

Pithing Needle takes a different approach. You name a card, and activated abilities of sources with that name can't be activated, unless they're mana abilities. It's a flexible answer to planeswalkers, equipment and artifacts with powerful abilities.

### Extra removal against aggro

Your main deck probably has some removal, but against a fast creature deck you want more. Cheap removal and sweepers like Pyroclasm come in, and slow, expensive cards go out. The goal is to survive the early turns and win the long game.

### Counters and discard against combo

Combo decks win by assembling specific cards. Your best tools are disruption that hits those cards before they resolve: discard like Duress or Thoughtseize to take pieces from their hand, and counterspells like Negate to stop key spells. Against combo, being proactive about disruption matters more than having answers to creatures.

### Lifegain and protection against burn

Burn decks aim at your life total with damage spells. Kor Firewalker has protection from red and gains you life whenever a player casts a red spell. Celestial Purge exiles a black or red permanent. Cheap lifegain and creatures that block well also buy time.

## Building a sideboarding plan

A sideboard without a plan is just a pile of cards. Before a tournament, write down a plan for each matchup you expect: what comes in and what goes out.

An example for a blue-white control deck against mono-red aggro might look like this:

| In | Out |
| --- | --- |
| 2 Kor Firewalker | 2 Dig Through Time |
| 2 Pyroclasm | 2 Negate |
| 1 Celestial Purge | 1 Sphinx's Revelation |

The principle: bring in cards that are strong in the matchup, take out cards that are weak in it. Against aggro, expensive card draw and counterspells for noncreature spells are weak. Against a slow control mirror, those same cards are excellent and the creature removal is weak.

A few habits make plans better:

- **Think about what your opponent will bring in.** If you know they'll board in counterspells against you, cheaper threats or instant-speed plays get better.
- **Keep the swap count even.** One in, one out keeps your deck at the right size and your mana base balanced.
- **Check your mana after.** If you swap out cheap cards for expensive ones, your [curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) and land count may need adjusting.

## Don't over-sideboard

The most common sideboarding mistake is bringing in too much. It's tempting to swap in every card that looks relevant, but each one replaces a card that was in your deck for a reason. Swap out too many and your deck stops doing what it does best.

Signs you've gone too far:

- **Your deck no longer has a plan.** If you've cut threats to fit in answers, you might stop the opponent and never win yourself.
- **You've weakened your mana.** Bringing in cards of a third color, or cards that need a lot of mana, can make your hands less consistent.
- **You've brought in narrow cards for a small part of their deck.** A graveyard hate card is great against a deck that relies on the graveyard and useless against one that only uses it occasionally.

A good rule: bring in cards only when they're clearly better than what they replace in this matchup. Three or four well-chosen swaps often do more than eight.

## On the play vs on the draw

Your sideboarding can change based on whether you go first or second in the next game.

**On the play,** you're ahead on tempo. Proactive cards get better, and cheap reactive cards slightly worse, since you'll often be the one making the first move.

**On the draw,** you see one extra card and react to your opponent's first plays. Cheap interaction gets better. Some players bring in an extra land or a cheap removal spell when on the draw against aggro, and take them out when on the play.

These are small adjustments, usually one or two cards. Don't rebuild your deck based on who goes first.

## Transformational sideboards

A transformational sideboard swaps a large part of your deck to change its plan entirely. For example, a combo deck might take out its combo pieces and bring in creatures, catching an opponent who sided in lots of anti-combo cards with nothing to use them on.

It's an advanced strategy. It can be powerful when the opponent overcommits to beating your main plan, but it takes up a lot of sideboard slots and requires knowing both versions of your deck well. Most players are better served by a sideboard of targeted answers.

## The short version

Build your sideboard around the matchups your deck struggles with, give every card a specific job, and write down an in/out plan before you play. Bring in only what's clearly better than what it replaces, keep your deck's plan intact, and make small adjustments for play or draw.
