In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), a combat trick is an instant that changes the outcome of a fight, usually by making a creature bigger, protecting it or giving it an ability like first strike. Used well, a trick wins a combat your opponent thought they'd won and kills one of their creatures for the price of a single cheap card. Used badly, it gets you blown out by a removal spell and costs you two cards for one. The difference comes down to timing, reading the board and knowing what your opponent might do in response.

This guide assumes you know the basics of combat. It covers when tricks are worth playing, the right moment to cast them, how to bluff with open mana, and how their value differs between Limited and Constructed.

## What counts as a combat trick

Combat tricks are mostly instants, plus a few other instant-speed effects, that you cast during combat. The common types:

- **Pump spells** give a creature a temporary boost. Giant Growth gives a creature +3/+3 until end of turn, and so does Brute Force.
- **Protection spells** save a creature from removal or damage. Gods Willing gives a creature you control protection from the color of your choice until end of turn, and Blossoming Defense gives a creature you control +2/+2 and hexproof until end of turn.
- **Ability grants** give a creature first strike, deathtouch, trample, flying or similar for the turn.
- **Flash creatures** can serve as tricks: casting one during combat creates a surprise blocker.

What they have in common is surprise. A trick works because your opponent made their combat decisions without knowing it was coming.

## Why tricks are powerful

Picture this: you attack with a 2/2. Your opponent blocks with a 3/3, expecting to kill your creature and survive. You cast Giant Growth. Your 2/2 becomes a 5/5, kills the 3/3 and survives.

In card terms, you spent one card (the trick) and your opponent lost one card (the 3/3). That's a one-for-one trade. But you also kept your creature, which would otherwise have died, and you used one mana to remove a creature that probably cost them more. That's a big swing in board position and tempo.

Tricks also work on defense. If your opponent attacks with a 4/4 into your 2/2 and you block, a +3/+3 trick lets your blocker kill the attacker and survive.

## The risk: getting two-for-one'd

Tricks have a real downside. A trick is only good if the creature it targets survives to use it. If your opponent responds by removing your creature, your trick fizzles and you've lost two cards to their one.

Here's how that goes. You attack with a 2/2, they block with a 3/3, and you cast Giant Growth. In response, they cast a removal spell on your 2/2. Their removal resolves first, your creature dies, and Giant Growth has no legal target, so it does nothing. They spent one card. You lost two.

This is called being **two-for-one'd** (or "blown out"). It's the main reason many players see pump spells as a risky category of card. A few ways to reduce the risk:

- **Cast tricks when your opponent is tapped out.** If they have no untapped mana, they usually can't respond.
- **Use protection tricks.** A trick that grants hexproof or protection can counter removal by making the creature an illegal target. If the removal spell's only target becomes illegal, it does nothing.
- **Watch for mana held up.** If your opponent has left mana open on your turn and skipped plays they could have made, they may be holding something.

## Timing: when to cast a trick

The right time to cast a trick is usually **after blockers are declared**, during the declare blockers step, before combat damage.

Here's why:

- **Before attacks**, a pump spell just shows your opponent your plan. They can block differently or not block at all.
- **After attackers are declared but before blocks**, your opponent sees the bigger creature and simply doesn't block it, or chump blocks. You've used the trick for a little extra damage at most.
- **After blocks**, your opponent is committed. Their blockers are locked in, and the trick changes the fight in your favor.

### Who acts first after blocks

After blockers are declared, the attacking player gets priority first. If you're the attacker, you can cast your trick right away. Your opponent can then respond with their own instant.

If you're the defender, you get priority after the attacker passes. That means the attacker may have already cast a trick. You can respond to it, or, if they pass without doing anything, cast your own trick then. If the attacker then responds with another trick, the last spell cast resolves first.

### When to cast a trick before blocks

There are exceptions. Casting a trick before blocks can be right when:

- **It gives evasion.** Granting flying or trample before blocks changes which blockers can stop the creature or how much damage gets through.
- **It forces through lethal.** If your opponent has to block everything to survive, pumping one creature can create a lethal attack they can't handle.
- **You're casting it in response to removal.** If your opponent targets your creature with damage-based removal at any point, a pump spell in response can save it.

## Bluffing with open mana

Tricks don't need to be in your hand to affect the game. Leaving mana untapped and attacking into a bigger creature suggests you have a trick. Many players will hesitate to block, especially in Limited, where tricks are common.

Bluffing works best when:

- **The attack makes sense only with a trick.** Attacking a 2/2 into an untapped 3/3 with two mana open looks like a trick, and opponents often respect it.
- **Your opponent is cautious or behind.** A player who can't afford to lose a creature will often avoid the risk.
- **You've shown tricks earlier.** If they've seen you cast a Giant Growth, they're more likely to believe in another.

Bluffing has a cost. If your opponent calls it and blocks, you lose the creature. And holding up mana to bluff means not using that mana for your own spells. Bluff when the potential damage or tempo is worth it, not every turn.

The reverse matters too: when you do have a trick, sometimes it's better to make your attack look ordinary, so your opponent blocks and walks into it.

## When tricks are worth a card

Tricks aren't automatically good. A trick is worth including and casting when:

- **It wins a fight that kills a creature.** A trick that turns a bad block into a dead creature is effectively removal.
- **It saves a creature from removal.** Protection tricks and pump spells against damage-based removal let you keep your best threat.
- **It pushes through the final points of damage.** In a close race, a pump spell can be the last few points you need.

A trick is a poor use of a card when:

- **It just adds a few points of damage** that don't matter to the race.
- **Your board is too weak to make it relevant.** If you're behind on creatures, a trick won't fix that.
- **Your opponent has lots of instant-speed removal** and mana open.

Whether you should be using tricks aggressively or defensively also depends on your role in the matchup. If you're the aggressor, tricks push damage; if you're trying to survive the long game, they save blockers. ## Combat tricks in Limited

In Limited, tricks are a staple. Draft and Sealed decks are built from whatever you open, and every set has several tricks at common and uncommon. Some reasons they play better in Limited:

- **Removal is scarcer.** Your opponent is less likely to have an instant-speed answer, so the risk of being two-for-one'd is lower.
- **Boards are full of creatures.** Creatures trade in combat much more often than in Constructed, so tricks have more chances to win fights.
- **Small swings matter.** In a board stall, one trick that kills a key blocker can open the game up.

Still, don't overload your deck with them. Two or three tricks in a Limited deck is often a good number, because too many leaves you with hands that need creatures to function. For how to weigh tricks against creatures and removal when you build, see [how to evaluate cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited), and for how deck-building differs between the two main Limited formats, see [draft vs sealed](/blog/draft-vs-sealed-limited-guide).

Before a Limited event, it also pays to know the common tricks in the set. If your opponent leaves two green mana open, knowing what a two-mana green trick does in that set helps you decide whether to block.

## Combat tricks in Constructed

In Constructed formats, most decks play fewer pure pump spells. Removal is more efficient, decks are built around specific plans, and a card that does nothing outside combat is often worse than a card that answers a threat or draws a card.

Tricks still have a place:

- **Protection spells** like Blossoming Defense are a natural fit for creature decks because they also stop removal.
- **Aggressive decks** sometimes use pump spells to deal surprise damage or to trigger abilities that reward casting instants.
- **Combo-style creature decks** use pump spells to push huge amounts of damage in one attack.

If your Constructed deck plays tricks, it's usually because they do more than one thing, such as protect a creature, trigger an ability or deal a surprise finishing blow.

## Common combat trick mistakes

1. **Casting tricks before blocks.** Your opponent just changes their blocks. Wait until after blocks.
2. **Casting into open mana carelessly.** You may get two-for-one'd. Think about what they could have.
3. **Using a trick for a few points of damage that don't matter.** Save it for a fight that kills a creature or saves yours.
4. **Forgetting tricks wear off.** A +3/+3 until end of turn doesn't last past the turn.
5. **Playing too many tricks.** A deck needs creatures for tricks to work.

For more general habits worth fixing, see [common MTG beginner mistakes](/blog/mtg-beginner-mistakes).

## The takeaway

Cast combat tricks after blocks, ideally when your opponent can't respond, and use them to win fights that kill creatures or save your own. Respect your opponent's open mana, use bluffs when the payoff is worth it, and include tricks mostly in Limited or when they double as protection in Constructed. A well-timed trick can turn a game around.
