A Magic: The Gathering (MTG) tribal deck is built around a single creature type, such as Elves, Goblins or Zombies, and wins by playing cards that reward you for having lots of creatures of that type. Each creature is fine alone, but together they pump each other, cost less, draw cards or make more copies of themselves. The result is a deck where the whole board is much stronger than its parts.

This guide covers the pieces that make a tribal deck work, how many creatures of the type you actually need, the classic tribes and their signature cards, and how tribal decks differ between Commander and 60-card formats.

## Tribal or typal? A note on the name

Players have called these decks "tribal" for decades, and that is still the word you will hear at most tables and in most deck lists. Wizards of the Coast has moved away from it in official language. The old Tribal card type, which let noncreature cards like Tarfire carry a creature type, was renamed Kindred, and Wizards now generally describes creature-type strategies as "typal."

The strategy is the same either way. This guide says "tribal" because most players still do.

## What makes a tribal deck work

A pile of creatures that share a type is not a tribal deck yet. What turns it into one is a set of cards that care about the type. Most of these fall into four jobs.

### Lords

A lord is a creature that gives other creatures of its type a bonus, usually +1/+1 and often a keyword. Lord of Atlantis is the classic example: it gives your other Merfolk +1/+1 and islandwalk. Goblin King does the same for Goblins with mountainwalk.

One lord turns a board of 1/1s into 2/2s; two lords turn them into 3/3s. Since most lords pump *other* creatures, two lords also pump each other.

### Payoffs that count the type

These cards scale with how many creatures of the type you control. Elvish Archdruid gives your other Elves +1/+1 and taps for one green mana for each Elf you control. Krenko, Mob Boss taps to create a 1/1 Goblin token for each Goblin you control, so it doubles your Goblin count every time it activates.

Counting payoffs are where tribal decks get explosive: each creature you add makes them better.

### Cost reducers

Some cards make creatures of the type cheaper to cast. Goblin Warchief makes your Goblin spells cost `{1}` less and gives your Goblins haste. In Commander, Dragonspeaker Shaman makes Dragon spells cost `{2}` less, which matters a lot when your creatures cost six or seven mana.

Faster deployment feeds directly into lords and counting payoffs.

### Enablers and card flow

Tribal decks spend cards quickly, so they need ways to keep their hands full and find key pieces. Some creatures draw cards when they enter, like Silvergill Adept for Merfolk. Others search for more of the tribe, like Goblin Matron. Type-agnostic support cards also exist: Kindred Discovery draws you a card whenever a creature of the chosen type enters or attacks, and Cavern of Souls is a land whose colored mana makes spells of the chosen creature type impossible to counter.

There are also changelings, creatures that have every creature type. Changeling Outcast or Mirror Entity count as an Elf, a Goblin and a Merfolk at once, so they fit any tribe.

## How many creatures of the type do you need?

The short answer: more than you think. Lords and payoffs only work if there are creatures of the type on the battlefield to benefit from them, and a lord with nothing to pump is usually a weak creature for its cost.

A practical rule of thumb:

| Deck size | Creatures of the type | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60-card Constructed | Roughly 20-28 | Enough that most opening hands have two or more, so lords have targets early |
| 100-card Commander | Roughly 25-35 | Enough to support counting payoffs while still leaving room for ramp, removal and draw |

These are starting points, not laws. A deck with many changelings or token makers can run fewer natural members of the type. A deck that leans on counting payoffs like Krenko wants to sit at the high end.

Also check your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained). Tribal aggro decks want lots of cheap creatures so the board is full when the lords arrive. If most of your tribe costs four or more, you will be slower than the archetype wants.

### Don't forget interaction

The most common tribal mistake is the "all creatures, nothing else" deck, which folds to the first big flier or combo piece it can't answer. Even a focused 60-card tribal deck wants a handful of removal spells, and a Commander deck needs a normal package of ramp, card draw and removal on top of the tribe.

## Classic tribes and their signature cards

Some creature types have been supported for most of Magic's history, so they have deep card pools across many sets. These are the long-standing favorites.

### Elves

Elves are the green ramp tribe. Their creatures are cheap and many of them make mana, so Elf decks can deploy a huge board quickly and then cash it in.

- **Llanowar Elves:** a one-mana 1/1 that taps for green mana. The model for every mana Elf since.
- **Elvish Archdruid:** a lord that also taps for mana equal to your Elf count.
- **Priest of Titania:** taps for green mana for each Elf on the battlefield.
- **Ezuri, Renegade Leader:** a legendary Elf that can give all your Elves +3/+3 and trample for one big attack.

Elf decks usually win by flooding the board, then pumping everything at once or spending their extra mana on a finisher.

### Goblins

Goblins are red's aggressive, chaotic tribe. Their creatures are cheap and often hit hard or come with haste.

- **Goblin King:** the original Goblin lord.
- **Krenko, Mob Boss:** makes huge numbers of Goblin tokens and is a popular Commander.
- **Goblin Warchief:** cost reduction plus haste for the whole team.
- **Goblin Matron:** searches your library for another Goblin.

Goblin decks aim to overwhelm opponents before they stabilize.

### Merfolk

Merfolk are blue's main tribe and one of the most lord-heavy. Many Merfolk lords give islandwalk, which makes them unblockable against opponents who control an Island.

- **Lord of Atlantis:** the classic two-mana lord.
- **Master of the Pearl Trident:** a later lord with the same effect.
- **Silvergill Adept:** a cheap Merfolk that draws a card.
- **Merrow Reejerey:** a lord that taps or untaps a permanent whenever you cast a Merfolk spell.

Merfolk combine an aggressive board with access to blue's counterspells, so they can protect their lords.

### Zombies

Zombies are mostly black and lean on recursion: bringing creatures back from the graveyard. That makes them more resilient to removal than many tribes.

- **Lord of the Undead:** pumps other Zombies and can return Zombie cards from your graveyard to your hand.
- **Undead Warchief:** reduces Zombie costs and gives a big power boost.
- **Diregraf Captain:** a lord that gives deathtouch and drains an opponent when another Zombie dies.
- **Death Baron:** gives Skeletons and other Zombies +1/+1 and deathtouch.

### Slivers

Slivers are the most unusual tribe. Almost every Sliver gives its ability to *all* Slivers, so each new Sliver upgrades the whole team. Muscle Sliver gives every Sliver +1/+1, and a winged Sliver gives them all flying.

The catch is that most Sliver abilities affect every Sliver on the battlefield, including an opponent's. In practice that rarely matters, since few opponents play Slivers. Sliver Queen, a five-color legendary Sliver that makes 1/1 Sliver tokens, is a well-known choice as a Commander.

### Dragons in Commander

Dragons are less a 60-card tribe and more a Commander staple. They are big, expensive flying creatures, so the deck's real work is ramp and cost reduction. The Ur-Dragon is a popular five-color commander whose Eminence ability makes your Dragon spells cheaper even while it sits in the command zone. Support cards like Dragonspeaker Shaman and Dragonlord's Servant lower costs further, and Scourge of Valkas deals damage whenever a Dragon enters under your control.

Dragon decks are slow, but a few Dragons attacking in the air end games fast.

## Strengths of tribal decks

**Synergy.** Every lord, payoff and cost reducer makes your other cards better. A board of individually average creatures can outclass a board of individually strong ones.

**Consistency.** Because so many of your cards share a type, many of them play a similar role. You rarely draw the wrong half of a combo, since almost any tribe member advances the plan.

**Clear deckbuilding.** The creature type gives you a natural filter. When you are deciding between two cards, the one that shares the type or rewards it usually wins. That makes tribal one of the friendliest [archetypes](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) for new deckbuilders.

## Weaknesses of tribal decks

**Board wipes.** A tribal deck puts a lot of its resources onto the battlefield. A single Wrath of God or Pyroclasm can wipe out most of your hand's worth of cards at once. Against decks with sweepers, holding back a few creatures is often right, even if it means a slower clock.

**Lords die.** Your best cards are also your most obvious removal targets. When a lord dies, every creature it was pumping shrinks, which can turn a winning attack into a losing one mid-combat. Plan attacks assuming your opponent might remove a lord in response.

**Weak filler.** The worst members of a tribe are often just bad cards. Playing one only because it has the right type can hurt more than it helps.

**Predictability.** Opponents can see your plan, and cards that punish wide boards work well against you.

## Tribal in Commander vs 60-card formats

The core idea is the same, but the formats push tribal decks in different directions.

**In 60-card formats** like Modern or Pioneer, you play up to four copies of each card. That means you can run multiple copies of your best lords and payoffs, so you see them often. These decks are usually aggressive, low-curve and focused. Whether a tribe is competitive depends on which cards are legal and the current environment, so check legality before you build.

**In Commander,** you have one copy of each card in a 100-card singleton deck, so you need many more different tribe members to reach the same density. In exchange you get a commander that is always available. Many tribal decks are built around a commander that is a payoff itself, like Krenko, Mob Boss or The Ur-Dragon, which gives the deck a reliable engine every game. Games are also longer and multiplayer, so tribal Commander decks can be slower and more value-oriented than their 60-card versions. ## The short version

A good tribal deck is more than creatures that share a type. It needs lords to pump the team, payoffs that count the type, cost reducers to deploy quickly and enablers to keep cards flowing. Aim for a high density of the type, keep some interaction, and respect board wipes by not overextending when you don't need to.
