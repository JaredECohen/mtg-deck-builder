A Magic: The Gathering format is a set of deck-building rules: which cards you may use, how many copies of each, and how big your deck must be. Every format plays by the same core game rules, but the card pool changes everything about how games feel. This guide walks through each major format, compares them side by side, and helps you pick one to start with.

If you are brand new to the game itself, read [how to play Magic: The Gathering](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) first. Everything below assumes you know what a land, a spell and a turn are.

## Constructed vs Limited: the first big split

Every format falls into one of two families.

**Constructed** formats let you build a deck ahead of time from any cards you own that are legal in that format. You arrive at the table with a finished deck you have tuned over days or weeks. Standard, Pioneer, Modern, Legacy, Vintage, Pauper and Commander are all Constructed formats.

**Limited** formats hand you a small, random pool of cards at the event itself, and you build a deck on the spot from only those cards. Draft and Sealed are the two main Limited formats. Nobody can show up with a better collection, because everyone starts from the same kind of sealed product.

The difference shapes what you need to enjoy each one:

- Constructed rewards preparation. You can research, test, trade for specific cards and refine a list over time. It also means you need to acquire the cards, and in some formats that adds up.
- Limited rewards on-the-fly evaluation. You have to judge cards quickly, read what your neighbors are taking, and make a coherent deck out of imperfect parts. You pay an entry fee and keep what you open.

Within Constructed there is another split worth knowing. **Rotating** formats (Standard) only use recent sets, so the card pool changes as new sets arrive and old ones leave. **Non-rotating** or **eternal** formats (Pioneer, Modern, Legacy, Vintage, Pauper, Commander) keep adding cards without removing old sets, so a deck you build can stay legal for years, subject to the format's ban list.

## Standard

Standard is the format built around the newest cards. It uses only the most recent Magic sets, and older sets rotate out periodically as new ones join. Wizards of the Coast announces which sets are in Standard and when rotation happens, so check the official format page for the current list rather than trusting a number you read somewhere.

**Deck rules:** at least 60 cards in your main deck, no more than four copies of any card other than basic lands, and an optional sideboard of up to 15 cards for best-of-three matches.

**What it feels like:** because the pool is small, Standard games are easier to follow. You will see many of the same cards across the table, and a new player can learn most of the important ones fairly quickly. Each new set noticeably shifts which strategies are good, so Standard rewards players who enjoy a changing puzzle.

**The trade-off:** cards you buy for Standard eventually leave it. They stay legal in non-rotating formats like Pioneer and Modern, but if you only want to play Standard, your collection has a shelf life. Standard is also widely played on MTG Arena, Wizards' digital client, which is a common way to try it without buying paper cards.

## Pioneer

Pioneer is a non-rotating format whose card pool starts with the Return to Ravnica set and includes every Standard-legal set released after it. Nothing rotates out, so the pool grows with every new expansion, minus whatever cards are on Pioneer's ban list.

**Deck rules:** the same as Standard: 60-card minimum, up to four copies of each card, up to 15 sideboard cards.

**What it feels like:** Pioneer sits between Standard and Modern in power and complexity. The pool is big enough for a wide range of strategies but leaves out the oldest and most powerful staples. Many players come to Pioneer when their favorite Standard cards rotate, because those cards remain legal here.

**Who it suits:** players who like Standard-style deck construction but want their decks to last.

## Modern

Modern is a non-rotating format whose card pool begins with Eighth Edition. It includes every core set and expansion released from that point forward, plus sets designed to be printed directly into Modern without passing through Standard first, such as the Modern Horizons sets.

**Deck rules:** 60-card minimum, up to four copies of each card, up to 15 sideboard cards.

**What it feels like:** Modern is fast and powerful. Efficient cards like Lightning Bolt are legal, games can end in the first handful of turns, and there are many distinct archetypes, from aggressive creature decks to combo decks that win in a single big turn. Knowing what opponents might be doing matters a lot, which makes your sideboard important.

**The trade-off:** the card pool is large and deep, so learning the format takes time, and competitive decks often cost more than Standard or Pioneer decks. Modern decks also tend to hold their relevance for a long time, which is a big part of the appeal.

## Legacy

Legacy uses nearly every card ever printed for tournament play. Instead of starting from a particular set, it starts from the whole history of Magic and removes cards through a ban list.

**Deck rules:** 60-card minimum, up to four copies of each card, up to 15 sideboard cards.

**What it feels like:** Legacy has access to some of the strongest spells in the game, including Force of Will, Brainstorm and Swords to Plowshares. Decks are extremely efficient, cheap interaction is everywhere, and games can swing on a single decision. Many longtime players consider it the most skill-intensive format there is.

**The trade-off:** some key Legacy cards come from Magic's early years and were never reprinted because they sit on Wizards' Reserved List, a commitment not to reprint certain old cards. That keeps the cost of entry high for many decks. Legacy is still popular in local communities and online, and its decks rarely become obsolete.

## Vintage

Vintage has the largest card pool of any sanctioned Constructed format. Almost every card is legal, and instead of banning most powerful cards, Vintage uses a **restricted list**: a restricted card is legal, but you may run only a single copy. A small number of cards are banned outright, mostly for reasons that have nothing to do with power, such as cards that involve gambling (ante) or physical dexterity.

**Deck rules:** 60-card minimum, up to four copies of each unrestricted card, one copy of each restricted card, up to 15 sideboard cards.

**What it feels like:** this is where you find the famous Power Nine, including Black Lotus and the original Moxen, plus cards like Ancestral Recall and Time Walk. Games are explosive and can be decided in the first turn or two. It is Magic with almost every limit removed.

**The trade-off:** the original printings of the most iconic cards are scarce and famously expensive, so Vintage in paper is the least accessible format by far. Many players experience it online instead, or at community events that permit proxies.

## Pauper

Pauper is a Constructed format defined by rarity: you may only use cards that have been printed at common. If a card was ever released as a common in an eligible product, it is generally legal, even if other printings are rare. Pauper has its own ban list.

**Deck rules:** 60-card minimum, up to four copies of each card, up to 15 sideboard cards.

**What it feels like:** you might expect a commons-only format to be weak, but Pauper is sharp and competitive. Classic commons such as Counterspell and Lightning Bolt show up, and decks are built around efficient, focused game plans. It plays more like a smaller Modern than like a casual kitchen-table format.

**Who it suits:** budget-minded players and anyone who wants a deep, competitive format without chasing rare cards. If cost is your biggest concern, our guide to [budget MTG decks under $50](/blog/budget-mtg-decks-under-50) is a good companion.

## Commander

Commander is the most popular casual format in Magic and works very differently from everything above. You build a 100-card deck that includes a legendary creature (or another card that says it can be your commander) as your **commander**. Apart from basic lands, every card in the deck must be a different card. This is called a **singleton** format.

**Key rules in brief:**

- Exactly 100 cards, counting your commander.
- One copy of each card, except basic lands.
- Every card must match your commander's **color identity**, the colors of the mana symbols on the commander.
- Your commander starts in a special zone called the command zone and can be cast from there.
- Players usually start at 40 life, and games are typically multiplayer, most often with four players.

**What it feels like:** Commander games are long, social and full of big plays. Because every deck is singleton, no two games play out quite alike, and politics (making deals, choosing whom to attack) is part of the experience. The format leans casual, and many groups talk about power level before playing to keep games fair.

Commander has enough depth that it gets its own guide: [what is Commander (EDH)](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) covers the full rules, color identity, commander tax and how players match power levels.

## Draft and Sealed

Draft and Sealed are the two main Limited formats. Both have you build a deck from cards you open at the event, using a **40-card minimum** deck rather than 60. Basic lands are normally supplied for free, so you only spend picks and pack slots on spells and nonbasic lands. There is no four-copy limit: if you open three copies of a great common, you can play all three.

### Draft

In a booster draft, a group of players (often eight) sits in a circle. Everyone opens a pack, takes one card, and passes the rest to the next player. You keep picking from the packs that come to you until they run out, then repeat with the next pack, usually alternating the passing direction. After the draft, you build a deck from the cards you picked.

Draft tests card evaluation and reading signals: if a color keeps flowing to you late in the pack, your neighbors probably aren't taking it, and it may be worth moving into.

### Sealed

In Sealed, you open a set number of packs yourself and build a deck from that pool, with no passing. Prerelease events, where players get early access to a new set, typically use Sealed. It is a relaxed way to explore a brand-new set, because you see a lot of cards and nobody is competing with you for picks.

**Why Limited is great for learning:** everyone is on equal footing, games use fewer cards, and you learn fundamentals like the [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) and creature combat quickly. Draft and Sealed are also offered on MTG Arena and Magic Online, and at most local game stores.

## Which format should you start with?

There is no single right answer, but a few honest questions narrow it down fast.

**Do you have a regular playgroup of three or more friends?** Start with Commander. It is built for multiplayer, the social side carries a lot of the fun, and you can begin with a preconstructed deck and upgrade it over time. It is also forgiving: one weak card in a 100-card deck rarely loses you the game.

**Do you want to learn the game as efficiently as possible?** Try Draft or Sealed. Limited strips the game down to its fundamentals, puts everyone on equal footing, and teaches card evaluation faster than anything else. Your local store's prerelease is an especially welcoming first event.

**Do you want one-on-one games and mostly play online?** Standard on MTG Arena is the smoothest entry point. The card pool is manageable, and you can learn what decks look like before spending money on paper cards.

**Do you want a paper deck that lasts?** Look at Pioneer or Modern. They are non-rotating, they have active tournament scenes, and the cards you buy stay legal for the long run (barring bans).

**Are you on a tight budget but want competitive games?** Pauper is the answer. It delivers real tournament-style Magic with commons.

**Already experienced and want the deepest pool?** Legacy and Vintage are waiting, though be realistic about the cost of paper decks before diving in.

Whichever format you pick, a few habits carry over everywhere: learn the core deck archetypes (aggro, control, midrange and combo), keep your mana curve in check, and play plenty of games before spending heavily on cards.
