To beat an aggro deck, survive the first four or five turns with your life total high enough that burn can't finish you, then win with cards that are individually stronger than theirs. That means cheap removal, good blocks, mana-efficient plays every turn and a willingness to trade early. Aggro decks are built to end the game before your best cards matter, so your whole job is to make the game go long.

This guide assumes you know the basics of combat and deck archetypes. If you're new to the idea of aggro as a strategy, start with the [deck archetypes overview](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained).

## Understand what aggro is trying to do

An aggro deck plays cheap creatures early, attacks every turn and finishes with burn or a final pump effect. It trades long-game power for speed. Its cards are usually weaker one for one than yours, but it gets to use them first.

That gives you two clear facts to work with:

1. **Aggro runs out of cards.** A typical aggro hand empties by turn four or five. If you're still healthy at that point, each of its topdecks is usually weaker than yours.
2. **Aggro needs every point of damage.** A deck built to deal exactly 20 damage can't afford to have attackers blocked, removed or neutralized. Every creature you stop costs it more than one card's worth of damage over the game.

So your goal isn't to race. It's to trade off their early threats, keep your life total out of burn range and reach the turns where your deck is better.

## Mulligan for the early game

Against aggro, the most important part of your opening hand is what it does on turns one through three. A hand of powerful four- and five-drops is a liability if you have nothing to do until turn four.

When you know you're facing aggro, especially in games two and three:

- **Value cheap interaction.** A hand with a one-mana removal spell and a two-drop blocker is often better than a hand with your best late-game threat.
- **Don't keep greedy two-landers on the play.** You can't afford to stumble on mana when the opponent is curving out.
- **Mulligan hands that do nothing early.** Under the London mulligan, a six-card hand with early plays usually beats a seven-card hand that sits there for three turns.

The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) covers the full keep-or-mull process. Against aggro, just weight the early turns more heavily than you normally would.

## Blocking: trade early and often

Many players who lose to aggro lose because they don't block. They protect their creatures for later, take damage and find themselves dead to burn on turn five.

### Trade when you can

Against aggro, trading a creature for an attacker is usually good for you. Aggro's creatures are its damage. Every one you remove reduces the number of turns it has to kill you. Your creatures, by contrast, are mostly there to stop damage. Their job is done when they trade.

### Chump-blocking is a tool, not a failure

A chump block is blocking with a creature that will die without killing the attacker. It's a bad trade in card terms, but against aggro it can be right when you're about to lose to damage. Save a chump block for when the damage actually threatens your life total, not to stop two points on turn three.

### Watch for tricks

Aggro decks often run cheap pump spells. When the opponent attacks a 2/2 into your 3/3 with mana open, consider that they might have a trick. Sometimes you block anyway because trading your creature for their trick is still fine. Sometimes you take the damage. The detailed mechanics of combat, including when tricks can be cast, are in [MTG combat explained](/blog/mtg-combat-explained).

## Life is a resource, so spend it wisely

Your life total isn't a score. It's a budget. Against aggro, the question isn't "how do I take no damage?" but "how much damage can I afford to take before I stabilize?"

- **Early damage matters most.** Two points on turn two often means a lot more by the end of the game, because the creature dealing it keeps attacking.
- **Count burn.** Many aggro decks can finish from six or seven life with burn spells like Lightning Bolt. Treat your real life total as whatever is left after the opponent's likely burn.
- **Avoid self-damage.** Painlands, shocklands paid with 2 life and other cards that cost life are a real cost in this matchup. Play shocklands tapped when you don't need the mana that turn.

## Cheap removal is your best friend

One-mana removal is the single best tool against aggro. It lets you answer a threat and still develop your board in the same turn, so you don't fall behind on tempo.

Classic examples include Lightning Bolt, Fatal Push, Path to Exile and Swords to Plowshares. What makes them good here isn't raw power. It's that they cost less than or about the same as the creature they kill.

Some guidelines:

- **Kill the creatures that scale.** A creature that grows, gains abilities or generates value each turn should die first. A vanilla 2/1 can sometimes be blocked instead.
- **Use removal on your turn when possible.** Unless you need to respond to a pump spell or kill an attacker mid-combat, killing a creature on your turn lets you plan the rest of your mana.
- **Don't save removal for a better target.** Against aggro, the best target is often the one attacking you right now.

## Lifegain buys time

Gaining life works especially well against aggro because it directly undoes the damage the opponent spent cards to deal. The best lifegain cards do something else as well.

Kitchen Finks is a classic example. It's a creature that gains you 2 life when it enters the battlefield, and with persist it comes back after it dies the first time, gaining you another 2. That's a blocker, lifegain and a second blocker in one card.

Pure lifegain that does nothing else is usually weaker. It doesn't affect the board, so the opponent keeps attacking. Prefer cards that gain life and block, kill a creature or draw a card.

## Sweepers: the card that breaks the matchup

A sweeper is a spell that damages or destroys many creatures at once. Against aggro, a well-timed sweeper can turn a losing game into a winning one, because aggro commits creatures to the battlefield quickly and has trouble rebuilding.

Cheap sweepers matter most. Pyroclasm deals 2 damage to each creature, and Anger of the Gods deals 3 damage to each creature and exiles any that would die. Bigger effects like Wrath of God destroy every creature, but cost four mana, and by then you may have taken a lot of damage.

How to get the most from a sweeper:

- **Don't overcommit your own creatures** before you cast it, unless they survive.
- **Wait a turn if you can afford it.** Letting the opponent add one more creature can turn a two-for-one into a three-for-one. Only wait if your life total can take the extra hit.
- **Know the follow-up.** After a sweeper, aggro's hand is often empty. Deploy a blocker or threat right away so its topdecks don't steal the game back.

## Your sideboard plan against aggro

When you sideboard against aggro, the goal is to lower your curve and raise your interaction.

**Bring in:**

- Extra cheap removal
- Sweepers, especially the cheap ones
- Efficient blockers with high toughness
- Lifegain attached to something useful

**Take out:**

- Expensive cards that do nothing in the early turns
- Slow card draw that doesn't affect the board
- Narrow answers aimed at other strategies, like counterspells for big spells the opponent doesn't play
- Cards that cost life to use

Don't overboard. If you strip out all your win conditions, you'll survive the early game and then have nothing to finish with. For more on building and using sideboards, see [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101).

## Common mistakes against aggro

1. **Racing when you shouldn't.** Unless your deck is faster, don't try to match their damage. Defend first.
2. **Not blocking to save creatures for later.** Against aggro, there may not be a later.
3. **Ignoring burn range.** If you're at six against a red deck with cards in hand, you're closer to dead than it looks.
4. **Keeping slow hands.** A hand with no plays before turn four is a mulligan in this matchup much more often than usual.
5. **Spending removal on the wrong threat.** Kill what scales or what would deal the most damage over time.
6. **Wasting mana in the early turns.** Against aggro, a turn where you leave mana unused and cast nothing is often the turn that loses the game. Plan your curve so every early turn does something.

## The takeaway

Beating aggro is about reaching the late game alive. Keep hands with early plays, block and trade early, treat your life total as a budget with burn subtracted, lean on cheap removal and sweepers, and pick lifegain that also affects the board. Once their hand is empty and you're stable, your stronger cards take over.
