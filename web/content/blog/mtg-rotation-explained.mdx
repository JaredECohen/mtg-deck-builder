Rotation is the process that keeps Standard limited to recent sets. Standard only allows cards from a small window of recently released sets, so when new sets are added, the oldest ones eventually leave. That departure is called rotation, and the cards from those sets are no longer legal in Standard. They don't disappear, though. Rotated cards stay legal in every non-rotating format that already allowed them, such as Pioneer, Modern, Legacy, Vintage and Commander, unless that format bans them separately.

This guide explains how rotation works, why Wizards of the Coast uses it, what happens to your cards afterward, and how to buy and build with rotation in mind. It intentionally gives no schedule. The rotation schedule has changed several times over Magic: The Gathering's history, so always check the most recent official announcement from Wizards before making plans around a date.

## How rotation works

Every Magic format has a list of sets whose cards are allowed. Formats come in two kinds:

- **Rotating formats** have a moving window. New sets join, and after some time the oldest sets in the window leave. Standard is the best-known rotating format.
- **Non-rotating formats**, often called eternal formats, only ever add sets. Once a set is legal there, it stays legal. Pioneer, Modern, Legacy, Vintage and Commander all work this way.

When a set rotates out of Standard, every card from that set loses Standard legality at once, with one important exception covered below.

### Legality is by card name, not printing

A card's legality is determined by its name. If a card from a rotating set is reprinted in a set that's still in Standard, the card stays Standard-legal, and every printing of it counts, including the older one you already own. This is why staple cards that get reprinted often survive rotation even when the set they first appeared in leaves.

The reverse is also true: a card doesn't become legal just because you own a newer printing of it. What matters is whether that card name appears in a currently legal set.

### Rotation isn't banning

Rotation and banning are different things, and they're easy to confuse.

| | Rotation | Banning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What triggers it | A planned schedule | A card proving too strong or unhealthy for a format |
| What leaves | Whole sets at once | Individual cards |
| Which formats | Rotating formats, mainly Standard | Any format, each with its own list |
| Effect elsewhere | None. The cards stay legal in eternal formats | Only the format that bans the card |

A card that rotates out of Standard can still be banned in Pioneer or Modern, and a card banned in Standard can still be legal elsewhere. Each format keeps its own banned list, so check the list for the format you're playing.

## Why rotation exists

Rotation can feel like it costs players something, so it's worth understanding why Magic uses it at all. There are several reasons, and they reinforce each other.

### It keeps Standard fresh

If Standard never rotated, the format would grow by several sets a year forever. The best decks would settle in and stay, and new sets would have to beat years of accumulated strong cards to matter. Rotation guarantees regular change: the decks people play have to be rebuilt from what's legal, and new archetypes get room to appear.

### It lowers the barrier to entry

A small card pool is easier to learn and cheaper to collect. A new player can join Standard knowing they only need cards from a limited number of recent sets, rather than decades of history. The [Standard format guide](/blog/mtg-standard-format-guide) goes into why that makes Standard a common starting point.

### It gives designers room

Some mechanics and effects only work well in a small environment. With rotation, designers can print a strong card for a specific theme and know it will eventually leave Standard instead of shaping the format forever. They can also bring back popular mechanics in new forms without worrying about every past printing.

### It manages power creep

Without rotation, every card ever printed would interact with every new one. Combos would pile up across years of sets, and designers would have to check new cards against an ever-growing list. Rotation keeps the interactions in Standard limited to a known set of cards.

The cost of all this is that Standard cards lose Standard legality eventually. Eternal formats exist partly so those cards still have somewhere to be played.

## What happens to rotated cards

Your cards don't go anywhere when they rotate. You still own them, and they're still legal in a lot of places.

### They stay legal in eternal formats

A card from a Standard set is generally also legal in Pioneer, Modern, Legacy, Vintage and Commander, unless one of those formats bans it. Pioneer and Modern each define their own starting point in Magic's history, and Standard sets from within those windows carry their cards in. The [formats overview](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) explains which formats include which sets.

That means a rotating card can find a second life:

- **In Pioneer**, many strong Standard cards keep seeing play after they rotate. The [Pioneer guide](/blog/mtg-pioneer-format-guide) covers how that format is built.
- **In Modern and Legacy**, a smaller number of rotated cards are strong enough to matter against older, more powerful cards.
- **In Commander**, almost any card can find a home. Rotation has no effect there at all.
- **At the kitchen table**, rotation doesn't apply unless your group decides it does.

### On MTG Arena

Magic's digital client has its own non-rotating formats, such as Historic, where cards from rotated Standard sets can still be played. If you mostly play online, rotation reduces where you can use a card but doesn't make it useless.

### Values often shift

Because demand for a card often comes partly from Standard players, cards that are only played in Standard tend to become less sought after once they rotate. Cards that also see play in eternal formats are less affected. That's a general pattern, not a guarantee, and this guide doesn't track prices.

## Planning a collection around rotation

Knowing rotation is coming changes how you should buy and build.

### Know where you are in the cycle

Before buying into Standard, check the most recent official announcement for which sets are legal and when the next rotation happens. A card from a set that's about to rotate has a short Standard life left. A card from the newest set has the longest. Since the schedule has changed over the years, don't rely on what you remember. Look it up.

### Prioritize cards with more than one home

Cards that are strong in Standard and also playable in Pioneer, Modern or Commander keep their usefulness after rotation. Lands that fix colors and flexible removal spells often fall in this category. Narrow cards that only work in one Standard deck are the most likely to sit in a binder afterward.

### Build around the newest sets

A Standard deck whose core comes from the most recent sets will survive the next rotation with fewer changes. Decks built mostly from the oldest legal sets will need a lot of replacing.

### Play the format that matches your goals

If you'd rather never think about rotation, a non-rotating format may suit you better. Commander and Pioneer both let you keep a deck for years with occasional upgrades. If you like a format that changes regularly and rewards learning new cards, Standard's rotation is a feature rather than a cost.

### Budget for change

Standard players tend to keep their spending manageable by trading, building one deck at a time, and moving rotating cards into casual or eternal decks rather than holding everything. ## Common rotation misconceptions

1. **"Rotated cards are banned."** They aren't. They're just outside Standard's window and remain legal in eternal formats.
2. **"My old printing is illegal now."** If the same card name is in a current Standard set, every printing is legal.
3. **"Rotation happens on a fixed date every year."** The timing has changed more than once. Check the official announcement for the current schedule.
4. **"Rotation affects Commander."** It doesn't. Commander has its own banned list and no rotation.
5. **"Rotation is the same as a ban list update."** Rotation removes whole sets on a schedule. Bans remove specific cards when Wizards decides a format needs it.

## The takeaway

Rotation keeps Standard small, fresh and approachable by moving the oldest sets out as new ones come in. Rotated cards stay yours and stay legal in Pioneer, Modern, Legacy, Vintage and Commander unless separately banned. Before you buy, check the official rotation announcement, favor cards that are useful in more than one format, and build Standard decks around the newest sets so they last longest.
