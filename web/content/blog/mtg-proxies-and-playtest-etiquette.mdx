A proxy is a stand-in card used in place of a real Magic: The Gathering card, usually a printout, a handwritten note or a basic land with the card's name written on it. Proxies are not allowed in sanctioned tournaments, but many casual groups accept them, especially for testing decks or playing cards that are hard to find. The rule of etiquette is simple: ask before you play one, and make it obvious that it's a proxy.

This guide covers what counts as a proxy, why sanctioned events don't allow them, how casual groups usually handle them, and the line that should never be crossed: passing a proxy off as a real card.

## What a proxy is

A proxy represents a specific Magic card during a game. Everyone at the table agrees to treat it as that card, with the same name, cost, types and rules text.

Common kinds of proxies:

- **Handwritten proxies.** The card's name, and often its cost and text, written on a basic land, a spare common or a slip of paper inside a sleeve in front of another card.
- **Printed proxies.** An image or text version of the card printed at home, cut out and sleeved in front of a real card for stiffness.
- **Placeholder cards.** A card from your collection standing in for another, with a note saying what it represents.

Players also use the term **playtest card** for a proxy that exists only to test a deck. The word signals intent: it's a temporary tool, not a replacement for the real card.

What makes something a proxy rather than a problem is that it's clearly identifiable as a stand-in. Anyone looking at it should be able to tell immediately that it isn't a genuine card.

## Why sanctioned tournaments don't allow proxies

Sanctioned events follow Wizards of the Coast's official tournament rules, which require you to play with genuine Magic cards. Proxies aren't allowed in your deck at those events, whether it's a local store event that's sanctioned or a large competitive tournament.

There's a narrow exception at some events: if a card in your deck gets damaged during the tournament, a judge may be able to issue a temporary replacement so you can keep playing. That's a judge's tool for that specific situation. It isn't something you can bring with you.

If you're not sure whether an event is sanctioned or what its rules are, ask the store before you register. Many stores also run casual nights with their own house policies, which may be more relaxed.

## When casual groups allow proxies

Outside sanctioned play, whether proxies are allowed is up to your group. Policies vary a lot, but most fall into a few patterns.

### "Proxy what you own"

Some groups only allow proxies of cards you already own. The idea is to avoid moving cards between decks: if you own one copy of a card and play it in three Commander decks, you can proxy it in two of them. This is one of the most widely accepted uses.

### Playtesting before buying

Many groups are happy to let you test a card before you buy it. Trying a card for a few games tells you whether it actually does what you hoped in your deck. It's a sensible way to avoid buying cards that end up in a box.

### Budget proxies

Some groups allow proxies of expensive cards so players with smaller budgets can compete on even footing. Others prefer everyone play only real cards. Neither is wrong; it's a matter of what your group enjoys.

### Limits and exceptions

Groups that allow proxies often add limits:

- **A cap on how many** proxies each deck can include.
- **No proxies of specific cards** the group finds unfun, regardless of who owns them.
- **Power-level expectations.** A group might allow proxies but still expect decks to match the table's power level. In Commander, the official brackets help everyone describe that.

These conversations are the heart of a [rule zero discussion](/blog/commander-rule-zero-guide), where Commander players agree on house rules before a game.

## How to ask

The polite approach is to ask before the game, not mid-game when the proxy hits the battlefield. A short, clear question is enough:

- "I've got two proxies in this deck, both cards I own in another deck. Is that OK?"
- "I'm testing a card I'm thinking about buying. Mind if I play a proxy of it?"

Then accept the answer. If someone says no, swap in a real card or play a different deck. It's their game too, and a casual group stays friendly when everyone feels the rules are fair.

At a store's casual event, ask the organizer first. Even if the players at your table don't mind, the store may have its own policy.

## Making proxies the right way

A good proxy is easy to read and impossible to mistake for the real thing.

- **Mark it clearly.** Write "proxy" on it, use an obvious placeholder card, or print it in a way that's clearly not an official card.
- **Make it readable.** Opponents need to know what the card does. Include the name, mana cost, types and rules text, or be ready to show the real card's text on request.
- **Keep it in a matching sleeve,** with a real card behind it if it's thin paper. That keeps your deck uniform when shuffling.
- **Don't keep it in your trade binder** or anywhere it could get mixed in with real cards.

When you're done testing and decide to buy the real card, retire the proxy. If you decide not to, you've just saved money and learned something about your deck.

## Playtesting before you buy

Proxies are most useful as a testing tool. A few ways to get the most out of them:

1. **Test against real opponents,** not just in solo goldfish games. A card that looks strong alone may be weak against interaction.
2. **Play several games.** One great draw doesn't prove a card belongs in the deck.
3. **Track what you would have cut.** Every new card replaces something. Make sure the new one is actually better.
4. **Compare a few options.** If you're unsure between two cards, proxy both and play each.

The guide on [how to playtest a deck](/blog/how-to-playtest-a-deck) covers structured testing in more depth, including how many games you need before trusting the results.

You can also test ideas before you even make proxies. Building a list in a deck builder like [MTG Deck Lab](/) lets you compare options, check your curve and see the shape of a deck before you print or buy anything.

## Proxies are not counterfeits

This is the most important line in the whole topic. A proxy is a stand-in that everyone knows is a stand-in. A **counterfeit** is a fake card made to look like a real one and passed off as genuine.

The differences are clear:

| | Proxy | Counterfeit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Represents a card in a game with consent | Imitates a real card to deceive |
| Appearance | Obviously not a genuine card | Designed to look genuine |
| Use | Casual play and testing, with permission | Sold, traded or played as real |

Counterfeits are a serious problem. Selling or trading a fake card as real is fraud, and it hurts the person who receives it, the stores that handle it and the trust that makes trading work.

So a few rules apply to every proxy you make:

- **Never sell or trade a proxy.** Even if you explain what it is, it can end up passed along to someone who doesn't know.
- **Never present a proxy as a real card,** in a game, a trade or a photo.
- **Never play a proxy in a sanctioned event.**
- **Destroy or clearly mark old proxies** when you're done with them, so they can't be mistaken for real cards later.

If you buy cards secondhand and suspect one is fake, talk to the seller or a store you trust before using it.

## Proxies across formats

How common proxies are depends on the format and setting. Casual Commander groups are the most likely to allow them, because Commander is a social format with a strong tradition of house rules. Competitive formats like Modern or Legacy are mostly played at sanctioned events, where proxies aren't allowed, though playtesting groups often use them in practice sessions. For an overview of how formats differ, see [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).

## The takeaway

Proxies are a tool for casual play and testing, used with the group's permission and always clearly marked. Ask before the game, accept the answer, and keep proxies out of sanctioned events. Use them to test cards before you buy, and never sell, trade or present one as a genuine card. Follow those simple rules and proxies make Magic more accessible without hurting anyone.
