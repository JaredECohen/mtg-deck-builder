Every Magic: The Gathering card has one of four main rarities: common, uncommon, rare or mythic rare. You can tell which by the color of the set symbol on the right side of the card, below the art: black for common, silver for uncommon, gold for rare and orange-red for mythic rare. Rarity tells you how often a card shows up in booster packs. It does not, by itself, tell you how strong the card is.

This guide explains each rarity, how to read the set symbol, why Wizards of the Coast uses rarity the way it does, what rarity means (and doesn't mean) for a card's power, and how special versions like foils and alternate art fit in.

## Where to find a card's rarity

The set symbol is a small icon printed on the right edge of the card, on the line between the art and the text box, next to the card type. Each set has its own symbol, which tells you which set the card was printed in. The symbol's color tells you its rarity.

On modern cards, the small print in the bottom-left corner also includes a letter for rarity next to the collector number, such as C for common or R for rare. That's handy when the symbol is small or hard to see.

If you're still getting used to all the parts of a card, the [guide to reading a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) walks through each one.

## The four rarities

| Rarity | Set symbol color | Typical role in a set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common | Black | The backbone of a set: simple creatures, basic removal and core mechanics |
| Uncommon | Silver | More complex cards, stronger effects and cards that define a strategy |
| Rare | Gold | Powerful or unusual effects, build-around cards and many legendary cards |
| Mythic rare | Orange-red | Splashy, flavorful showpieces and some of a set's most complex cards |

### Common

Commons are the most plentiful cards in a set. A booster pack contains more commons than any other rarity, so you'll see a lot of them. Commons are usually simpler, because they're the cards every player encounters, including newer players opening their first packs.

Being common doesn't mean being weak. Some of the most famous cards in the game have been printed at common, including Lightning Bolt, Counterspell, Llanowar Elves and Dark Ritual. Commons also matter a great deal in Limited formats like draft, where most of your deck comes from them.

### Uncommon

Uncommons appear less often than commons, with a few per pack. They tend to have more complex text or stronger effects. In many modern sets, uncommons are also where designers put cards that point you toward a specific strategy, like a gold card that rewards a particular two-color plan.

Sol Ring, one of the most famous cards in Commander, was originally printed at uncommon.

### Rare

Rares are scarcer still. They're where you'll find powerful, unusual or complex effects, cards that a deck can be built around, and many of a set's legendary creatures and planeswalkers. Rares are usually the most sought-after cards for Constructed play because of how many powerful effects sit at this rarity.

### Mythic rare

Mythic rare is the newest rarity, added to the game in 2008. Mythics show up less often than rares. They're used for cards that should feel special when you open them: famous characters, splashy effects, big dragons and angels, and many planeswalkers.

Mythic doesn't automatically mean "best card in the set." It means the card is meant to be rarer and more exciting to open. Some mythics are tournament staples. Others are fun but rarely played competitively.

### Other rarity markers

Over the game's history, a few other markers have appeared. Some special sets used a purple symbol for "timeshifted" or bonus cards. These are exceptions, and the four main rarities cover nearly everything you'll open.

## Why rarity exists

Rarity serves a few purposes for the people who design and develop Magic sets.

### Managing complexity

Commons are what new players see most. Keeping them simpler makes Limited games and first experiences with a set easier to follow. More complicated cards go to higher rarities so that a typical game has only a few of them.

### Shaping Limited

In draft and sealed, you play mostly with commons and uncommons. Designers balance those rarities carefully so that Limited games are fair and interesting. If a set's commons were wildly powerful, every draft would be decided by who opened the right one. Rarity spreads the power so that skill in picking and building matters more than luck. The [guide to evaluating cards for Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) shows how rarity interacts with what you should pick.

### Excitement in packs

Opening a pack is more fun when some cards are rare. Higher-rarity slots give each pack a chance at something memorable.

## What rarity does and doesn't mean for power

This is the part that trips up most new players.

### What rarity does tell you

- **How often you'll find a card.** Commons are easy to come by. Mythics are hard to find.
- **How complex a card tends to be.** Higher rarity cards are more likely to have unusual or complicated text.
- **How a card will matter in Limited.** A powerful rare is a rare sight in a draft. A powerful common might show up several times at the same table.

### What rarity doesn't tell you

- **Whether a card is good in your deck.** A card's strength depends on the format, the deck, and what else it's played with. A cheap common removal spell can be more valuable to a deck than a flashy mythic that does nothing on its own.
- **Whether a card is legal.** Rarity has nothing to do with whether a card is allowed in a format. That's decided by the format's rules and banned list.
- **How it compares across sets.** A common from one set can easily be stronger than a rare from another.

### Rarity can change between printings

Cards are often reprinted, and a reprint doesn't always use the same rarity as the original. A card might be common in one set and uncommon in another, or move up or down in rarity in a special product. When you look up a card, check which printing you're looking at.

### Formats built on rarity

A few formats use rarity directly. The best-known is Pauper, where only cards that have been printed at common are allowed. It shows how strong commons can be: Pauper decks can be fast, interactive and powerful. The [Pauper guide](/blog/what-is-pauper-mtg) explains the format in detail.

On MTG Arena, rarity also determines which wildcard you need to add a card to your collection, which is why the [MTG Arena beginner guide](/blog/mtg-arena-beginner-guide) treats rare and mythic wildcards as the scarce resource.

## Special treatments

Many cards are also printed in special versions. These treatments change how a card looks, not what it does or how rare its base version is.

- **Foil:** a shiny, reflective finish. Foil versions of a card show up less often than regular versions.
- **Borderless:** the art extends to the card's edges without the usual black border.
- **Extended art:** the art is widened to fill more of the card's frame.
- **Full-art:** the art covers most of the card, often used for basic lands.
- **Showcase and alternate frames:** some sets use special frames that match the set's theme.
- **Serialized and other premium versions:** a few products include individually numbered or otherwise limited versions of cards.

A special version of a card plays exactly the same as the regular version. The rules text is what matters. Tournament rules may ask that your cards are not marked or distinguishable in your deck, so make sure your sleeves hide any differences in finish or thickness.

## How to use rarity as a player

- **For deckbuilding, ignore rarity.** Judge cards by what they do for your deck.
- **For Limited, use rarity as a hint.** Rares and mythics are more likely to be bombs, but read every card.
- **For budget decks, lean on commons and uncommons.** They're often easier to get, and many are strong.
- **For collecting, rarity plus treatment matters.** Collectors often care about specific printings and finishes, and that is a separate question from how good a card is in play.

## The takeaway

Rarity is about how often a card appears, shown by the set symbol color: black common, silver uncommon, gold rare and orange-red mythic rare. Higher rarities tend to be more complex or exciting, but they're not automatically stronger, and plenty of the game's most important cards are commons and uncommons. Judge cards by what they do in your deck, and treat foils and alternate art as cosmetic.
