A strong budget Magic: The Gathering (MTG) deck comes from three choices: a format that rewards cheap cards, a mana base with one or two colors, and cards picked for the job they do rather than their reputation. Most of the money in an expensive deck sits in a handful of cards, and nearly every one of those cards does a job that a cheaper card can also do, a little less efficiently. Find those swaps and $50 goes a long way.

This guide covers principles, not a shopping list. Card prices move every week as cards get reprinted, banned, or picked up by a popular deck, so any price quoted here would be wrong soon. What stays true is how to spot where the money goes and how to replace it.

## Pick a format where cheap cards compete

The format you play decides more about your budget than any single card. Some formats are built so cheap decks can win. Others assume you own the expensive staples. (For a full rundown of each format, see [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).)

| Format | Why it suits a budget | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pauper | Only cards printed at common are legal, so every deck is built from the cheapest cards in the game | A few commons are in demand and cost more than you'd expect |
| Commander (lower brackets) | Casual tables expect focused, fair decks, not optimized ones | Mana rocks, dual lands and "auto-include" staples add up fast |
| Kitchen-table | You and your friends set the rules, including a budget cap | Agree on the rules before you build |
| Limited (draft, sealed) | You build from what you open, so everyone starts even | Each event costs money, but you keep the cards |

Pauper is the purest budget format because the rarity rule caps how expensive a card can be. It also has a real competitive scene, so cheap does not mean weak.

Commander is the most popular casual format, and it can be cheap or very expensive depending on the table. The official Commander brackets give groups a shared language for how powerful a deck is. Low-bracket tables are where a budget deck feels at home, because the other players aren't running the cards that would leave you behind. [Commander brackets explained](/blog/commander-brackets-explained) walks through the system.

A kitchen-table budget league is underrated. Set a dollar cap and nobody can buy their way ahead.

## Fewer colors means a cheaper mana base

In many competitive decks, the lands cost more than the spells. Dual lands that enter untapped and produce two colors are some of the most sought-after cards in the game, and a three- or four-color deck needs a lot of them to cast its spells on time.

A mono-color deck sidesteps this. Basic lands are free at most stores and cost almost nothing online, and they always produce the right color. A mono-red or mono-green deck can put every dollar of its budget into spells.

Two colors is the next best option. You can usually get by with basics plus a few cheap dual lands that enter tapped, or "gain lands" that enter tapped and give you a point of life. These are slower than the premium versions, but a two-color deck only needs a handful. Three colors and up is where budget mana bases start to hurt: more tapped lands, more hands that can't cast their spells, and more games lost to your own mana.

If you're building Commander, the same logic holds. One or two colors is comfortable; five colors on a budget will stumble often.

## Where the money goes: staples vs chase cards

Look at the price breakdown of an expensive deck and you'll usually see a steep curve: a few cards make up most of the cost, and the rest are cheap. Those costly cards come in two kinds.

- **Format staples.** Cards that show up in many decks across a format, like premium removal, efficient card draw, or the best mana fixing. They're expensive because so many players want them.
- **Chase cards.** Rare or mythic rare cards that are powerful, popular, scarce, or all three. Sometimes they're a deck's centerpiece. Sometimes they're just very good at a job a cheaper card can also do.

Neither kind is required to build a deck that wins games. Staples are expensive because they're slightly better than the alternatives across many decks, not because the alternatives don't work. A chase card that is the whole point of your deck is a different story. If a deck only works with one expensive card, pick a different deck rather than building a weaker version of that one.

## Replace cards by role, not by name

The best budget habit is to stop thinking of cards by name and start thinking of them by role. Every card in a deck is there to do something: kill a creature, draw cards, ramp your mana, pressure the opponent, or protect your plan. When a card is too expensive, ask what job it does and find the cheapest card that does the same job well enough.

Here's how that looks in practice:

1. **Write down the role.** "Instant-speed removal for a creature." "Two-mana card draw." "Ramp that also fixes colors."
2. **List the requirements.** Does it need to be an instant? Does it need to hit any creature, or just small ones? Does the mana value matter for your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained)?
3. **Search for cards that meet them.** Card search sites let you filter by type, color, mana value, and oracle text, which makes this quick.
4. **Accept a small downgrade.** The replacement might cost one more mana, be a sorcery instead of an instant, or have a minor restriction. That's the trade you're making, and it's usually fine.

A budget swap that matches the role keeps the deck's plan intact. A swap that just grabs a cheaper card in the same color can quietly break it: you end up with nine creatures and no removal, or a curve that peaks a turn too late.

<Callout title="Tip">
Swap the most expensive cards first. Replacing the top five or six cards by price often cuts a deck's cost by more than half, while the cheap cards stay as they are.
</Callout>

## Commons and uncommons that punch above their weight

Some of the most famous cards in Magic have been printed at common or uncommon, often many times. Reprints keep them easy to find, and they're still worth playing. A few long-standing examples by role:

- **Removal:** Lightning Bolt deals 3 damage for one mana and has been a benchmark for decades. Doom Blade and Pacifism are simple, reliable ways to deal with a creature.
- **Counterspells:** Counterspell is the original two-mana answer to anything.
- **Ramp:** Llanowar Elves taps for green mana on turn two. Rampant Growth and Cultivate fetch basic lands, which also fixes your colors.
- **Mana rocks for Commander:** Mind Stone and Commander's Sphere are cheap ways to get ahead on mana.
- **Card advantage:** Mulldrifter draws two cards when it enters the battlefield and leaves a flying body behind, and Divination is the plain "pay three, draw two" baseline.

These aren't consolation prizes. Pauper decks are built entirely from cards like these, and they play tight, interactive games. In Commander, a deck of well-chosen commons and uncommons with a clear plan will often beat a pile of expensive cards with no plan.

## Buy singles, not boosters

If you want a specific deck, buy the specific cards. Booster packs are great fun to open, and drafting is a good way to play, but as a way to collect a particular deck list they're a lottery. You'll pay for a lot of cards you don't need and may never open the one you want.

Buying singles, either from a local game store or an online marketplace, means every dollar goes to a card in your deck. A few habits help:

- **Buy the whole list at once** when you can. Shipping costs add up when you order a card at a time.
- **Look for older printings.** A card's cheapest printing is often an older one or a plain version without special frame treatments.
- **Check your local store's bulk bins.** Many stores sell commons and uncommons for very little, and a lot of good playables live there.
- **Trade with friends.** Your extra rares are someone else's missing piece.

Preconstructed Commander decks are a good exception. They're designed as a complete, playable deck and come with a mix of new cards and reprints, so they're often one of the cheapest ways to get a full 100-card deck to start upgrading.

## Proxies and playtesting etiquette

A proxy is a stand-in for a card you don't own, anything from a basic land with the card name written on it to a printed replica. Proxies are a great way to test a deck before you buy it. Play a few games, see what works, then spend money only on the cards that earned their spot.

A few ground rules keep proxies friendly:

- **Ask first.** Many casual groups are happy with proxies, especially for testing. Some aren't. It's the table's call, not yours.
- **Make them readable.** The card name and enough of the text to play it correctly is the minimum.
- **Know where they're not allowed.** Proxies are not legal in sanctioned tournaments. The official playtest cards Wizards of the Coast has printed are also not tournament legal, even though they're real, printed cards.
- **Don't pass them off as real.** Counterfeits are a different thing entirely. A proxy is a placeholder everyone at the table knows about.

## Try decks digitally before you buy

MTG Arena and Magic Online (MTGO) are both ways to try a deck before you spend money on paper copies. Arena is free to play and gives you cards and currency as you play, so you can build and test decks over time without paying. MTGO uses its own digital cards with a separate economy, and many Pauper players use it because the format has a long history there.

Neither has every card or every format, so check that the format you care about is supported. Even so, a few dozen games online teach you more about a deck than reading lists ever will.

## The short version

Pick a format that rewards cheap cards, keep to one or two colors, and replace every expensive card with a cheaper one that does the same job. Buy singles for the cards you need, test with proxies or online before you spend, and remember that a clear plan beats an expensive pile.
