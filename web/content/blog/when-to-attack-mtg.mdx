In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), attack when the attack improves your position more than it risks. In practice that means checking three things before you declare attackers: what the opponent can block with and what those blocks cost you, how the race looks if they hit you back, and what tricks their untapped mana could represent. If attacking gains you damage or forces bad blocks without leaving you dead or badly outnumbered on the crack-back, attack.

This guide is about the decision, not the mechanics. If you need a refresher on declaring attackers, blockers and damage, start with [MTG combat explained](/blog/mtg-combat-explained). Here we'll cover how to evaluate an attack, how to do racing math, when to keep creatures home, how to play around open mana, and how to use attacks to force chump blocks.

## The core question: what happens if they block?

Every attack starts with listing the opponent's possible blocks and asking how you feel about each. For each attacking creature, ask:

- **Can they block it profitably?** A profitable block for them kills your creature without losing theirs, or trades a worse creature for a better one.
- **Can they trade evenly?** Your 3/3 into their 3/3 trades one for one. Whether you want that depends on whose deck benefits from a smaller board.
- **Can they double-block?** Two creatures blocking one attacker can kill something bigger than either blocker alone.
- **Will they take the damage?** If no block is good for them, the damage goes through.

An attack is good when every reasonable response leaves you better off or even. If their best block wins them a creature for free, that attacker should usually stay home, unless you have a trick or a reason to want the trade.

### Attacks that can't go wrong

Some attacks are free. If your creature is bigger than everything they could block with, or has evasion they can't handle (flying against no fliers or reach), attacking costs nothing unless you need that creature as a blocker. Look for these first. Taking free damage every turn is how many games are won.

## Racing math

A **race** is when both players are attacking and neither is blocking much. Whoever deals lethal damage first wins. Racing math tells you whether you're winning that race, and it decides whether you should keep attacking or switch to defense.

### Count the clocks

Your **clock** is how many turns it takes you to kill the opponent if nothing changes. Divide their life by your damage per turn and round up.

Say the opponent is at 14 life and you attack for 5 each turn. 14 divided by 5 is 2.8, so you need three attacks. You're at 12 and they attack for 4 each turn. 12 divided by 4 is exactly 3, so they need three attacks too.

Now add **who attacks first**. If it's your turn and you're attacking first, your third attack lands before their third attack. You win the race by a turn. If it's their turn, they get there first.

### Make the race work for you

Once you know the clocks, look for changes that swing them:

- **One more point of damage per turn** can drop your clock by a full turn. In the example above, attacking for 7 instead of 5 turns three attacks into two.
- **One chump block** delays their clock by a turn. If they're one turn ahead in the race, a single chump block can flip it.
- **Life gain and burn** change the math directly. A burn spell to the face shortens your clock; a lifegain card lengthens theirs.
- **Haste and flash creatures** add damage the opponent didn't account for.

The key habit is to redo the math every turn. Clocks change whenever a creature enters or leaves, and the player who notices first usually wins.

### When you're losing the race

If the math says you lose the race, stop racing. Keep creatures back to block, trade where you can, and look for a turn when the board shifts in your favor. Continuing to attack while losing a race just gets you killed faster. Knowing whether you're the aggressor or the defender in a matchup is the subject of [who's the beatdown](/blog/whos-the-beatdown-mtg), which pairs well with this guide.

## Holding back blockers

Attacking with everything is tempting. The cost is that tapped creatures can't block on the opponent's turn (unless they have vigilance). Before attacking, look at the **crack-back**: the attack the opponent can make on their next turn against whatever you've left untapped.

Ask these questions:

- **Can they kill me on the crack-back?** Add up the power of every creature they could attack with, plus any haste threats or burn they might have. If that's lethal or close to it against your untapped blockers, keep enough home to survive.
- **Will my attack leave me facing a worse trade?** If you attack with your best blocker and it trades, you might lose the ability to stop their biggest threat.
- **Is the damage worth the exposure?** Sending a 2/2 in for 2 damage while their 4/4 then hits you for 4 is a bad deal unless the race math says otherwise.

A useful compromise is to attack with the creatures that are bad at blocking (small attackers, creatures with evasion) and keep the good blockers home. A 1/1 flier deals its damage just as well whether or not your 4/5 ground creature stays back.

### Vigilance and defensive bodies

Creatures with vigilance don't tap to attack, so they attack and block in the same round. Treat them as free attackers whenever the attack is safe on its own. Creatures with high toughness and low power usually belong on defense; their job is to make the opponent's attacks bad.

## Attacking into open mana

When the opponent has untapped mana during your attack, they might have an instant: a combat trick, a removal spell, or a creature with flash. Playing around everything means never attacking, so think about what they plausibly have.

**Look at what the mana can pay for.** One untapped land represents a cheap trick or removal. Four untapped lands on your turn, from a player who passed without casting anything on their own turn, often means they're holding something.

**Ask what happens if they have it.** If they have a pump spell and your attacker dies, how bad is that? If losing that creature to a trick would lose you the game, attack more carefully. If it's just a trade you can absorb, attack and make them use the trick.

**Ask what happens if they don't.** If the opponent has nothing and you didn't attack, you gave up free damage. Over a whole game, being too cautious is often a bigger leak than occasionally walking into a trick.

**Play to your outs and theirs.** Attacking with a creature you don't mind losing first can draw out a trick before your important attackers commit. And if you're holding your own trick, attacking into their open mana with mana up yourself puts the pressure back on them. The [combat tricks guide](/blog/combat-tricks-guide) covers how to use and play around instants in combat.

### Instant-speed removal and attacking

Some removal can only be cast at instant speed or is best used during combat. A creature that attacks alone into open mana can be removed without the opponent losing anything in a block. If you have several attackers and the opponent removes one, the others still connect. When you suspect removal, attacking with more creatures at once is usually better than attacking with one big one.

## Forcing chump blocks

A **chump block** is a block where the blocking creature dies without killing the attacker, usually just to prevent damage. When the opponent is low on life, every attack threatens lethal, and they have to chump to survive. That costs them a creature each turn.

This is one of the most powerful ways to close a game. Once you have more damage on the board than they have life, they must chump or die, and each chump shrinks their board while yours stays intact. A few principles:

- **Get them low first.** Chump blocks only become necessary when the damage would be lethal or close to it. Early damage, even when it looks small, is what sets this up later.
- **Present more lethal threats than they have blockers.** If they can chump only two of your three attackers and the third is lethal, they're in trouble.
- **Use evasion.** Fliers, menace (needs two blockers) and trample make chumping less effective or impossible.
- **Don't get greedy.** Forcing chumps is a slow squeeze. Keep enough defense home that a desperate counterattack doesn't swing the race.

## A quick attack checklist

Run through this before declaring attackers, especially in close games:

1. What are their possible blocks, and do I like each outcome?
2. What are both clocks, and who wins the race right now?
3. If I attack, what can they hit me with on the crack-back?
4. What could their untapped mana represent, and can I live with it?
5. Does this attack get them closer to the point where they must chump?

Most attack mistakes come from skipping step 3, sending everything in and losing to a counterattack. Attacking too timidly is the other classic error, one of the habits in [common MTG beginner mistakes](/blog/mtg-beginner-mistakes).

## Takeaway

Attack when the blocks are bad for the opponent, when the race math says you win, and when you can survive the crack-back. Respect open mana without being frozen by it, and once the opponent is low, use your attacks to force chump blocks every turn.
