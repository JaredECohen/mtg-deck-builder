A tempo deck is a Magic: The Gathering deck that plays a few cheap, efficient threats early and then spends the rest of the game using cheap interaction, like counterspells, bounce and one-mana removal, to keep the opponent from catching up. It doesn't try to win the long game or flood the board. It tries to get slightly ahead fast and protect that lead until its creatures finish the job.

This guide is about the deck: what goes in it, how to build one and how to play it. If you want the underlying idea of tempo as a resource that every deck uses, read [what tempo means in MTG](/blog/tempo-explained-mtg) first. For how tempo compares with aggro, control, midrange and combo, see [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained).

## What a tempo deck looks like

Most tempo decks share the same skeleton:

- **A low curve.** Almost everything costs one or two mana. A typical list has very few cards above three.
- **A small number of efficient threats.** Usually 8 to 14 creatures, often evasive (flying) or hard to answer, that hit for a meaningful amount early.
- **Cheap, flexible interaction.** Counterspells, bounce and removal that cost one or two mana, so the deck can deploy a threat and still hold up an answer in the same turn.
- **Card selection.** Cantrips like Brainstorm, Ponder, Preordain and Opt smooth draws so a deck with few lands keeps finding what it needs.
- **Fewer lands than other decks.** Because the curve is so low, many tempo lists run around 18 to 20 lands in 60 cards.

Blue is the classic tempo color because it owns cheap counterspells, bounce and card selection. It's usually paired with red for burn, green for efficient creatures, or black for cheap removal and discard. Mono-blue tempo decks also exist, especially in formats built around commons.

## How a tempo deck wins

A tempo deck wins by doing more with its mana each turn than the opponent does, for just long enough. A typical game goes like this:

1. **Turn one or two:** play a cheap threat.
2. **The next few turns:** attack, and use your remaining mana to counter, bounce or kill whatever the opponent does to stabilize.
3. **Mid game:** the opponent is low on life and behind on board. One more threat or a burn spell ends it.

The deck rarely has a big board. Often a single creature backed by two or three answers is enough, because each answer costs less than the thing it stops. The opponent spends four mana and a card, you spend one mana and a card, and the creature keeps attacking.

## Typical tempo cards

The names change across formats, but the roles stay the same. These long-standing cards show what each role looks like.

### Threats

- **Delver of Secrets.** A one-mana 1/1 that checks the top card of your library each upkeep and transforms into a 3/2 flier, Insectile Aberration, if you reveal an instant or sorcery. In a deck packed with cheap spells, it's a one-mana 3/2 flier on turn two surprisingly often. Tempo decks are often called "Delver decks" because of it.
- **Snapcaster Mage.** A two-mana creature with flash that gives an instant or sorcery in your graveyard flashback until end of turn. It's a body and a second copy of your best cheap spell.
- **Spellstutter Sprite.** A two-mana flash flier that counters a spell with mana value up to the number of Faeries you control when it enters. A creature and a counterspell in one card.
- **Ninja of the Deep Hours.** Its ninjutsu ability lets you return an unblocked attacker to your hand and put the Ninja onto the battlefield attacking, drawing a card when it connects. It turns a cheap evasive creature into card flow.
- **Young Pyromancer** and **Monastery Swiftspear** in red-based lists, which reward casting lots of cheap instants and sorceries.

### Counterspells

- **Counterspell.** Two blue mana, counter target spell. The standard.
- **Mana Leak.** Counters a spell unless its controller pays three more. Strong early, weaker late when the opponent has spare mana.
- **Spell Pierce.** One mana, counters a noncreature spell unless its controller pays two. Excellent against removal aimed at your threat.
- **Daze.** Can be cast by returning an Island to your hand instead of paying its cost, and counters a spell unless its controller pays one. A free counter that costs you a land drop.
- **Remand.** Counters a spell and returns it to its owner's hand, then draws you a card. It doesn't remove the card, but it wastes the opponent's whole turn of mana.

### Bounce and cheap removal

- **Vapor Snag** and **Unsummon.** One mana to return a creature to its owner's hand. Bounce doesn't get rid of a creature permanently, but it costs the opponent another turn of mana to replay it.
- **Lightning Bolt.** One mana, 3 damage to any target. Kills most early creatures or finishes the opponent.
- **Petty Theft**, the adventure half of **Brazen Borrower**, bounces any nonland permanent an opponent controls, and the card later becomes a three-mana flash flier.

### Card selection

**Brainstorm**, **Ponder**, **Preordain** and **Opt** keep the engine running. They're card-neutral but let a deck with few lands and few threats see the right cards at the right time. They also turn Delver of Secrets over.

## How to build a tempo deck

Start with these questions rather than a card list.

### 1. Pick your threats first

Choose 8 to 14 creatures that are cheap and threatening on their own. Good tempo threats share some of these traits:

- They cost one or two mana, or can be cast at instant speed.
- They're evasive, so the opponent can't just block them.
- They generate value or are hard to remove profitably.

A threat that needs support to be good is a poor fit, because tempo decks only deploy a few creatures and need each one to matter.

### 2. Fill out cheap interaction

Next, add 12 to 18 pieces of interaction. Mix types so you're not helpless against any one plan:

- Hard counters for the opponent's best spells.
- Soft counters (Mana Leak, Spell Pierce) for early turns.
- Bounce for blockers and for buying a turn.
- Cheap removal for opposing early creatures.

Lean toward instants. Instant-speed interaction lets you pass the turn with mana open and decide later whether to use it.

### 3. Add card selection

Six to eight cantrips is common in blue tempo shells. They let you run fewer lands, find threats when you need pressure and find answers when you need defense.

### 4. Keep the curve low and the land count lean

Check your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained). If you have more than a couple of cards at three mana or above, ask whether each one is worth it. A low curve is what lets you cast a threat and hold up an answer in the same turn, which is the whole point of the deck.

### 5. Choose a second color for what blue lacks

- **Red** adds burn for reach and removal.
- **Green** adds efficient, hard-to-kill creatures.
- **Black** adds cheap removal and discard.
- **White** adds efficient exile-based removal.

## How to pilot a tempo deck

Tempo is one of the hardest archetypes to play well, because almost every turn asks you to choose between developing and holding up an answer.

### Deploy a threat early, then protect it

The ideal start is a threat on turn one or two, then using leftover mana each turn for interaction. A tempo deck without a threat on the board is just a pile of answers with no clock.

### Leave mana up at the right times

The question each turn is: "Is it better to add pressure or to stop their next play?" Usually you want to tap out early to establish a threat, then play at instant speed once it's attacking. Casting a second threat while the first is still working is often a mistake if it means you can't answer their key spell.

### Spend answers on what matters

You have a limited number of cheap answers. Save them for the cards that would actually stop your plan: blockers that wall your threat, removal that kills it, or a big spell that turns the game. Don't counter a small creature that can't block your flier.

### Use bounce on tempo-heavy targets

Bounce is strongest on something the opponent spent a lot of mana on, on a creature carrying an Aura or counters, or on a token, which stops existing when it leaves the battlefield. It's weakest on a cheap creature with a strong enters-the-battlefield ability, since they get to use it again.

### Know when to switch roles

If the opponent's deck is faster than yours, you're the defender. Use your cheap answers on their threats and win later. If their deck is slower, you're the attacker. Keep the pressure on and save answers for the cards that would let them catch up.

## Where tempo decks struggle

- **Long games.** Tempo decks trade some card quality for speed. If the opponent survives and stabilizes, their more powerful cards usually win.
- **Cheap, efficient threats from the opponent.** Aggro decks with many one-drops are hard to stop with one-for-one counters and bounce.
- **Board wipes and mass removal.** Tempo decks put few creatures on the board, which helps, but losing your only threat to a sweeper can leave you with no clock.
- **Mana flood.** With few threats and a low curve, drawing lots of lands leaves you with nothing to do. Cantrips help but don't solve it.
- **Opponents with lots of mana.** Soft counters like Mana Leak and Daze get much worse once the opponent can pay the extra cost.

## Tempo in different formats

Tempo shows up wherever there are cheap threats and cheap interaction to support them. In Pauper, where every card is a common, blue tempo decks built around Delver of Secrets, Faeries and Ninjas have a long history; [what is Pauper](/blog/what-is-pauper-mtg) explains the format. Eternal formats like Legacy have cheap counters such as Daze and Force of Will to support aggressive tempo lists. In newer formats the names change, but the shell of efficient threats, instant-speed answers and cantrips stays recognizable.

## The takeaway

A tempo deck is cheap threats plus cheap answers, built so you can attack and interact in the same turn. Choose threats that matter alone, back them with instant-speed interaction and cantrips, keep the curve low, and spend answers only on the cards that would stop you. Win before the opponent's bigger cards take over.
