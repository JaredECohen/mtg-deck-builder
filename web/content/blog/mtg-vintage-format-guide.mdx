Vintage is the Constructed format with the largest card pool in Magic: nearly every card ever printed is legal. Instead of banning the most powerful cards, Vintage puts them on a **restricted list**, which means you may play exactly one copy of each. That single rule is why Vintage is the only format where you can legally cast Black Lotus, Ancestral Recall and Time Walk, and why its games can be decided before either player has taken a second turn.

This guide explains how the restricted list works, what the famous Power Nine actually do, why the format feels the way it does, and how players get into a format whose most iconic cards are famously scarce. If you want the short comparison of every format first, start with [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).

## What Vintage is

Vintage is a non-rotating Constructed format, sanctioned by Wizards of the Coast, that allows cards from every set in Magic: The Gathering's history. The basic deck rules match other 60-card formats:

- A main deck of at least 60 cards.
- Up to four copies of any card with the same name, except basic lands, which are unlimited.
- Only one copy of each restricted card.
- An optional sideboard of up to 15 cards.

The format grew out of the earliest days of tournament Magic, when the only real limit on a deck was which cards you owned. Over time, players and Wizards realized that some early cards were far too strong to allow four copies of, but that taking them out of the game entirely would erase a big part of Magic's history. The restricted list was the compromise: those cards stay playable, just not in multiples.

### Restricted versus banned

A **banned** card can't be in your deck at all. A **restricted** card can be in your deck, but only one copy, counting both the main deck and the sideboard together.

Vintage bans very few cards, and the reasons are mostly practical rather than about power. Cards that involve playing for ante (wagering cards you own) are banned in every sanctioned format. So are cards that require physical dexterity, like flipping a card onto the table, and a handful of cards whose rules simply don't work in a tournament setting. Everything else powerful enough to be a problem goes to the restricted list.

Compare that with Legacy, which uses nearly the same card pool but bans the cards Vintage restricts. That one difference shapes everything about how the two formats play. The [Legacy format guide](/blog/mtg-legacy-format-guide) covers its side of that split.

The exact contents of the restricted list change from time to time, so check the official Vintage banned and restricted announcement from Wizards before you build. The principle stays the same: a card goes on the list when four copies of it would make the format about that card.

## The Power Nine

The Power Nine are nine cards from Magic's earliest sets that are widely considered the most powerful cards ever printed. All nine are restricted in Vintage. They come in three groups.

### Black Lotus

Black Lotus costs `{0}`. You can sacrifice it to add three mana of any one color. Playing it on your first turn alongside a land means you can cast a four-mana spell before your opponent has done anything. It's only usable once, but in a format where a single big early play can win, that's plenty.

### The five Moxen

Mox Pearl, Mox Sapphire, Mox Jet, Mox Ruby and Mox Emerald each cost `{0}` and tap for one mana of their color (white, blue, black, red and green). They're artifacts, not lands, so you can play one on the same turn as your land drop. Each Mox is effectively a land that costs nothing and doesn't use your land drop, which accelerates every turn of the game, not just the first.

### Ancestral Recall, Time Walk and Timetwister

The three blue spells in the Power Nine are about card advantage and turns:

- **Ancestral Recall** costs `{U}` and has target player draw three cards. Three cards for one mana, at instant speed, is a rate no modern card comes close to.
- **Time Walk** costs `{1}{U}` and gives you an extra turn. That means an extra untap, an extra draw, an extra land drop and an extra attack, for two mana.
- **Timetwister** costs `{2}{U}`. Each player shuffles their hand and graveyard into their library, then draws seven cards. In a deck that empties its hand quickly with cheap artifacts, that's a refill to a full grip.

These cards are powerful because of how much they give for how little. Magic's designers learned from them, and modern cards that draw three or take an extra turn cost far more mana.

## Why Vintage games feel so explosive

Vintage is fast because almost every limit other formats impose has been removed. A few features stand out.

**Free and cheap mana.** Between Black Lotus, the Moxen and other fast mana like Sol Ring, a Vintage deck can have three or four mana available on its first turn. Spells that are fair at their printed cost become devastating when they arrive two or three turns early.

**Restricted cards still show up.** You might think one copy of a card in a 60-card deck rarely matters. But Vintage decks are full of efficient card draw and tutors (cards that search your library for another card), such as Demonic Tutor and Vampiric Tutor. Those let a deck find its single copy of a key card far more often than you would expect from the raw odds.

**Cheap, powerful interaction.** Force of Will can be cast without paying its mana cost by exiling a blue card from your hand and paying 1 life, and it counters a spell. Because the format is so fast, free and nearly free answers like this are a big part of why games aren't decided purely by who goes first. A Vintage player expects to be tested on their opening turns and plans accordingly.

**Combo is everywhere.** With so much card selection and so much mana, decks that assemble a game-winning combination of cards are a natural fit. The [combo decks guide](/blog/mtg-combo-decks-explained) explains the general archetype; in Vintage, combo is less a single deck and more a threat every player keeps in mind.

The result is a format where a turn-one play can decide the game, but where skilled players also have the tools to stop it. Games often hinge on a small number of high-stakes decisions: whether to counter this spell or save the answer for the next one, whether to keep a hand that's explosive but fragile.

## The kinds of decks you'll see

Specific Vintage decklists shift as new cards are printed and the restricted list changes, so this section describes the long-standing families of strategies rather than a current metagame.

### Blue control and tempo

Blue decks are the backbone of Vintage because the most powerful restricted cards are blue. These decks play cheap card draw, counterspells like Force of Will, and their copies of the blue Power Nine, then win with an efficient threat. They aim to control the key turns of the game and outdraw everyone.

### Artifact prison and aggro

Mishra's Workshop is a land that taps for three colorless mana, which can only be spent to cast artifact spells. Decks built around it play cheap artifact creatures and artifacts that make spells cost more or restrict what opponents can do. These decks try to lock the opponent out of casting spells while beating down with artifact creatures.

### Graveyard decks

Bazaar of Baghdad is a land that, when tapped, draws you two cards and then makes you discard three. That's terrible in a normal deck. In a deck that wants cards in its graveyard and can use them from there, it's a powerful engine. Graveyard strategies in Vintage can function with few or no spells cast in the traditional way, which makes them a very different kind of opponent.

### Big mana and combo

Some decks use the format's fast mana to cast huge spells early, or to assemble a combo that ends the game on the spot. They lean on tutors to find their pieces and on protection to force them through.

Because these strategies attack from such different angles (the stack, the graveyard, mana and artifacts), sideboarding matters enormously in Vintage. A card that is excellent against one family can be a blank against another. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers the general approach.

## Accessibility: how people actually play Vintage

Here's the honest trade-off. The original printings of the Power Nine and several other key cards were printed in small quantities in Magic's first years, and Wizards has a long-standing policy against reprinting a list of those early cards. That makes a complete paper Vintage deck one of the least attainable things in the hobby.

Players have found a few ways around that.

**Playing online.** Digital versions of Vintage give players access to the card pool without needing the original paper cards. This is how many players first experience the format.

**Proxy-friendly community events.** A proxy is a stand-in for a card you don't own. Wizards' own sanctioned tournaments generally don't allow proxies, but many community-run Vintage events and local groups do, sometimes with a limit on how many proxies each deck can include. Every organizer sets their own rules, so check before you show up. Many groups also expect proxies to be clearly marked as such and never sold or passed off as real cards.

**Casual kitchen-table Vintage.** Some playgroups simply agree to play Vintage with proxies among friends, the same way a Commander group might agree on house rules. Nothing about the restricted list requires a tournament.

If you're curious about Vintage but don't want to commit, a good path is to start with Legacy, which shares most of the card pool and much of the play style, or to build a Vintage-inspired deck in another non-rotating format. Many of the tactical lessons, such as respecting free counterspells and planning around fast starts, carry over directly.

## Tips for your first Vintage games

- **Respect the opponent's first turn.** Assume they can do something powerful with three or four mana on turn one, and keep a hand that can either race it or interact with it.
- **Count blue cards.** If you play Force of Will, you need other blue cards in hand to exile to it. A hand with Force of Will and no other blue cards can't use it for free.
- **Mulligan for function, not power.** A hand with three restricted cards that doesn't do anything coherent is still a bad hand. The usual mulligan principles apply here just as they do anywhere else.
- **Know the graveyard and artifact plans.** Even if you play blue control, learn what the Workshop and Bazaar decks are trying to do so you recognize their key turns.
- **Play slowly and precisely.** Vintage games often have fewer, bigger decisions. Take the time to get each one right.

## Takeaway

Vintage is Magic with almost every limit removed: one copy of each of the most powerful cards ever printed, a card pool spanning the whole history of the game, and games that can turn on a single opening play. The restricted list is the key idea, and the Power Nine are its most famous residents. You can try the format online or at proxy-friendly community events, and many of its habits, like respecting fast mana and free interaction, will make you a sharper player in Legacy and beyond.
