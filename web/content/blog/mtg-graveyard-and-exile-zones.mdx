In Magic: The Gathering, the graveyard is the discard pile where cards go when they're destroyed, sacrificed, discarded, countered, or finish resolving. Exile is a separate zone for cards that have been removed from the game, usually more permanently. Both zones are public by default, so anyone can look at the cards there. The big difference is how easy it is to get a card back: many cards interact with the graveyard, but far fewer can reach into exile.

This guide covers what goes to each zone, what you can see there, how decks use the graveyard, which exile effects return cards, and what happens to tokens and copies when they leave the battlefield. If you're new to the game, the [beginner's guide to playing Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) introduces the zones first.

## A quick map of the zones

A zone is a place a card can be during the game. The main ones are:

| Zone | What it is | Public? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Library | Your deck, face down | No |
| Hand | Cards you're holding | No, only you see them |
| Battlefield | Where permanents are in play | Yes |
| Stack | Where spells and abilities wait to resolve | Yes |
| Graveyard | Your discard pile | Yes |
| Exile | Cards removed from the game | Usually yes |
| Command zone | Special cards like your commander | Yes |

Each player has their own library, hand and graveyard. The battlefield, the stack and exile are shared.

## What goes to the graveyard

Cards end up in the graveyard in many ways:

- **A permanent is destroyed,** for example by Wrath of God or Murder.
- **A creature dies from damage,** or its toughness becomes 0 or less.
- **A permanent is sacrificed.**
- **A card is discarded** from your hand.
- **An instant or sorcery resolves.** After it does its job, the card goes to your graveyard.
- **A spell is countered.** Counterspell sends the countered card to its owner's graveyard.
- **A card is milled,** meaning it's put into the graveyard straight from the library.

"Dies" is the game's word for a creature being put into a graveyard from the battlefield. It covers destruction, lethal damage and sacrifice alike.

### Cards go to their owner's graveyard

A card always goes to its owner's graveyard, not its controller's. If you take control of your opponent's creature and it dies, it goes to their graveyard. The owner is the player who started the game with the card in their deck.

### The graveyard is public

Any player can look through any graveyard at any time. That's useful information: you can see what removal your opponent has already used, how many copies of a card are gone, and what they might try to bring back. Get in the habit of checking your opponent's graveyard before making a big play.

## The graveyard as a resource

To a new player, the graveyard looks like a place for used-up cards. To many decks, it's a second hand.

### Casting from the graveyard

Some cards can be cast from the graveyard. Flashback is the classic example: a card with flashback can be cast from your graveyard by paying its flashback cost, and then it's exiled instead of going back to the graveyard. Think Twice draws a card, then can be cast again from the graveyard for its flashback cost.

### Returning cards to hand

Raise Dead returns a creature card from your graveyard to your hand. Gravedigger does the same when it enters the battlefield. Effects like these turn a creature you lost into a fresh card.

### Reanimation

Reanimation puts a creature card from a graveyard directly onto the battlefield. Reanimate returns a creature from any graveyard under your control, and you lose life equal to its mana value. Animate Dead is an Aura that does a similar job. Reanimator decks put huge creatures into their graveyard early, by discarding or milling them, then bring them back far ahead of schedule.

### Counting the graveyard

Other cards care about how many cards are in your graveyard, or what types they are. Threshold and delirium are keyword mechanics built on this. Delve lets you exile cards from your graveyard to help pay for a spell. Sacrifice decks use the graveyard too, since every creature they sacrifice lands there; see the [aristocrats guide](/blog/aristocrats-sacrifice-deck-guide) for how they use it.

### Graveyard hate

Because the graveyard is so useful, there are cards built to shut it down. Tormod's Crypt can be tapped and sacrificed to exile a player's graveyard. Rest in Peace exiles all graveyards when it enters and then exiles any card or token that would be put into a graveyard instead. These cards are common in sideboards in formats with strong graveyard decks.

## What goes to exile

Exile is the zone for cards removed from the game. Cards go there because a spell or ability says "exile," never by default. Common sources include:

- **Removal that exiles,** like Swords to Plowshares, which exiles target creature and gives its controller life equal to its power, or Path to Exile.
- **Flashback and similar mechanics,** which exile the card after you cast it from the graveyard.
- **Graveyard hate,** which exiles cards from graveyards.
- **Mechanics that hold cards for later,** like foretell and suspend, which exile a card so it can be cast later.

### Why exile removal is strong

Exiling a creature is usually better than destroying it. A destroyed creature goes to the graveyard, where Gravedigger, Reanimate or flashback can use it again. An exiled creature is out of reach of almost all of that. Exile also gets around abilities that trigger when a creature dies, and it beats indestructible, since indestructible only stops "destroy."

### Is exile public?

Most exiled cards are face up, and anyone can look at them. Some effects exile cards face down. In that case, the card's identity is hidden, and only players the effect allows can look at it.

## Exile effects that return cards

Not all exile is permanent. Some effects exile a card only for a while.

### "Until" effects

Banisher Priest exiles target creature an opponent controls until Banisher Priest leaves the battlefield. As soon as the Priest leaves, the exiled card returns to the battlefield under its owner's control. This is strong removal, but it has a weak point: remove the Priest and you get your creature back.

Oblivion Ring does a similar job with two separate triggered abilities: when it enters, it exiles another target nonland permanent, and when it leaves, the exiled card returns. Because the abilities are separate, there's a classic trick. If Oblivion Ring leaves the battlefield while its enters ability is still on the stack, the leaves ability resolves first and returns nothing. Then the enters ability resolves and exiles the target with no way back. A one-sentence "until" ability like Banisher Priest's doesn't have this problem: if the Priest is gone before its ability resolves, nothing gets exiled.

### Flicker

Flicker effects exile a permanent and return it right away. Cloudshift exiles target creature you control, then returns that card to the battlefield under your control. People use flicker to reuse enters-the-battlefield abilities or to save a creature from removal.

A flickered permanent comes back as a new object. Counters, Auras and damage on it are gone, it's untapped, and it has no memory of its old self. A flickered creature also has summoning sickness, so it can't attack or use `{T}` abilities that turn unless it has haste.

### Casting from exile

Some mechanics let you cast cards from exile later, like foretell, suspend and the Adventure cards that go on an adventure in exile before you cast the permanent half. If a card in exile can be cast, its own text will say how.

## Tokens and copies cease to exist

Tokens aren't cards, and they follow a special rule when they leave the battlefield. A token that leaves the battlefield does go to the new zone briefly, whether that's the graveyard, exile or your hand. Then, the next time the game checks state-based actions, it ceases to exist.

That has a few consequences:

- **A dying token still "dies."** Abilities that trigger when a creature dies, like Blood Artist's, see tokens die. The token goes to the graveyard, triggers the ability, and then disappears.
- **A token can't come back.** Reanimation can't return it, because it no longer exists. A flickered token is exiled and then can't return, so flickering a token removes it for good.
- **Bouncing a token removes it.** A token returned to your hand ceases to exist.

Copies of spells work the same way: when a copy resolves or is countered, it simply ceases to exist. For decks that make lots of tokens, the [tokens guide](/blog/mtg-tokens-deck-guide) explains how to build around them.

## Commanders and these zones

In Commander, your commander is special. If it would go to the graveyard or exile, you can choose to move it to the command zone instead, and cast it again from there for an additional cost each time. That's why removal that exiles or destroys a commander only answers it temporarily.

## The takeaway

The graveyard collects everything that's been used, destroyed, discarded or countered, and for many decks it's a resource worth protecting. Exile takes cards out of reach, which makes exile removal stronger, unless the effect says "until." Both zones are public, so read them. And remember that tokens and copies cease to exist when they leave the battlefield, which is why you can't bring them back.
