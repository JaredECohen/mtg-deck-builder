Darksteel is the Magic: The Gathering expansion released in February 2004 as the second set of the Mirrodin block, set on a plane made entirely of metal. It introduced the indestructible keyword and modular, and it printed some of the most powerful artifacts in the game's history: Skullclamp, Arcbound Ravager, Aether Vial and Sword of Fire and Ice. It is also one of the most banned sets ever printed.

## The story

Mirrodin was built by Karn, the silver golem planeswalker from the Weatherlight Saga, as an artificial world he originally called Argentum. When he left, he put a guardian golem in charge and gave it the Mirari, a powerful artifact. The Mirari's influence awakened the guardian into a new being called Memnarch, who renamed the world, populated it by kidnapping creatures from other planes, and slowly went mad. Memnarch became obsessed with leaving Mirrodin to find his creator, and the only way he could imagine doing it was to steal a planeswalker spark.

The block's hero is Glissa Sunseeker, an elf of the Tangle whose parents were killed in an attack by Memnarch's levelers. In the first set, Mirrodin, she teams up with Slobad, a goblin tinkerer, and Bosh, an ancient iron golem trying to recover his memories. The Darksteel storyline comes from Jess Lebow's novel *The Darksteel Eye*.

Before dying, the troll elder Chunth tells Glissa a secret: the world is hollow. To prove it, she fights her way into Lumengrid, the capital of the vedalken, and into a sacred pool in its depths, which hides a tunnel to Mirrodin's interior. Inside, towers of a strange metal-eating growth called mycosynth reach toward a sun of pure mana at the center of the world. There she learns that Memnarch is real, and that he has been hunting her because she carries a latent planeswalker spark.

Glissa gathers the legendary Kaldra artifacts, the sword, shield and helm whose cards appear across the block, to form a champion that can stand against Memnarch. But Memnarch seizes control of the Kaldra avatar and turns it on her, and Bosh fights it to save her. Memnarch's plans unravel when the mana core destabilizes and a green sun erupts from the world's center. That fifth sun gives the block's final set, Fifth Dawn, its name.

## Mechanics

### Indestructible

Darksteel made indestructible a keyword. An indestructible permanent can't be destroyed, either by effects that say "destroy" or by lethal damage. It can still be exiled, sacrificed, bounced or shrunk to zero toughness. Darksteel Citadel is an indestructible artifact land, Darksteel Ingot is an indestructible mana rock, and Darksteel Colossus is an 11-mana 11/11 trampler that also can't be destroyed. Indestructible later spread far beyond artifacts and is now an evergreen keyword.

### Modular

A modular artifact creature enters with a number of +1/+1 counters. When it dies, you can move those counters onto another artifact creature. Arcbound Worker is a one-mana 0/0 with one counter. Arcbound Ravager is the payoff: it lets you sacrifice artifacts to grow, and when it dies its counters move to another creature, so killing it rarely ends the threat.

### Affinity and entwine

Affinity for artifacts returned from Mirrodin. A spell with affinity costs `{1}` less for each artifact you control, as on Chromescale Drake and Furnace Dragon. Darksteel also introduced affinity for basic land types on a cycle of Golems like Tangle Golem. Entwine, from Mirrodin, lets you pay extra to get both modes of a spell, as with Barbed Lightning.

### The Pulses and the Echoing cycle

Each color got a Pulse: a spell that returns to your hand if your opponent is ahead in some resource. Pulse of the Forge deals 4 damage and comes back if the opponent has more life than you. A common cycle of Echoing spells, like Echoing Truth, hit every permanent with the same name as the target.

## Limited

The Mirrodin block was drafted with Darksteel as the third pack behind two packs of Mirrodin, and later in a mixed format with Fifth Dawn. Darksteel is a small set, so its job in draft was to add depth to the artifact themes already established. Affinity for artifacts rewarded you for drafting cheap artifacts like Myr Moonvessel and Darksteel Pendant, while modular creatures like Arcbound Stinger made artifact creature decks resilient. Equipment such as Vulshok Morningstar was premium, and colored removal like Barbed Lightning, Echoing Decay and Murderous Spoils was valuable.

Because the format was heavy on artifacts, artifact removal like Oxidize and Echoing Ruin was more playable in the main deck than usual. We don't have reliable archetype-by-archetype data from that era, so it's best described as an artifact-centric format where colorless cards let you stay flexible. For a primer on how to judge cards in any draft, see our guide to [evaluating cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited).

## The meta: how it played in Constructed

### Standard

Darksteel's arrival in Standard was explosive. Skullclamp made every small creature a source of card advantage, and decks of 1/1 creatures could draw a huge number of cards. Wizards banned it in Standard and Mirrodin Block Constructed on June 1, 2004, the first card banned in Standard in about five years.

The bigger problem was Affinity. Combining Mirrodin's artifact lands and Frogmites with Darksteel's Arcbound Ravager, Darksteel Citadel and Blinkmoth Nexus, plus Fifth Dawn's Cranial Plating later, Affinity decks could deploy a board almost for free and drain opponents out with Mirrodin's Disciple of the Vault. It dominated tournaments through 2004. In March 2005, Wizards banned eight cards in Standard to end its reign: Arcbound Ravager, Disciple of the Vault, Darksteel Citadel and the five other artifact lands (Ancient Den, Seat of the Synod, Vault of Whispers, Great Furnace and Tree of Tales). Darksteel itself rotated out of Standard in October 2006.

### Eternal formats

As of October 2026, Scryfall's legality data shows:

- **Skullclamp** is banned in Modern and Legacy, legal in Vintage, and a staple in Commander.
- **Mycosynth Lattice** was banned in Modern in January 2020 because of its lock with Karn, the Great Creator.
- **Trinisphere** has been restricted in Vintage since 2005.
- **Arcbound Ravager** has powered Modern Affinity and Hardened Scales decks, and is legal in Modern, Legacy and Vintage.
- **Aether Vial** is a key card in Legacy Death and Taxes and in creature decks in Modern.
- **Sword of Fire and Ice** has been one of the strongest Equipment cards in Modern and Legacy for years.
- **Darksteel Citadel** and **Arcbound Worker** are legal in Pauper and appear in its artifact aggro decks.

In Commander, Skullclamp, Darksteel Forge, Mycosynth Lattice and the Swords are popular, and Memnarch is a memorable artifact commander. For more on why cards get banned, read our [ban list guide](/blog/mtg-ban-list-explained), and for the formats themselves see [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).

**Sundering Titan**, the eight-mana Golem that destroys a land of each basic type when it enters or leaves the battlefield, is banned in Modern, where it was too easy to cheat into play early.

## Cards from Darksteel worth owning

- **Sword of Fire and Ice**: a powerful Equipment in Modern, Legacy and Commander.
- **Aether Vial**: the engine of Legacy Death and Taxes and other creature decks.
- **Arcbound Ravager**: the centerpiece of artifact aggro decks in Modern.
- **Skullclamp**: banned in many formats but one of Commander's best card-draw artifacts.
- **Darksteel Citadel**: an indestructible artifact land for Modern and Pauper artifact decks.
- **Darksteel Forge**: makes an artifact-heavy Commander board nearly impossible to destroy.
- **Trinisphere**: a lock piece in Vintage and some Modern prison decks.

Darksteel shows how quickly a strong theme becomes too strong, and its artifacts still define how Magic handles them today.
