Commander, also called EDH, is a Magic: The Gathering format where each player builds a 100-card deck around a single legendary creature, the commander, using only one copy of each card. Games are usually multiplayer, everyone starts at 40 life, and your commander can be cast again and again from a special zone. It is the most popular casual way to play Magic, and this guide covers everything you need to sit down at a Commander table.

## Where Commander came from

Commander began as a player-created format called **Elder Dragon Highlander**, which is where the initials EDH come from. The original idea was to build a deck led by one of the Elder Dragons, the five legendary dragons from the Legends set, and "Highlander" is a nod to the "there can be only one" rule that every card appears only once.

The format spread from playgroup to playgroup for years before it was widely played in stores. As it grew, the name Commander became standard and the rules expanded to allow any legendary creature as a commander. Wizards of the Coast now prints products designed specifically for it, including preconstructed Commander decks that are ready to play out of the box. Many players still say "EDH" and "Commander" interchangeably.

## The core rules of Commander

The rules below are the ones that make Commander different from other formats. Everything else uses normal Magic rules. If you need a refresher on those, start with [how to play Magic: The Gathering](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering).

### 100 cards, one of each

Your deck contains **exactly 100 cards, including your commander**. That usually means 99 cards in the deck plus the commander.

Commander is a **singleton** format: you may include only one copy of any card, with the exception of basic lands (Plains, Island, Swamp, Mountain, Forest and Wastes), which you can play in any number. So you can run one Sol Ring, one Counterspell and one Wrath of God, but never two of any of them.

Singleton is a big part of why Commander games feel fresh. With 99 different cards, you rarely draw the same opening twice.

### Choosing a commander

Your commander is normally a **legendary creature**. Some other cards say in their text that they can be your commander, and those work too. A few commanders also let you pair two of them together; their own rules text explains how.

Pick a commander you want to see over and over, because you will. Its abilities usually set the theme for the whole deck.

### Color identity

Every card in your deck must fit your commander's **color identity**. A card's color identity is made up of every colored mana symbol that appears in its mana cost **and** in its rules text. Reminder text, the italic explanation in parentheses, doesn't count.

That second part trips people up. A commander that costs only green mana but has an ability with a blue mana symbol in its text has a green and blue color identity. Your deck may then use green, blue and colorless cards, but nothing red, white or black.

The same test applies to every card in the 99. A colorless artifact like Sol Ring fits in any deck. A land that produces two colors only fits if both are in your commander's identity.

### The command zone

Your commander doesn't start in your deck. It begins the game in the **command zone**, a separate area of the table, and you can cast it from there whenever you could normally cast it.

If your commander would go to your graveyard or into exile, you may move it back to the command zone instead. You can also choose to return it there if it would be put into your hand or library. This is what lets you keep coming back to your commander, even after it has been removed.

### Commander tax

Each time you cast your commander from the command zone, it costs **two more generic mana** for each previous time you have cast it from there this game. The first cast is at its normal cost, the second costs two more, the third costs four more, and so on. This extra cost is called the **commander tax**.

The tax keeps a commander from being impossible to deal with. Removing it is still worth doing, because every recast takes more of its owner's mana.

### 40 starting life and commander damage

Each player starts at **40 life**, double the usual 20. Multiplayer games run longer, and the extra life gives everyone time to set up.

Commander also has a second way to lose: if a player has been dealt **21 or more combat damage by a single commander** over the course of the game, that player loses. Damage from two different commanders doesn't add together, and damage that isn't combat damage doesn't count toward the 21. This rule makes big, aggressive commanders a real threat even at 40 life.

### Multiplayer and the free mulligan

Commander is usually played with **four players**, though three or five also work. Most games are free-for-all: everyone plays for themselves, and the last player standing wins.

Commander uses the London mulligan, the same as other formats: if you don't like your opening hand, you shuffle it away and draw seven new cards, then put one card on the bottom of your library for each time you have mulliganed. In multiplayer games, **your first mulligan is free**, meaning you don't put a card on the bottom for it. For more on when to keep and when to send a hand back, see our [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide).

## The social contract and the Rule 0 conversation

Commander is a casual format by design, and the community treats it that way. Before a game, players often have a quick chat about what kind of game they want. This is commonly called the **Rule 0 conversation**, after the idea that a playgroup can agree to adjust things before it starts.

A good Rule 0 conversation covers a few questions:

- **How powerful are our decks?** A brand-new preconstructed deck and a tuned, tournament-style deck shouldn't usually face each other.
- **Is anything off the table?** Some groups dislike destroying everyone's lands, very long turns, or winning out of nowhere with a two-card combo.
- **Are we okay with proxies or house rules?** Some groups allow stand-in cards for expensive ones; others don't.

The goal is simple: everyone at the table should have a fun game. Nobody is wrong for liking high-power or low-power Commander, but the experience falls apart when one player's deck is tuned for a completely different game than everyone else's.

## The Commander bracket system

To make that conversation easier, Wizards of the Coast introduced an official **Commander bracket system** in 2025. It sorts decks into five brackets, from 1 (Exhibition) to 5 (cEDH, short for competitive EDH), based on how powerful and optimized they are. It also uses a list of especially strong cards, called **Game Changers**, along with a few kinds of play such as mass land destruction and extra-turn chains, as signals for which bracket a deck belongs in.

Brackets don't replace talking to your table, but they give everyone a shared vocabulary. Saying "my deck is a bracket 2" tells the table a lot in a few words. For the full breakdown of what each bracket means, read [Commander brackets explained](/blog/commander-brackets-explained).

## Why people love Commander

Commander has grown into one of Magic's most played formats for a handful of reasons.

**Self-expression.** Your commander is your deck's identity, and the singleton rule pushes you to find interesting cards instead of playing four copies of the same best ones. Two decks built around the same commander can look nothing alike.

**Big, memorable moments.** With 40 life and several opponents, games last long enough for huge spells and dramatic turnarounds. A board wipe like Wrath of God can reset a table that looked lost.

**Social play.** Multiplayer games include negotiation, temporary alliances and table talk. Choosing whom to attack, and convincing others not to attack you, is a skill of its own.

**Old cards get a home.** Because Commander allows cards from across Magic's history, a card that never saw play elsewhere can shine in the right deck. Your collection stays useful.

**Easy entry.** Preconstructed Commander decks let new players start with a complete, playable deck, then swap cards in and out over time.
