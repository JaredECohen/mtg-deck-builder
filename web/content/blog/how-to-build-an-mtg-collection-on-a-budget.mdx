The cheapest way to build a Magic: The Gathering collection is to decide what you want to play first, then buy the specific single cards you need instead of opening packs. Add cards that work in many decks before cards that only work in one, trade fairly for the rest, and use digital Magic to test ideas before you spend on paper. A collection built that way grows into decks you actually play instead of a box of random cards.

This guide is about building a collection over time. If you want to build one strong, cheap deck right now, the [budget deck guide](/blog/budget-mtg-decks-under-50) covers role-based swaps, strong commons and cheap mana bases in detail.

## Start with a goal, not a pile

The biggest budget mistake is buying cards without a plan. A pile of random rares feels like progress, but it rarely turns into a playable deck. Before you spend anything, decide:

- **Which format you'll play.** Commander, Pauper, Standard and Modern all need different cards. The [formats guide](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) compares them.
- **Where you'll play.** Your local playgroup's format and power level matter more than what's popular online.
- **What kind of deck you enjoy.** Aggressive creatures, control, combos, big spells. Picking a style narrows down which cards are worth owning.

With a goal, every purchase moves you toward a finished deck. Without one, you'll spend more and end up with less.

### Pick formats that reward a small collection

Some formats are friendlier to a growing collection than others:

- **Commander** uses one copy of each card, so you need fewer copies of expensive cards than in four-copy formats. Many cards also fit several Commander decks.
- **Pauper** only allows cards printed at common, which keeps decks affordable by design. The [Pauper guide](/blog/what-is-pauper-mtg) explains how it works.
- **Non-rotating formats** like Commander, Pauper and Modern let you keep using cards for years. In a rotating format like Standard, sets leave the format over time, so cards bought only for Standard lose their place in your decks sooner.

## Singles vs packs

Opening packs is fun, and that's a good reason to buy them. But if your goal is a specific deck, buying singles is almost always more efficient.

### Why singles usually win

A pack gives you a random handful of cards, most of which won't go in your deck. Buying the exact cards you need avoids paying for the ones you don't. Over a whole deck, the difference adds up quickly.

Singles also let you choose the version of a card you want. Many cards have been reprinted several times, and the plainest printing is usually the cheapest. A card works the same in your deck regardless of which set it came from, as long as it's legal in your format.

### When packs make sense

- **Limited events.** Draft and sealed are built around opening packs, and you keep what you open.
- **For the experience.** If you enjoy cracking packs, budget for it as entertainment, not as a way to build a deck.
- **Starting from nothing.** A few packs or a starter product can give you a base of lands and commons to learn with.

### Preconstructed decks

Preconstructed decks, especially Commander precons, are one of the best ways to start a collection. You get a complete, playable deck with a commander, lands and a set of cards that work together, plus cards you can move into other decks later. Upgrading a precon a few cards at a time is a classic budget path.

## Staples that span decks and formats

A staple is a card that shows up in a lot of decks. Staples are good early buys because they keep their usefulness as your collection grows.

### Commander staples

Some cards appear in a huge share of Commander decks:

- **Sol Ring**, **Arcane Signet** and **Command Tower** fit almost any Commander deck of any colors.
- **Swords to Plowshares** fits nearly any deck with white.
- **Beast Within** and **Cultivate** fit nearly any deck with green.
- **Counterspell** fits nearly any deck with blue.

Because Commander allows only one copy per deck, a single copy of each of these can serve several decks, as long as you're willing to move cards between them or don't play them all at once.

### Colorless and mono-color first

When choosing what to buy, colorless cards are the most flexible, since they fit any deck. After that, cards in one color fit any deck with that color. Multicolor cards fit fewer decks, so buy them when you're committed to a specific deck.

### Lands

Lands are easy to overlook, but good mana makes every deck better. Lands that produce two of your colors are useful in any deck with that color pair, in many formats. Basic lands are the easiest cards in the game to find, so don't spend much on them.

### Cards legal in many formats

Some long-standing cards are legal in several non-rotating formats. **Lightning Bolt**, for example, has seen play in Modern, Legacy, Pauper and Commander. Format legality and ban lists change, so check the official legality of a card for your format before you buy it for that reason.

## Trading etiquette

Trading is a great way to turn cards you don't use into cards you will. Good trading habits keep it fun and keep people willing to trade with you.

- **Ask before you touch.** Someone's binder is their property. Ask before flipping through it, and handle cards carefully by the edges.
- **Agree on a shared value source.** Before trading, agree on how you'll value cards, such as a price guide you both trust, so there's no argument about what's fair.
- **Be honest about condition.** Point out any wear, bends or damage on cards you're offering.
- **Don't take advantage of newer players.** Trading a newer player out of a valuable card for something worth much less damages your reputation and the community. If someone doesn't know a card's value, tell them.
- **Accept "no" gracefully.** People aren't obligated to trade. Don't pressure.
- **Keep it simple.** Small, even trades are easier than complicated multi-card deals.
- **Trade for what you'll play.** Trading for speculative cards you won't use often leaves you with less of what you need.

Trading at your local game store's events or with your playgroup is usually the most comfortable. Everyone knows each other, and that keeps trades fair.

## Digital options

Digital Magic is a good way to explore decks and formats before buying paper cards.

- **MTG Arena** is free to play. You earn cards and in-game currency by playing, and you can build decks without spending money if you're patient.
- **Magic: The Gathering Online (MTGO)** has its own digital collection and economy. It has been around for a long time and supports many paper formats, including Pauper and older non-rotating formats.
- **Free deck builders and testing tools**, including the builder at [Deck Lab](/), let you put a list together and think through it before you buy anything.

Digital and paper collections are separate. Cards you own digitally don't transfer to paper, and vice versa. Use digital to learn what you like, then buy paper cards for the decks you're sure about.

### Playtest before you buy

Before buying a full deck, test the list. Proxies, which are stand-in cards for casual testing, are common in many playgroups for trying a deck before committing to it, though not every group or event allows them. Ask your group first. Testing first stops you from buying a deck you end up not enjoying.

## Habits that keep a collection affordable

1. **Make a want list.** Write down exactly what you need for your next deck and stick to it.
2. **Buy one deck at a time.** Finish a deck before starting the next.
3. **Reuse cards.** Move staples between decks instead of buying extra copies.
4. **Be patient.** Reprints and new printings often make cards easier to find over time. There's rarely a need to buy everything on day one.
5. **Sell or trade what you don't use.** Cards sitting in a box could become cards you play.
6. **Protect your cards.** Sleeves and a basic storage box keep your cards in good condition, which helps if you ever trade them.

## The takeaway

Decide what you want to play, then buy singles for that deck rather than opening packs. Start with staples and colorless cards that fit many decks, pick up a precon if you play Commander, and trade honestly for the rest. Use digital Magic to test before you buy, and finish one deck before starting another.
