The Commander brackets are an official Magic: The Gathering (MTG) system from Wizards of the Coast, introduced in 2025, that sorts Commander decks into five power levels: 1 Exhibition, 2 Core, 3 Upgraded, 4 Optimized and 5 cEDH. Each bracket describes what kind of game a deck is built for and which especially strong cards and strategies it includes. Their purpose is to help players find opponents with similar expectations before a game starts.

If you are new to the format itself, start with [what is Commander (EDH)](/blog/what-is-commander-edh). This guide assumes you know the basic rules.

## Why Commander needed brackets

Commander has no single power level. One player at the table might bring an untouched preconstructed deck, while another brings a list tuned to win as early as possible. Before brackets, players described their decks with a vague "it's about a 7 out of 10", and everyone's 7 meant something different.

The bracket system replaces that guesswork with shared terms. It combines two things:

1. **Intent.** What kind of game is the deck trying to have? A themed, laid-back game or a competitive one?
2. **Concrete signals.** Does the deck contain cards and patterns that reliably push games toward high power, such as Game Changers, mass land denial, chains of extra turns or two-card infinite combos?

Neither part works alone. A deck with no flagged cards can still be built to crush a casual table, and a deck with one powerful card can still be relaxed. That is why brackets are best understood as a starting point for a conversation.

## The signals: what moves a deck between brackets

Before going bracket by bracket, it helps to understand the four signals the system uses.

### Game Changers

**Game Changers** are a named list of individual cards that Wizards considers strong enough to shift a game on their own, whether by generating huge advantage, finding exactly the right card, or shutting other players out. Examples from the list include:

- Rhystic Study
- Cyclonic Rift
- Demonic Tutor
- Smothering Tithe
- The One Ring

The official list is longer and is updated over time, with cards added and removed as Wizards reviews the format, so check the current version on Wizards' site rather than relying on any copy you find elsewhere. Notably, the Game Changers list is separate from the Commander banned list: Game Changers are legal, they just indicate a higher bracket.

### Mass land denial

**Mass land denial** means cards that destroy, lock down or otherwise deny lands to many players at once. Armageddon, which destroys all lands, is the classic example, and cards like Winter Orb or Blood Moon, which stop lands from untapping or from producing their normal colors, fall in the same category. These effects can leave a whole table unable to play for many turns, which is why they are kept out of the lower brackets.

### Extra-turn chains

Taking a single extra turn is a normal part of Magic. Taking extra turn after extra turn, often by recasting or copying the same effect, means the other players at the table don't get to play. The bracket system treats **chaining** extra turns as a high-power pattern.

### Two-card infinite combos

A **two-card infinite combo** is a pair of cards that, together, produce an unbounded loop and usually win the game on the spot. Thassa's Oracle with Demonic Consultation is a well-known example, as is Exquisite Blood with Sanguine Bond. Because just two cards can end the game, often with little warning, the lower brackets avoid them and the middle bracket only allows ones that come together late in the game.

## The five brackets, one by one

### Bracket 1: Exhibition

**Intent:** the theme comes first, and winning is secondary. Bracket 1 decks are built to show off an idea: a deck where every card features a particular character, a deck that only uses cards from one old set, or a joke deck built around a silly win condition.

**Typical deck:** heavily themed, often with cards that are deliberately weaker than the best options because they fit the concept.

**What's expected:** no Game Changers, no mass land denial, no extra-turn chains, no two-card infinite combos, and very few tutors (cards that search your library for a specific card). Expect slow, interactive games where everyone gets to show off their deck.

### Bracket 2: Core

**Intent:** a fair, casual game where every player has a real chance. This is roughly the level of a typical preconstructed Commander deck.

**Typical deck:** a coherent strategy with reasonable card quality, but without the most efficient or game-warping cards. Games tend to develop over several turns, and players expect to interact with each other's boards.

**What's expected:** the same restrictions as bracket 1: no Game Changers, no mass land denial, no extra-turn chains, no two-card infinite combos, and tutors kept sparse. The difference from bracket 1 is intent: a bracket 2 deck is trying to win a fair game, not just tell a story.

### Bracket 3: Upgraded

**Intent:** a stronger deck that has been tuned past a preconstructed level, but still aims for a game that isn't decided in the first few turns.

**Typical deck:** more synergy, better mana, stronger removal and card draw, and a clearer plan to close out the game.

**What's expected:** **up to three Game Changers.** Still no mass land denial and no chained extra turns. Two-card combos are only acceptable if they come together late in the game, not as an early win. Players at this bracket should expect stronger interaction and faster games than bracket 2.

### Bracket 4: Optimized

**Intent:** high power. These decks use the strongest cards available to them and aim to win efficiently.

**Typical deck:** a focused, streamlined list that can include any number of Game Changers, fast mana, plenty of tutors and powerful combos.

**What's expected:** no restrictions beyond the format's banned list. Mass land denial, extra-turn chains and two-card infinite combos are all fair game. Everyone at a bracket 4 table should be prepared for explosive turns and early threats.

### Bracket 5: cEDH

**Intent:** competitive play. cEDH, short for competitive EDH, means building and playing to win at the highest level, often with tournament-style expectations.

**Typical deck:** a bracket 4 card pool, played with a tournament mindset. Lists are tuned for speed and consistency, and players closely follow what other competitive decks are doing.

**What's expected:** the card pool is the same as bracket 4. The difference is in mindset: the whole table agrees that the goal is to win as efficiently as possible, and nobody expects to hold back.

| 1 | Exhibition | Theme first | None | No | No | No |
| 2 | Core | Fair, casual games | None | No | No | No |
| 3 | Upgraded | Tuned, but not early wins | Up to 3 | No | No | Late game only |

## Brackets are a conversation tool, not a rulebook

The most important thing to understand about brackets is what they aren't. They don't change the rules of the game, and they aren't a legality check. A deck that technically meets bracket 2 limits can still be built to steamroll a casual table, and a bracket 4 card in a relaxed deck doesn't make that deck a threat.

Use brackets as the start of your pre-game conversation (often called the Rule 0 conversation), then fill in the details:

- "My deck is a bracket 3 with two Game Changers."
- "It has a late-game combo, but it usually wins through combat."
- "It's a bracket 2 deck, but it plays a lot of removal."

This also explains why brackets 1 and 5 are hard to judge from a decklist alone. Bracket 1 depends on whether the deck is truly built around a theme, and bracket 5 depends on the table's competitive intent, not on cards a bracket 4 deck couldn't also play. The cards in a deck can tell you a lot about brackets 2 through 4, but intent is something only the players can describe.

When in doubt, aim for a deck that sits comfortably in the middle of a bracket, and be honest if it is pushing against the top.

## How Deck Lab uses brackets

Deck Lab reads the bracket signals in any Commander list it builds. It flags Game Changers, mass land denial, extra-turn cards, tutors and known two-card combos, then estimates the deck's bracket. Because brackets 1 and 5 depend on intent, the estimate is a floor between 2 and 4, with notes explaining what moved it there.

You can also ask the [builder](/) to aim for a target bracket. At brackets 1 through 3 it won't add mass land denial or extra-turn cards on its own. At brackets 1 and 2 it also leaves out Game Changers and breaks up two-card combos. At bracket 3 it allows up to three Game Changers, and only ones you specifically ask for. For the rest of the deck-building process, see [how to build your first Commander deck](/blog/how-to-build-your-first-commander-deck).

Know your bracket, say it out loud before the game, and adjust when the table tells you a deck feels stronger or weaker than its label.
