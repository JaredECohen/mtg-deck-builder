In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), a spellslinger deck is built around casting lots of instants and sorceries and getting rewarded for each one. Instead of relying on a few big creatures, it uses cheap spells that do two jobs: each spell has its own effect, and it also triggers payoff cards that make tokens, deal damage, grow creatures or draw more cards. The deck works when you have enough cheap spells to keep those payoffs firing every turn.

This guide covers the main kinds of payoffs, how prowess and copy effects work, why card flow is the engine of the deck, and how to balance payoffs against the spells that feed them. The archetype lives mostly in blue and red, so it overlaps with [burn](/blog/mtg-burn-deck-guide) and [tempo](/blog/mtg-tempo-decks-explained), but its plan is its own.

## What makes a deck "spellslinger"

Plenty of decks play instants and sorceries. A spellslinger deck is different because the spells are also the fuel for its threats. Three features define it:

- **A high count of instants and sorceries,** usually well above what other decks run, and mostly cheap ones.
- **Payoff cards** that trigger or scale when you cast those spells.
- **Card flow,** meaning cheap draw and selection spells that keep the deck moving so you never run out of things to cast.

The appeal is that every card does double duty. A removal spell kills a creature and also makes a token. A cantrip draws you a card and also pumps your attacker. Your opponent has to respect both halves.

## Instant and sorcery payoffs

Payoffs are the reason to build the deck. They come in a few flavors.

### Token makers

Young Pyromancer creates a 1/1 red Elemental creature token whenever you cast an instant or sorcery spell. Talrand, Sky Summoner does the same with 2/2 flying Drakes. These cards turn a stream of small spells into a board, which lets a spell-heavy deck pressure the opponent without running many creatures.

### Damage and drain

Guttersnipe deals 2 damage to each opponent whenever you cast an instant or sorcery spell. Payoffs like this let you win by casting spells alone, which makes them great finishers but weak on an empty hand. They're especially strong in multiplayer, where "each opponent" can mean three players.

### Cost reducers and value engines

Some cards make your instants and sorceries cheaper, which lets you cast two or three spells in a turn. Others reward you for casting spells with extra cards or bigger effects. These are less flashy than token makers but often make the deck feel smooth.

## Prowess

Prowess is a keyword built for this archetype. It's a triggered ability: whenever you cast a noncreature spell, the creature with prowess gets +1/+1 until end of turn. Each spell triggers it separately, so casting three spells gives +3/+3.

Monastery Swiftspear is the classic prowess creature. It costs one red mana, has haste and prowess, and attacks the turn it comes down. On its own it's a 1/2. With two cheap spells cast during combat, it hits like a 3/4.

A few details that matter:

- **Prowess counts any noncreature spell,** not just instants and sorceries. Artifacts and enchantments trigger it too.
- **The bonus lasts until end of turn,** so cast your spells on the turn you attack, ideally after blockers are declared, when the opponent has already committed.
- **It's a trigger, so it uses the stack.** If your opponent kills the creature in response, the pump never helps.

Prowess decks lean aggressive. They want cheap creatures, cheap burn and cantrips, and they use spells as both reach and pump. If that sounds like a burn deck with more creatures, it largely is, and the line between the two is blurry.

## Copying spells

Copy effects let you get two or more uses out of one spell. Twincast and Reverberate each copy target instant or sorcery spell, and you may choose new targets for the copy. Copy a Lightning Bolt and you get two Bolts. Copy a big draw spell and you draw twice as much.

### Copies aren't cast

This is the single most important rule for copy effects. A copy of a spell is created on the stack, not cast. That means:

- **"Whenever you cast" payoffs don't see copies.** Young Pyromancer, Guttersnipe and prowess trigger for the copy spell itself, like Twincast, but not for the copy it creates.
- **Some cards care about copies too.** Magecraft is a keyword that triggers whenever you cast or copy an instant or sorcery spell, so it rewards copy effects directly.
- **Copies aren't cards.** When a copy resolves or is countered, it simply ceases to exist. It doesn't go to the graveyard.

Copy effects can target any instant or sorcery spell on the stack, including your opponent's, so a well-timed Twincast can turn their removal spell or draw spell into one of your own. Since copying works by putting new objects on the stack, it helps to know [how the stack works](/blog/how-the-stack-works-mtg) before you build around it.

### Storm

Storm is copying taken to the extreme. A spell with storm gets copied once for each spell cast before it that turn. A deck that casts eight cheap spells and then Grapeshot deals damage for each of them. Storm decks are really combo decks with a spellslinger engine, and they're built to win in a single turn rather than over several.

## Card flow is the engine

A spellslinger deck burns through cards fast. If you cast two spells a turn and draw one, your hand empties quickly and your payoffs go quiet. Card flow fixes that.

The best card flow spells are cheap and replace themselves:

- **Opt** scries 1, then draws a card, for one blue mana.
- **Preordain** and **Ponder** let you look at the top cards and set up your next draws.
- **Brainstorm** draws three, then puts two back, which is strong when you can shuffle afterward.

These cards don't add to your hand size, but they don't shrink it either, and each one triggers your payoffs. A cantrip that costs one mana and makes a 1/1 token with Young Pyromancer on the battlefield is pure value. They also dig you toward your payoffs and your best spells, which smooths out the deck's draws.

For bigger refills, spellslingers use draw spells that put you ahead on cards, and recursion that lets you cast spells again from the graveyard. Flashback spells and Snapcaster Mage, which gives an instant or sorcery in your graveyard flashback until end of turn, effectively turn one card into two casts. ## Balancing payoffs and spells

The hardest part of building a spellslinger deck is the ratio. Too many payoffs and you draw a hand of creatures with nothing to trigger them. Too few and you're just a pile of spells with no threat.

### Spells come first

Think of payoffs as the engine and spells as the fuel. The engine doesn't run without fuel, so most of the nonland slots should be spells. As a rough rule of thumb, a 60-card spellslinger deck with prowess or "whenever you cast" payoffs usually wants well over half of its nonland cards to be instants and sorceries. Treat that as a starting point and test from there.

### Make every spell useful on its own

Each spell should be something you'd be happy to cast without a payoff. Removal, card draw, counterspells and burn all qualify. Pure filler that only exists to trigger payoffs makes the deck fold when the payoffs get removed, which they will.

### Keep the curve low

Payoffs reward the number of spells, not their size. Two one-mana spells trigger Young Pyromancer twice, while one two-mana spell triggers it once. A low curve lets you cast multiple spells per turn and keeps your mana efficient.

### Diversify your payoffs

Removal-heavy opponents will kill your first payoff. Running payoffs of different kinds, like a token maker, a prowess creature and a payoff that isn't a creature at all, makes you harder to shut down. Payoffs that leave something behind, like tokens, also keep working after they die.

### Instants beat sorceries

When the effect is similar, prefer the instant. Instants let you hold mana up on the opponent's turn, react to their plays, and trigger payoffs at the moment that matters most, such as pumping a prowess creature during combat.

## Playing a spellslinger deck

- **Sequence payoffs first.** Cast Young Pyromancer before your spells, not after, so every spell that follows triggers it. The one exception is when you need to clear a blocker or a threat right away.
- **Count your spells per turn.** The deck's power is spikiest on turns when you chain several spells. Sometimes it's right to pass with mana open and then cast two spells on the opponent's end step.
- **Watch for hate cards.** Opponents may have effects that punish you for casting multiple spells, or creatures that tax noncreature spells. Plan around them by sequencing carefully.
- **Use the graveyard.** Flashback and recursion turn your used spells into a second hand. Protect that resource when you can.

## Spellslinger in Commander

The archetype is a popular Commander theme. A commander like Talrand, Sky Summoner gives you a payoff that's always available, and the format's 100-card singleton rules mean you'll run a wide mix of cantrips, removal and copy effects. Multiplayer games reward payoffs that hit each opponent and copy effects that let you use other players' spells against them.

## The takeaway

A spellslinger deck wins by making every cheap spell count twice. Pick a few strong payoffs, fill the rest with cheap, useful instants and sorceries, keep card flow high so you never run dry, and remember that copies aren't cast. Get the ratio right and your opponent will be facing a board that grows with every spell you cast.
