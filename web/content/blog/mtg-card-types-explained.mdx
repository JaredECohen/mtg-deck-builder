Magic has nine main card types you will meet in normal play: land, creature, instant, sorcery, artifact, enchantment, planeswalker, battle and kindred. A card's type tells you when you can play it, whether it stays on the battlefield after it resolves, and which rules apply to it. Supertypes (like legendary and basic) add extra rules on top, and subtypes (like Elf, Equipment or Aura) sort cards into families that other cards care about.

All of this lives on the type line, the strip across the middle of the card. If you are still learning where each part of a card sits, start with [how to read a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card). This guide goes one level deeper: what each type actually does in a game and how decks use it.

## The type line in one sentence

A type line reads left to right as supertypes, then card types, then a long dash, then subtypes. "Legendary Creature — Elf Warrior" is a card with the legendary supertype, the creature card type, and two creature subtypes, Elf and Warrior.

Not every card has all three parts. Lightning Bolt just says "Instant."

## Permanents and non-permanents

The biggest split in the game: six card types are **permanents**: land, creature, artifact, enchantment, planeswalker and battle. When a permanent spell resolves, the card goes onto the battlefield and stays there until something removes it.

Two types are not permanents: **instant** and **sorcery**. When one resolves, it does what its text says and then goes to its owner's graveyard. Those are one-shot effects.

Kindred is the odd one out. It never appears alone; it always sits next to another card type, so a kindred card is a permanent or not depending on what it is paired with.

## Land

Lands are your mana sources, and most decks are roughly 35 to 40 percent lands. You don't cast a land. Instead, you play it as a special action during one of your main phases, when the stack is empty, and you can normally do that only once per turn. Because playing a land doesn't use the stack, your opponent can't respond to it.

The basic lands are Plains, Island, Swamp, Mountain and Forest, plus Wastes for colorless mana. Each basic land type has a built-in ability to tap for one mana of its color. Nonbasic lands can do more, like making two colors or having an extra ability.

## Creature

Creatures are permanents that can attack and block. Each has power (combat damage it deals) and toughness (damage it can take before it dies).

Llanowar Elves is a classic example: it costs `{G}`, it's a 1/1 "Creature — Elf Druid," and it can tap to add one green mana. That shows how creatures do more than fight. Many have abilities that ramp your mana, draw cards or remove other creatures.

Creatures have one rule worth learning early: a creature can't attack, and can't use abilities with the tap symbol in their cost, unless you have controlled it continuously since the start of your most recent turn. Players call this summoning sickness. It's why Llanowar Elves played on turn one can't tap for mana until turn two.

**How decks use them:** creatures are the main way most decks win. Aggro decks play many cheap ones, while control decks may run only a handful of powerful finishers.

## Instant

Instants are spells you can cast almost any time you have priority, including during your opponent's turn and in response to other spells. That flexibility is the whole point of the type.

Lightning Bolt (`{R}`, 3 damage to any target) and Counterspell (`{U}{U}`, counter target spell) are the textbook instants. You can Bolt an attacking creature during combat, or hold up Counterspell on your opponent's turn and only use it if they cast something worth stopping.

**How decks use them:** removal, counterspells, combat tricks and card draw are often instants so you can wait for the best moment. Decks that pass the turn with mana open are relying on instants.

## Sorcery

Sorceries are one-shot spells that you can cast only during your own main phase, when the stack is empty. In exchange for that restriction, sorceries tend to be more powerful for their cost than comparable instants.

Wrath of God (`{2}{W}{W}`) destroys all creatures and they can't be regenerated. An effect that big is a sorcery partly so that it can't be used as a surprise in the middle of combat. "Sorcery speed" means "only on your main phase with nothing on the stack," and most permanent spells are cast at sorcery speed too.

**How decks use them:** board wipes, discard, big card draw and burn that only hits players are common sorceries.

## Artifact

Artifacts are permanents that represent objects, machines and treasures. Most are colorless, so any deck can play them, though colored artifacts exist too.

Sol Ring (`{1}`, tap for two colorless mana) is the most famous mana artifact. Artifacts come in several common families, marked by subtypes:

- **Equipment** attaches to a creature you control for its equip cost and boosts it. Bonesplitter gives the equipped creature +2/+0 and has an equip cost of `{1}`. If the creature leaves, the Equipment stays on the battlefield.
- **Vehicles** are artifacts with power and toughness that only become creatures when you crew them by tapping creatures with enough total power. Smuggler's Copter is a well-known example.
- **Tokens like Treasure, Food and Clue** are artifacts that you sacrifice for a small benefit: a mana, some life, or a card.

An "Artifact Creature," such as Solemn Simulacrum, is both. It can attack and block, and it can also be destroyed by anything that destroys artifacts.

**How decks use them:** mana acceleration, Equipment for aggressive creature decks, and colorless utility that fits anywhere.

## Enchantment

Enchantments are permanents that represent ongoing magical effects. Some affect the whole game, and some attach to a single permanent.

- **Auras** are enchantments that enchant something, usually a creature. Pacifism (`{1}{W}`) enchants a creature, and that creature can't attack or block. If the enchanted creature leaves the battlefield, the Aura goes to the graveyard.
- **Global enchantments** just sit on the battlefield and do something. Oblivion Ring exiles another nonland permanent when it enters and returns that card when it leaves.

Some cards are "Enchantment Creatures," which are creatures that also count as enchantments.

**How decks use them:** removal (Pacifism, Oblivion Ring), steady advantage engines, and in enchantment-themed decks, a pile of cards that reward each other.

## Planeswalker

Planeswalkers are powerful permanents that act like allies on your side. Each enters with the loyalty printed in its bottom-right corner, and has loyalty abilities marked with plus, minus or zero costs. Using a plus ability adds loyalty counters; a minus ability removes them.

Key rules:

- You can activate one loyalty ability of each planeswalker per turn, only at sorcery speed.
- Your opponents can attack your planeswalkers with their creatures instead of attacking you. Damage dealt to a planeswalker removes that many loyalty counters.
- A planeswalker with zero loyalty is put into its owner's graveyard.
- Planeswalkers are legendary, so you can't keep two with the same name.

Jace, the Mind Sculptor and Liliana of the Veil are long-standing examples. A planeswalker left alone for a few turns usually takes over the game.

**How decks use them:** as repeatable sources of card advantage, removal or tokens. Control and midrange decks lean on them most.

## Battle

Battles are the newest permanent type, introduced in March of the Machine. The most common battle subtype is Siege. When you cast a Siege, you choose an opponent to protect it, and it enters with defense counters (the number in its bottom-right corner). You, and any teammates, can attack the battle. Combat damage dealt to it removes defense counters, and its protector can block to defend it.

When the last defense counter is removed, the battle is defeated: you exile it and can cast its back face without paying its mana cost. Most battles also do something immediately when they enter, so even an undefeated battle has value.

**How decks use them:** as a removal or value spell with an upside if your creatures can push through the damage.

## Kindred

Kindred (formerly called tribal) is a card type that only appears alongside another type. Its job is to let non-creature cards have creature subtypes, so they count for effects that care about those types.

Bitterblossom is a "Kindred Enchantment — Faerie." It's an enchantment that creates 1/1 flying Faerie tokens each upkeep, and because it has the Faerie subtype, cards that look for Faeries can find or reward it. ## Supertypes

Supertypes appear before the card type and add rules of their own.

| Supertype | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic | Exempt from the usual copy limit in Constructed deck-building; you can run as many as you want | Island |
| Legendary | If you control two or more legendary permanents with the same name, you choose one and put the rest into their owners' graveyards | Most planeswalkers and many famous characters |
| Snow | No rule on its own; other cards check for snow permanents or snow mana | Snow-Covered Forest |
| World | If more than one world permanent is on the battlefield, all but the newest go to the graveyard. Very rare and very old | Old world enchantments |

Legendary is the one you will see most. It's also the gate for Commander: your commander normally has to be a legendary creature (some cards say they can be your commander instead).

## Subtypes

Subtypes come after the dash and work differently for each card type. They don't do anything by themselves in most cases; they matter because rules and other cards refer to them.

- **Creature types**, like Elf, Goblin, Human or Wizard. A creature can have several. Lords and tribal payoffs care about these.
- **Land types**, especially the five basic land types. A nonbasic land with the Forest subtype taps for green and counts as a Forest for any card that looks for one.
- **Artifact types**, like Equipment, Vehicle, Treasure, Food and Clue. Several of these carry rules (Equipment has equip, Vehicles have crew).
- **Enchantment types**, like Aura, Saga, Curse and Shrine. Aura and Saga carry real rules; others mostly exist for card interactions.
- **Spell types** for instants and sorceries, like Arcane, Lesson and Adventure.
- **Planeswalker types**, like Jace or Chandra, and **battle types**, like Siege.

## Cards with more than one type

A card with two card types follows all the rules for both. Some examples worth recognizing:

- **Artifact Creature:** dies to creature removal and artifact removal alike.
- **Artifact Land,** like Seat of the Synod: you play it as your land for the turn, and it's also an artifact for any effect that counts or destroys artifacts.
- **Land Creature,** like Dryad Arbor: a land you play, which is also a creature and so has summoning sickness, can attack, and can die to creature removal.

Read the full type line of any card your opponent plays: it tells you which of your answers can touch it.

## The takeaway

Learn the permanent and non-permanent split first, then the timing difference between instants and everything else. Once those two ideas click, the rest of the types are just variations: creatures fight, artifacts and enchantments provide lasting effects, planeswalkers and battles are permanents you defend or attack, and supertypes and subtypes are labels other cards care about.
