Combat in Magic: The Gathering happens in five steps: beginning of combat, declare attackers, declare blockers, combat damage, and end of combat. The active player chooses which creatures attack, the defending player chooses which creatures block, and then all the creatures deal their damage at the same time. Players can cast instants and activate abilities in every step, which is where most of combat's strategy comes from.

This guide walks through each step in order, then covers multiple blockers, first strike and the timing of combat tricks. If you've never played a game before, the [beginner's guide to playing Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) explains the turn as a whole first.

## Where combat fits in the turn

Each turn has five phases: beginning, first main phase, combat, second main phase, and ending. Combat sits between the two main phases. That placement matters because it gives you a choice: you can cast creatures and other spells before combat, or hold them until after you've seen how combat played out.

You get one combat phase per turn unless a card gives you an extra one. Attacking is optional. If you don't want to attack, you can let the combat phase pass with nothing happening.

A few terms you'll need:

- **Active player:** the player whose turn it is. They're the attacker.
- **Defending player:** the opponent being attacked.
- **Priority:** the right to cast a spell or activate an ability. In each combat step, both players get a chance to act before the game moves on.
- **Tap:** turning a card sideways to show it's been used.

## Step 1: Beginning of combat

Nothing is declared yet in this step. It exists so both players have a window to act before attackers are chosen.

Why it matters:

- **"At the beginning of combat" triggers** happen here. Some cards say exactly that, and they resolve before attackers are declared.
- **The defending player's last chance to stop an attacker in advance.** If you want to tap down or kill a creature before it can attack, this is the step to do it. If you act during their main phase, they still have the rest of that main phase to change plans, for example by casting a different creature with haste. Acting at the beginning of combat leaves them less room, because they've already chosen to move to combat.

## Step 2: Declare attackers

The active player chooses which of their creatures will attack, and what each one attacks. A creature can attack the defending player, a planeswalker that player controls, or a battle the player is protecting. All attackers are declared at once.

To attack, a creature must be:

- **Untapped.** A tapped creature can't attack.
- **Under your control since the start of your turn.** This is the rule people call summoning sickness. A creature you just cast this turn can't attack unless it has haste.

Declaring a creature as an attacker taps it, unless it has vigilance. That's important, because tapped creatures can't block on your opponent's next turn. Attacking with everything leaves you open to a counterattack.

Once attackers are declared, any abilities that trigger on attacking go on the stack, and then both players get priority. The defending player often uses this window to remove an attacker before blocks.

## Step 3: Declare blockers

The defending player now chooses which of their untapped creatures will block, and which attacker each one blocks.

The basic rules:

- **Each untapped creature can block one attacker,** unless a card says it can block more.
- **Blocking doesn't tap the blocker.** Tapped creatures can't block, but blocking itself doesn't cause tapping.
- **Several creatures can block the same attacker.** That's called a multiple block, covered below.
- **Blocking is optional.** Any attacker that isn't blocked is unblocked, and it will deal its damage to the player, planeswalker or battle it's attacking.
- **Evasion restricts blocks.** A creature with flying can only be blocked by creatures with flying or reach. Other abilities, like menace, require a creature to be blocked by two or more creatures.

### Once blocked, always blocked

An attacking creature that has been blocked stays blocked for the rest of combat, even if every creature blocking it leaves the battlefield. If you kill the only blocker with a removal spell, the attacker doesn't suddenly hit the player. Unless it has trample, it deals no combat damage at all.

After blockers are declared, both players get priority again. This is the most common window for combat tricks, because both players now know exactly how the combat lines up.

## Step 4: Combat damage

Each attacking and blocking creature deals damage equal to its power. All of this damage happens at the same time.

- **Unblocked attackers** deal their damage to the player, planeswalker or battle they're attacking.
- **Blocked attackers** deal their damage to the creatures blocking them.
- **Blockers** deal their damage to the attacker they're blocking.

A creature dies when the damage marked on it is equal to or greater than its toughness. So if a 3/3 blocks a 2/2, the 2/2 takes 3 damage and dies, while the 3/3 takes 2 damage and survives. Damage stays marked on a creature until the end of the turn, then it wears off.

Players don't get priority between assigning damage and dealing it. There's no way to respond to damage once the step begins. Priority comes after damage has been dealt, which matters for abilities that trigger on dealing damage.

### Trample

A creature with trample that's blocked can deal its excess damage to the player or planeswalker it's attacking. The attacker must assign at least lethal damage to its blockers first, which normally means damage equal to their remaining toughness, and anything beyond that can go through. A 5/5 trampler blocked by a 2/2 can assign 2 to the blocker and 3 to the player.

### Deathtouch

Any amount of damage from a creature with deathtouch is enough to destroy a creature. When a deathtouch creature has to assign lethal damage, 1 damage counts as lethal. That makes deathtouch plus trample a strong combination: the attacker only needs to assign 1 to each blocker.

## Step 5: End of combat

This step closes out combat. Some cards trigger "at end of combat," and players get one last chance to act before the second main phase. After this step, creatures are no longer attacking or blocking.

If no creatures were declared as attackers, the declare blockers and combat damage steps are skipped entirely, and the game moves from declare attackers straight to end of combat.

## Multiple blockers

When two or more creatures block the same attacker, all of them deal damage to the attacker, and the attacker splits its damage among them.

Picture a 4/4 attacking, blocked by a 2/2 and a 3/3. The two blockers deal 5 damage to the 4/4 combined, which kills it. The attacker's controller decides how to split the 4 damage between the blockers. They could put 2 on the 2/2 and 2 on the 3/3, which kills only the 2/2, or put all 4 on the 3/3 to kill it. They can't kill both.

Under the current Comprehensive Rules, the attacking player divides damage among blockers however they choose when combat damage is assigned. Older guides describe a "damage assignment order" that the attacker set when blockers were declared and that forced lethal damage onto the first blocker before the next. That order has since been removed from the rules, so if you learned it, you can drop it. Trample still requires lethal damage on every blocker before any damage goes through to the player.

Multiple blocks are a strong defensive tool. Ganging up lets two smaller creatures kill a big attacker. The risk is that the attacker might have a combat trick that saves it or kills both blockers.

## First strike and the extra damage step

Normally, all combat damage is dealt at once. First strike and double strike change that.

If any attacking or blocking creature has first strike or double strike as the combat damage step begins, combat has two damage steps instead of one:

1. **First-strike damage step:** only creatures with first strike or double strike deal damage.
2. **Regular damage step:** creatures without first strike deal damage, and creatures with double strike deal damage again.

Any creature that dies in the first step doesn't deal damage in the second. A 2/2 with first strike blocking a 2/2 without it kills the attacker before it can strike back. That's why first strike is such a strong ability in combat.

Players get priority between the two damage steps. You can cast a spell after first-strike damage, which can matter if you want to pump a creature that survived.

## When to cast combat tricks

A combat trick is an instant or ability used during combat to change the outcome, like a pump spell, a removal spell or a card that grants an ability. Timing is everything.

**For the attacker:**

- The best time is usually after blockers are declared. The defender has committed, and a pump spell can make a blocked creature win the fight.
- Casting a trick before blocks lets the defender change their block in response to what you did.

**For the defender:**

- Use removal in the beginning of combat step to stop an attacker before it can be declared.
- Use removal after attackers are declared to remove an attacker before blocks, which keeps your creatures safe.
- Use a trick after blockers are declared, often after the attacker passes priority, to win the fight you chose.

**Remember:** neither player gets to act during the actual dealing of damage. If you want to change the outcome, you must do it before the combat damage step begins.

## The takeaway

Combat has five steps, and each one is a decision point. Choose attackers knowing they'll be tapped for your opponent's turn, block knowing that a blocked creature stays blocked, and save your tricks for after blockers are declared, when everyone has committed. Learn how first strike and multiple blocks change the math, and you'll win far more of the fights on the battlefield.
