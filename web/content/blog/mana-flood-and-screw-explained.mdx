Mana flood is drawing too many lands and too few spells. Mana screw is the opposite: not drawing enough lands, or not the right colors, to cast the spells in your hand. Both happen because a Magic deck is shuffled, and any random draw will sometimes clump lands together or leave them at the bottom. You can't eliminate them, but you can make them a lot rarer and less painful with the right land count, sensible mulligans, card filtering and cards that give extra lands something to do.

This guide is about the variance itself and the tools that reduce it. For how to pick a land count from your curve, see the [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained). For which hands to keep, see the [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide). Both are linked again where they matter below.

## Why flood and screw happen

A shuffled deck is a random order, and random orders contain streaks. If your deck is 40% lands, you'll average a little under three lands in a seven-card hand, but "average" hides a wide spread. Some hands will have one land, some will have five, and the following draws can make either problem worse.

Here are the approximate odds for a 60-card deck with 24 lands, looking only at the opening seven:

| Lands in opening seven | Approximate chance |
| --- | --- |
| 0 or 1 | about 14% |
| 2 to 4 | about 77% |
| 5 or more | about 8% |

Roughly one hand in five falls outside the comfortable range before you've drawn a single card. Draws during the game add more variance on top. That's not bad luck in any meaningful sense. It's what a 24-land deck does, and every deck in the format faces the same math.

Dropping to 20 lands raises the chance of a zero- or one-land hand to roughly a quarter. Raising to 26 pushes the chance of five or more lands to over one in ten. There's no land count that avoids both problems, only a count that balances them for your deck.

### Screw comes in two flavors

**Land screw** is the familiar one: you're stuck on two lands with a hand full of four-drops.

**Color screw** is when you have enough lands but not the right types. You have three lands that all make white mana, and your hand is blue cards. Color screw gets more likely with each color you add and each spell that needs multiple mana of one color. A spell that costs `{U}{U}` is much harder to cast on curve in a two-color deck than one that costs `{1}{U}`.

### Flood is quieter but just as deadly

Flood rarely feels as dramatic as screw. You're casting something every turn, but by turn eight you're drawing a land each turn while the opponent draws spells. Each land you draw after you have enough is effectively a blank card, and a few blanks in a row hand the opponent a card advantage you never chose to give them.

## Step one: the right land count

The single biggest lever is the number of lands in your deck. Too few and you get screwed more often. Too many and you flood more often. The right number depends on your curve, not on a universal rule.

Common starting points:

- **Low-curve aggro decks** in 60-card formats often play around 20 to 22 lands.
- **Midrange and control decks** often play around 24 to 26, because they need to hit land drops for longer.
- **40-card Limited decks** usually start at 17 lands.
- **100-card Commander decks** often play somewhere in the mid to high 30s, adjusted for ramp.

These are starting points. The [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) walks through how your spells' mana values should move the number up or down.

Two adjustments are worth knowing:

1. **Cheap card draw and ramp can stand in for a land or two.** A deck full of one-mana cantrips or mana creatures like Llanowar Elves can often shave a land, because those cards help you find or replace them.
2. **Expensive, game-winning cards push the count up.** If your best card costs six, missing land drops is far worse than drawing an extra land.

## Step two: mulligan the hands that are already broken

A hand with zero or one land, or six or seven, is often flood or screw before the game begins. Mulliganing it trades one card for a much better chance at a functional game. Most players keep too many risky hands because going down a card feels bad.

The specifics, including how the current mulligan rule lets you bottom cards, live in the [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide). The short version for this topic: judge a hand by whether it can cast its spells on time, not by how good the spells are.

## Step three: card filtering

Filtering cards let you see more of your deck and choose what you keep, which turns some flood and screw draws into good ones. They don't add cards to your hand on their own, but they improve the quality of what you draw.

- **Scry** lets you look at the top cards of your library and put any of them on the bottom. Scry after a land-heavy opening and you can bottom extra lands. Scry when you're short and you can dig for one.
- **Cantrips** are cheap spells that replace themselves. Opt scries 1 and draws a card. Preordain scries 2 and then draws. Brainstorm draws three and puts two back. In formats that allow them, these cards are a large part of why blue decks feel consistent.
- **Looting** means drawing and then discarding. Faithless Looting draws two and discards two, which lets you pitch extra lands when flooded. Looting doesn't gain you cards, but it converts dead cards into live ones.
- **Cycling** is a keyword that lets you pay a cost and discard the card to draw a new one. Some lands have cycling, which makes them a land when you need one and a fresh card when you don't.
- **Modal double-faced cards** with a spell on one side and a land on the other are close to the perfect anti-variance card. Early, play it as a land. Late, cast the spell.

Filtering is strongest in blue and red, but almost every color has some version of it.

## Step four: mana sinks for flooded games

A mana sink is any card that lets you spend extra mana for value. Sinks are the main defense against flood, because they turn your fifth, sixth and seventh lands into something useful.

Common forms:

- **Creature lands** are lands that can become creatures. Mutavault, for example, taps for colorless mana or can become a 2/2 until end of turn for `{1}`. Each one is a land that also attacks when you have mana to spare.
- **X spells** scale with your mana. Walking Ballista enters with X +1/+1 counters, so it's a small play early and a large threat late. It also has an ability that adds another counter for `{4}`, which keeps soaking up mana after it's on the battlefield.
- **Activated abilities** on permanents, like card draw for mana or a creature you can pump, give every spare land a job.
- **Kicker and similar costs** let a card do more if you pay extra. They're fine early and better when you're flooded.
- **Flashback** gives you a second use of a card from your graveyard, which is another way to spend extra mana later.

You don't need a lot of these. One or two sinks in a midrange or control deck often decide the games where you'd otherwise flood out.

## Step five: ramp and mana fixing for screw

Screw has its own set of answers.

- **Cheap mana sources** like Llanowar Elves or two-mana mana rocks help you hit the next mana level even if you miss a land drop. The [ramp guide](/blog/mtg-ramp-deck-guide) covers these in more depth.
- **A lower curve** is the most reliable cure. If your deck functions on three lands, being stuck on three is fine.
- **Mana fixing** reduces color screw: dual lands, lands that fetch other lands, and mana sources that produce more than one color. Fixing matters more the more colors and multi-pip costs you play.
- **Fewer colors** is the simplest fix of all. Two colors with good lands is far more consistent than three colors with mediocre ones.

## Playing through it

Even a well-built deck will flood or get screwed sometimes. A few habits help in those games.

1. **When screwed, prioritize your cheapest useful spells.** Use your mana every turn, even if it's not the ideal play.
2. **When flooded, keep lands in hand when possible.** Holding a land you don't need can bluff a trick or a counterspell, and gives a looting effect something to discard.
3. **Change your role.** A deck stuck on lands might need to play defensively and wait. A flooded deck might need to use its sinks to grind instead of racing. The question of who's the aggressor shifts with your mana.
4. **Don't misread variance as a deck problem.** One flooded game tells you nothing. If you see a pattern over many games, adjust the land count by one and test again.

## The takeaway

Flood and screw are built into a shuffled deck. You reduce them by picking a land count that fits your curve, mulliganing hands that can't function, playing card filtering to smooth draws, and including a few mana sinks so extra lands still matter.
