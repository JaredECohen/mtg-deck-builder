A token deck wins by putting lots of small creature tokens onto the battlefield and then making them all bigger at once, or by turning them into another resource like damage, mana or life. Instead of relying on a few big threats, it spreads its power across a wide board, which makes one-for-one removal weak against it. Its main weakness is cards that hit every creature at once, so building and playing a token deck is mostly about going wide fast while protecting yourself from sweepers.

This guide covers how the strategy works, the card roles every token deck needs, how to play around board wipes, and how the plan differs between 60-card formats and Commander. For a broader view of archetypes, see [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained).

## What "going wide" means

There are two basic ways to build a board. **Going tall** means putting a lot of power into one or two creatures, often with equipment, auras or counters. **Going wide** means putting many creatures into play and using effects that reward having lots of them.

Token decks go wide. A single card might create two, three or even more creatures. Each token on its own is usually small, often a 1/1, but ten of them deal ten damage, and an effect that gives each one +1/+1 doubles that.

Going wide has some built-in advantages:

- **Removal is inefficient.** Using a removal spell on a single 1/1 token is a bad trade for your opponent.
- **Blocking is hard.** An opponent with three blockers can't stop seven attackers.
- **Payoffs scale.** A card that gives your creatures +1/+1 is much better when you have eight of them.

A quick note on rules: tokens are permanents that aren't represented by a card. They enter the battlefield like any creature and are subject to summoning sickness. If a token leaves the battlefield, whether it dies, gets exiled or returns to hand, it stops existing.

## The three parts of a token deck

Most token decks are built from three kinds of cards: token makers, payoffs that make the tokens stronger, and outlets that turn tokens into something else.

### Token makers

These are the cards that create your army. The best ones make multiple tokens at once, or keep making them turn after turn.

- **Burst makers** create several tokens from one card. Raise the Alarm creates two 1/1 white Soldier tokens at instant speed. Spectral Procession creates three 1/1 white Spirit tokens with flying. Lingering Souls creates two 1/1 flying Spirits, and its flashback lets you cast it again from the graveyard for two more.
- **Repeatable makers** produce tokens every turn. These are creatures, enchantments, artifacts or planeswalkers that create a token as an activated or triggered ability. Planeswalkers such as Garruk Wildspeaker, which can make a 3/3 Beast, fit here; see [how planeswalkers work](/blog/mtg-planeswalkers-explained) if you're new to them.
- **Scaling makers** create a number of tokens that grows with your mana, such as Secure the Wastes, an instant that creates X 1/1 white Warrior tokens.

Repeatable makers are especially valuable because they rebuild your board after a sweeper.

### Anthems and mass pump

An **anthem** is an effect that gives all your creatures a bonus, usually +1/+1, for as long as it's on the battlefield. The name comes from the classic enchantment Glorious Anthem, which gives creatures you control +1/+1.

Anthems are the reason a pile of 1/1s becomes a real threat. Some examples:

- **Glorious Anthem**: creatures you control get +1/+1.
- **Intangible Virtue**: creature tokens you control get +1/+1 and have vigilance.
- **Honor of the Pure**: white creatures you control get +1/+1.

Two anthems turn every 1/1 into a 3/3, and the effect stacks across your whole board.

**Mass pump** is the one-shot version: a spell that gives every creature a big bonus for one turn. Overrun gives creatures you control +3/+3 and trample until end of turn. With six tokens in play, that's 24 extra damage from one card. Mass pump is often how token decks finish games: build wide for a few turns, then cast it and swing with everything.

### Sacrifice outlets

A **sacrifice outlet** is a card that lets you sacrifice a creature as a cost, usually for free or for very little. Token decks love them because tokens are cheap to give up.

- **Viscera Seer** lets you sacrifice a creature to scry 1.
- **Ashnod's Altar** lets you sacrifice a creature to add two colorless mana.
- **Goblin Bombardment** lets you sacrifice a creature to deal 1 damage to any target.

Outlets do three jobs:

1. **Turn tokens into damage, mana or card selection.** Goblin Bombardment can finish the game when combat can't.
2. **Get value from tokens that would die anyway.** If an opponent casts a sweeper, sacrifice your tokens in response to get something for them.
3. **Fuel death triggers.** Cards that trigger when a creature dies, like Blood Artist, turn every sacrificed token into drain damage.

Decks that lean heavily into sacrifice become their own archetype, often called aristocrats. Token decks borrow from that plan without fully committing to it.

## The weakness: sweepers

The biggest problem for token decks is the **sweeper**, a spell that destroys or damages every creature at once. Wrath of God destroys all creatures. Pyroclasm deals 2 damage to each creature, which is enough to kill a board of 1/1s and 2/2s.

A sweeper is everything a token deck fears: one card that answers many of yours. A token deck that dumps its whole hand onto the battlefield and loses it to a single sweeper often can't recover.

### How to play around sweepers

- **Don't overextend.** If you already have enough creatures to pressure your opponent, keep the next token maker in hand. If they sweep, you rebuild immediately.
- **Use instant-speed token makers.** Raise the Alarm and Secure the Wastes can be cast at the end of your opponent's turn, after they've had their chance to sweep, so your new tokens are ready to attack.
- **Rely on repeatable makers.** An enchantment or planeswalker that makes tokens every turn survives most creature sweepers, so your army comes back.
- **Use flashback and recursion.** Lingering Souls is good against sweepers because half of it comes back from the graveyard.
- **Sacrifice in response.** With an outlet, you can respond to a sweeper by sacrificing your tokens for value, so the sweeper only takes out what you couldn't use.
- **Play protection.** Cards like Heroic Intervention, which gives your permanents hexproof and indestructible until end of turn, can blank a sweeper. Indestructible creatures survive both "destroy" effects and lethal damage, but not exile or effects that reduce toughness to 0.

Note that anthems help against damage-based sweepers. Two anthems turn your 1/1s into 3/3s, which survive Pyroclasm.

## Other weaknesses

Sweepers aren't the only problem:

- **Big blockers.** A single huge creature can block one token per turn, but tokens without evasion or pump spells may never get through a wall of large creatures. Flying tokens, trample from mass pump, and anthems help.
- **Removal on your payoff.** If your opponent removes your anthem or your repeatable maker, your tokens go back to being small. Spread your payoffs out so one removal spell doesn't stop the deck.
- **Running out of cards.** Token decks deal damage by deploying many cards. Long games against decks with more card advantage can favor the opponent unless your token makers are repeatable.

## Token decks in 60-card formats

In 60-card Constructed formats like Standard, Pioneer and Modern, token decks usually play as aggro or midrange. They want to go wide quickly and finish the game before sweepers and bigger threats take over.

Typical features of a 60-card token deck:

- **Lots of cheap token makers** to build a board fast.
- **Several anthems or mass pumps** to turn small creatures into lethal damage.
- **A curve that tops out low**, around four or five mana.
- **Some instant-speed makers** to play around sweepers.
- **A sideboard that adapts.** Against sweeper-heavy decks, add more instant-speed and repeatable makers.

Many token decks are also built around a creature type, such as Soldiers, Spirits or Goblins. If you like that angle, see the [tribal decks guide](/blog/mtg-tribal-decks-guide).

## Token decks in Commander

Commander is a great home for token decks. The format has 100-card singleton decks, more life (40 per player) and multiple opponents, which changes the plan:

- **Games are longer,** so repeatable token makers matter even more than burst ones.
- **Sweepers are more common,** because players need to answer boards from several opponents. Expect your board to be wiped at least once.
- **Bigger payoffs are available.** Cards like Anointed Procession and Parallel Lives double the tokens you create, and big finishers that pump everything can end games against multiple opponents at once.
- **Your commander can be the engine.** Krenko, Mob Boss can tap to create a 1/1 red Goblin token for each Goblin you control, which grows exponentially if it isn't answered.
- **Expect attention.** A board full of tokens makes you look threatening, so other players may target you. Spread your threats out and hold back resources when you can.

If you're putting together a Commander deck for the first time, [how to build your first Commander deck](/blog/how-to-build-your-first-commander-deck) walks through the structure.

## Common mistakes with token decks

1. **Playing every token maker as soon as possible.** Hold some back to recover from sweepers.
2. **Too few payoffs.** Tokens alone are weak. You need anthems, mass pumps or sacrifice outlets to make them matter.
3. **Too many payoffs.** An anthem without tokens does nothing. Keep a healthy ratio, with more makers than payoffs.
4. **Ignoring flying and evasion.** Ground tokens can be stopped by a single big blocker. Flying tokens are much harder to block.
5. **Not using sacrifice outlets before a sweeper resolves.** Respond to the sweeper with your outlets so you get value.

## The takeaway

A token deck is built from token makers, payoffs and sacrifice outlets. Go wide quickly, use anthems and mass pump to turn small creatures into a lethal attack, and keep some resources back so a sweeper doesn't end your game. In Commander, lean on repeatable makers and doublers to survive longer games.
