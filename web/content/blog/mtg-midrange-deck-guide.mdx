A midrange deck plays efficient threats and efficient answers, and wins by having better cards than the opponent at every point on the curve. It's slower than aggro and faster than control, so it can beat aggro by out-sizing it and beat control by pressuring it. The skill of playing midrange is knowing, every game, which of those two roles you're in.

The [deck archetypes overview](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) introduces midrange alongside the other major strategies. This guide goes further: why the plan works, what a midrange list is made of, and how to make the in-game decisions that define the archetype.

## The midrange game plan

Midrange doesn't try to win on a specific turn, and it doesn't try to answer every card. Instead, it tries to make each card it plays a little better than the card the opponent plays in response.

A typical game looks like this:

1. **Disrupt early.** Use a cheap removal spell or discard spell to blunt the opponent's start.
2. **Deploy strong threats in the middle turns.** Creatures and planeswalkers that cost three to five mana and are hard to deal with efficiently.
3. **Trade resources and grind.** Each threat either demands an answer or takes over the game. Each answer removes the opponent's best card.
4. **Win with whatever is left.** Midrange doesn't need a specific finisher. Whichever threat survives is the win condition.

The plan is flexible by design. Midrange doesn't have a single best line; it has a toolbox, and it picks the right tool for the matchup.

### Why card quality matters

Midrange relies on card quality, meaning each card is strong on its own and doesn't need other specific cards to work. Synergy-driven decks can produce more powerful turns, but they're vulnerable when a key piece is removed. Midrange is built so that removing any one card doesn't break the plan.

That's why midrange decks often play cards that are good in many situations rather than great in one. A removal spell that hits most threats is preferred over one that only hits certain creatures. A creature that's useful early and late is preferred over one that's only good in a specific board state.

## Switching roles: aggressor or controller?

Every game of Magic: The Gathering has a player who needs to win quickly and a player who benefits from the game going long. Midrange is the archetype that can be either, depending on the opponent.

**Against aggro, midrange is the control deck.** Aggro's creatures are smaller and cheaper. Midrange uses its removal to trade off early threats, then plays bigger creatures that aggro can't attack through. The goal is to stabilize and win with card quality. The tactics for this are covered in [how to beat aggro](/blog/how-to-play-against-aggro).

**Against control, midrange is the aggressor.** Control wants a long game with time to set up board wipes and card draw. Midrange applies steady pressure with threats that cost different amounts, so a single board wipe never catches more than one or two. It uses discard to strip answers and makes the control player act before they want to. Knowing how a [control deck](/blog/mtg-control-deck-guide) thinks makes this much easier.

**Against other midrange decks,** the role depends on the board. The player with stronger threats or more card advantage plays the long game, and the other player has to push damage.

### How to decide your role

Ask three questions at the start of each game, and again whenever the board changes:

- **Who has the faster clock?** If the opponent can kill you sooner than you can kill them, you're the controller.
- **Who wins a long game?** If the opponent's late game is better than yours, you need to be the aggressor.
- **What does my hand want?** A hand full of removal plays like control. A hand full of threats pushes damage.

Misreading your role is the most common midrange mistake. Attacking recklessly against an aggro deck, or sitting back against a control deck, throws away the advantages midrange is built on.

## The typical card mix

There's no fixed recipe, but most midrange decks in 60-card formats land in a similar shape.

| Card type | Rough count | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lands | 23 to 25 | Hit land drops through turn five or so |
| Cheap interaction | 8 to 12 | Removal and discard that trade with early threats |
| Mid-cost threats | 10 to 16 | Creatures and planeswalkers that dominate the board |
| Card advantage | A few | Cards that generate extra value or refill your hand |
| Flex slots | The rest | Matchup-specific or utility cards |

These ranges vary a lot by format and color, but the balance is the point: roughly as many answers as threats, with a curve centered around two to four mana. Your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) should be smoother than control's and higher than aggro's.

### Interaction

Midrange's interaction is chosen for efficiency and flexibility. Typical choices include:

- **Discard** like Thoughtseize, which lets you look at the opponent's hand and take their best nonland card. It costs you 2 life, but the information alone shapes the rest of the game.
- **Cheap removal** like Fatal Push or Lightning Bolt, which answers early threats without costing a whole turn.
- **Flexible removal** that hits multiple card types, valuable because midrange faces many different strategies.

### Threats

The best midrange threats are hard to answer cleanly or generate value even when they are. Classic examples include:

- **Creatures that bring value with them,** like Bloodbraid Elf, whose cascade casts a cheaper spell from the top of your library for free, or Kitchen Finks, which gains life and comes back once after dying.
- **Planeswalkers,** like Liliana of the Veil, which can force discards or remove creatures and threatens to take over a long game.
- **Efficient bodies** that are bigger than their mana cost suggests, like Tarmogoyf, whose size depends on the card types in all graveyards.

The common thread: the opponent should feel like answering the threat costs them something, and ignoring it costs them more.

### Card advantage

Midrange often gets card advantage through its threats rather than through dedicated draw spells. A creature that kills something when it enters the battlefield, or a planeswalker that produces value every turn, keeps your hand and board full without spending a slot on pure draw.

## Playing midrange well

A few habits make a big difference.

- **Use your discard to plan.** When you look at the opponent's hand, note what you didn't take. That's information about the next several turns.
- **Don't trade answers for weak threats.** Midrange has fewer answers than control. Spend them on the cards that would actually beat you.
- **Vary your threats against sweepers.** Against decks with board wipes, play one or two strong threats at a time instead of emptying your hand.
- **Count to the end of the game.** Midrange wins by small edges. Know how many turns your clock needs and how many the opponent's needs.
- **Sideboard aggressively.** Midrange's broad card pool makes it one of the easiest archetypes to tune between games. Cut the cards that are weak in the matchup and bring in the ones that aren't. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers the process.

## Common midrange mistakes

1. **Playing the wrong role.** The single most costly error. Decide whether you're the aggressor or controller, and act accordingly.
2. **Overloading on synergy.** Adding cards that only work together undermines the card quality that makes midrange resilient.
3. **Too many expensive threats.** A curve that's too high turns midrange into a slow deck that gets run over by aggro.
4. **Holding removal too long.** Waiting for the perfect target can let a cheaper threat deal a lot of damage.
5. **Not adapting after game one.** Midrange's strength is flexibility. A static deck against every opponent wastes it.

## The takeaway

Midrange wins with card quality and adaptability. Build a deck of strong individual cards with a balance of answers and threats, a curve centered on the middle turns, and enough lands to cast everything. Then play each game with a clear sense of role: control against aggro, aggressor against control, and whichever the board demands against everything else.
