Standard is Magic: The Gathering's main rotating Constructed format: you build a 60-card deck using only cards from the most recent sets, and as new sets are released, the oldest ones eventually leave the format. That keeps the card pool small and the format fresh. It's the format most new sets are designed around, it's playable on MTG Arena, and it's a common choice for store events.

If you want a quick comparison with Pioneer, Modern, Commander and the rest, start with our [MTG formats overview](/blog/mtg-formats-explained). This guide focuses on Standard alone: how the pool works, why it rotates, and how to get into it without wasting money or time.

## What Standard is

Standard is a **Constructed** format, which means you build your deck in advance from cards you own, as opposed to Limited formats like draft where you build from cards opened on the spot. It's also a **rotating** format: its card pool is limited to a window of recent sets rather than every card ever printed.

That second point is what makes Standard distinct. In non-rotating formats like Modern or Commander, cards printed decades ago are still legal, so the pool only grows. Standard's pool grows with each new set and shrinks when older sets rotate out. At any time, it's a relatively small, curated slice of Magic.

## Deck-building rules

Standard uses the same basic Constructed rules as most 60-card formats:

| Rule | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| Main deck | At least 60 cards, no maximum |
| Copies per card | Up to 4 of any card with the same name, except basic lands |
| Sideboard | Up to 15 cards |
| Starting life | 20 |
| Legal cards | Only cards from Standard-legal sets, minus any banned cards |

Two details trip up new players. First, the four-copy limit counts by English card name, so different printings of the same card all count toward the same four. Second, legality follows the card, not the printing: if a card appears in a Standard-legal set, an older printing of that same card is legal too.

## How the card pool works

Standard-legal sets are mainly Wizards of the Coast's regular expansion sets, the main releases that come out several times a year. Not every product counts. Cards that debut in products designed for other formats, such as Commander decks, are generally not added to Standard. Reprint-focused sets typically aren't either.

The safe habit is simple: check the official Standard format page from Wizards before you buy cards for Standard. It lists exactly which sets are legal and which cards are banned. Third-party deck-building tools, including Deck Lab, also flag cards that aren't legal in the format you've chosen.

### Rotation in general terms

Rotation is the process by which older sets leave Standard. When a rotation happens, the oldest group of sets in the format becomes illegal in Standard all at once, and the pool shrinks to the newer sets.

A few things are worth knowing about rotation without tying yourself to a specific calendar:

- **It's announced.** Wizards publishes when rotation happens and which sets leave. The schedule has changed several times over Magic's history, so trust the official announcement rather than an old forum post.
- **Rotated cards aren't worthless.** A card that leaves Standard stays legal in non-rotating formats where it was already legal, such as Pioneer, Modern and Commander. Many players move rotating decks into those formats.
- **Rotation shakes up the format.** When older sets leave, decks built around them lose key pieces, and new strategies get room to emerge. For many Standard players, the weeks after rotation are the most exciting time of the year.

### Bans

Standard has a banned list, like every competitive format. When a card or deck makes the format unhealthy, Wizards can ban cards. Bans are announced on Wizards' official channels, and they take effect on a stated date. Check the current list before you build.

## Why players like Standard

Standard has a few strengths that keep it popular:

- **A smaller card pool is easier to learn.** You'll see many of the same cards across games, so after a few weeks you'll recognize most of the important ones and know what your opponent's deck can do.
- **It's always changing.** Every new set shifts what's good. Players who enjoy figuring out a fresh format, or brewing new decks before anyone else, love that cycle.
- **It's where new cards debut.** Most new expansion cards are designed with Standard in mind, so it's the format where you'll get to play with the newest mechanics most directly.
- **It's widely supported.** Standard is available on MTG Arena and is a regular option at local game stores.
- **Power level is lower than older formats.** Without decades of the strongest cards ever printed, games tend to be more interactive and less dominated by turn-two combos. That makes Standard a good place to learn Constructed fundamentals.

### The trade-offs

The biggest downside is that your collection has a shelf life in Standard. Cards eventually rotate, and a player who only plays Standard needs to keep acquiring new cards. Some players are fine with that; others prefer to put their money into a non-rotating format. It also means that deck prices can swing as cards rise and fall in popularity, so be careful about buying expensive cards right before they rotate.

## How to get started

### 1. Try it digitally first

MTG Arena supports Standard and is the lowest-commitment way to see what the format feels like. You can try different archetypes, learn the popular cards and find out whether you prefer aggro, control or something in between before buying any paper cards.

### 2. Start with a preconstructed deck

Wizards has regularly released ready-to-play decks built from Standard-legal cards. They aren't tuned for competitive play, but they're legal, coherent, and a sensible base to improve over time. Upgrading a precon one card at a time teaches you more than copying a finished list.

### 3. Pick an archetype you'll enjoy

Every Standard format has some mix of aggressive, controlling, midrange and combo decks. A focused one- or two-color aggro deck is usually the cheapest and easiest to play well. Control decks reward patient players who enjoy long games. If you're not sure what those labels mean, the archetypes overview linked above explains them.

### 4. Build with the rotation in mind

If you're buying paper cards, favor cards from the newer sets in the pool, since they'll stay legal longer. Cards from the oldest sets are the first to rotate. Our [budget decks guide](/blog/budget-mtg-decks-under-50) has more ways to save money, including swapping expensive cards for cheaper ones that do the same job.

### 5. Go to a local event

Many game stores run regular casual events, including Friday Night Magic, and Standard is a common format for them. Bring a legal decklist, expect friendly players, and treat your first few events as practice. Learning your deck matters more than winning on day one.

### 6. Plan your sideboard

Standard is played best-of-three at most store events, which means you'll have a 15-card sideboard to adjust between games. A sideboard is where you put cards that are great against specific decks but weak in general. Read [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) before your first event so you know what to bring in and take out.

## Standard vs other 60-card formats

Standard shares deck-building rules with Pioneer and Modern, but the experience is different. Pioneer and Modern are non-rotating, so their pools are much larger and their strongest decks tend to be more powerful. Standard sits at the entry point of competitive Constructed: the newest cards, the smallest pool and the lowest power level. Many players start in Standard and later carry their favorite cards into a non-rotating format.

## The takeaway

Standard is the format of the newest Magic cards: a small, changing pool with simple 60-card rules. Check the official format page for which sets are legal, try decks on Arena before buying, start from a precon or a focused aggro deck, and buy with rotation in mind. Then build something you'll enjoy playing for a while.
