In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), sequencing is the order you make your plays in within a turn and across turns. Two players with the same hand can get very different results depending on which land they play first, whether they cast a creature before or after combat, and which spell they lead with. The general rule is to make the play that gives you the most information before you commit, and gives your opponent the least.

This guide assumes you know the basics of the turn, and covers land drops, pre- and post-combat plays, fetchlands, hiding information, and the sequencing errors that cost the most games. If you need a refresher on the phases themselves, see the [turn structure guide](/blog/mtg-turn-structure-phases).

## Why sequencing matters

Most mistakes in Magic aren't about which cards you put in your deck. They're about small ordering decisions that compound: the tapped land played on the wrong turn, the creature cast before combat that gave away your hand, the cantrip cast after the land drop instead of before. Each one costs a little, and over a game they add up to a loss.

Good sequencing comes down to a few questions you can ask every turn:

1. **What do I need to know before I decide?** Draw spells and scry effects can change the right play, so cast them first.
2. **What does my opponent learn from this play?** Every card you reveal narrows down what else you might have.
3. **What mana do I need, this turn and next?** Land drops and tapped lands are planned a turn or two ahead.
4. **What's the order that can't go wrong?** If one sequence works no matter what happens and another depends on luck, take the safe one.

## Land drops

### Play your land first, usually

The default is to play your land for the turn early in your first main phase. It's easy to forget a land drop when you're focused on combat, and missing one is a pure loss. Playing it early also makes all your mana available for whatever you draw into.

There are good reasons to hold the land, though:

- **You have a card that tells you which land to play.** If you have a one-mana cantrip like Opt and enough mana to cast it without the land, cast it first. What you draw might change which land you want.
- **You have a landfall payoff.** Cards that trigger whenever a land enters under your control want to be on the battlefield before the land drop. Cast the payoff, then play the land.
- **You want to keep cards in hand.** A player holding two cards might have a trick. A player holding one card that's obviously a land doesn't. Playing your land after combat can keep your opponent guessing during blocks.

### Plan tapped lands ahead

Lands that enter tapped are best played on turns when you won't use all your mana anyway. If your deck has no one-drop in hand, turn one is the perfect time for a tapped land. If you're holding a two-drop and a three-drop, play the tapped land on turn one or on a turn when you were going to float extra mana, not on the turn it delays your curve.

A useful habit: at the start of each turn, look at your hand and plan your mana for the next two turns. That's when you decide which land comes down now and which one waits. A smooth [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) only pays off if your lands arrive in the right order.

### Play the land that keeps options open

When you have several lands, play the one that leaves the most flexibility for later turns, and think about what it reveals. A basic Island tells your opponent less than a dual land that shows both of your colors. In the early turns, keeping your second color hidden can make your opponent misplay around the wrong threats.

## Fetchlands and shuffles

Fetchlands like Polluted Delta let you tap, pay 1 life and sacrifice the land to search your library for a land with the right type, put it onto the battlefield, and shuffle. They raise a few sequencing questions.

- **When to crack it.** If you don't need the mana this turn, you can wait. Cracking at the end of your opponent's turn hides which land you're getting until the last moment. But if a landfall card, a graveyard count or a shuffle matters this turn, crack it right away.
- **Brainstorm, then shuffle.** Brainstorm puts two cards from your hand back on top of your library. Cracking a fetchland afterward shuffles them away, so you trade unwanted cards for fresh draws. Leaving a fetchland uncracked until after a Brainstorm is a classic piece of sequencing.
- **Don't scry, then shuffle.** The opposite is true for scry and other "look at the top" effects. If you fetch after setting up the top of your library, you shuffle your setup away. Crack the fetchland first, then scry.
- **Know what's left in your library.** Fetching the last basic of a type, or a land you'll need later, can cut you off a color. Count before you search.

## Before combat or after?

The most common sequencing question in a turn is whether to cast spells in your first main phase or your second.

### The default: cast after combat

Unless a spell affects combat, cast it after. Holding spells through combat does two things:

- **It hides information.** Your opponent has to block without knowing what you're holding. Untapped mana and cards in hand represent combat tricks, and that threat alone can stop good blocks.
- **It lets you react.** After combat you know what died and what survived, so you can pick the best spell for the new board.

### When to cast before combat

There are clear exceptions:

- **Creatures with haste** need to come down first to attack.
- **Cards that change combat,** like an anthem that pumps your team, a creature that grants abilities, or Equipment you plan to attach, should be played first.
- **Removal for a blocker.** If a sorcery-speed removal spell lets your attackers through, use it before combat.
- **Draw and selection spells,** because what you draw might change your attack or give you a trick.
- **Cards that trigger on attacking or need to be on the battlefield first.** Read your cards for anything that cares about creatures attacking.

Combat itself has its own sequencing, like when to cast tricks and when to hold them. The [combat tricks guide](/blog/combat-tricks-guide) and the [attacking guide](/blog/when-to-attack-mtg) go deep on that side.

## Sequencing spells within a turn

When you cast more than one spell in a turn, order matters.

- **Information first.** Cantrips, scry and card draw go before anything that depends on what you'll draw.
- **Discard first.** A discard spell like Thoughtseize, cast before your other plays, shows you the opponent's hand. You can then pick the safest threat to cast into what they have.
- **Bait before the real threat.** Against a deck with counterspells, lead with the spell you care about less. If they counter it, the important one resolves. If they don't, you've still developed. Against removal, the same logic applies: make them use their answer on the less important target.
- **Payoffs before the cards that trigger them.** A creature that triggers whenever you cast a spell should come down before the spells.
- **Tap the right sources.** Pay with mana that can't be used for anything else first, and keep the colors open that let you cast your instants. If you have a mana creature and a land and only need one mana, tapping the land leaves the creature untapped and able to block.

## Hiding information

Your opponent is always trying to figure out your hand. Each choice you make gives them a clue, so make the choices that give away the least.

- **Represent tricks.** Leaving mana open and cards in hand during combat makes your opponent respect tricks you might not have.
- **Don't reveal colors early.** If you don't need a dual land yet, play the basic that shows less.
- **Use instants at the last moment.** Casting a draw spell or removal at the end of your opponent's turn gives them the least time to react and keeps them guessing during their own turn.
- **Act consistently.** If you always pause before blocks only when you have a trick, a good opponent will read it. Take the same time and make the same motions whether or not you have something.
- **Don't announce your plans.** Tapping a specific set of lands, or picking up a card early, tells your opponent things too.

## Common sequencing errors

- **Playing a land, then casting a cantrip.** If the cantrip could have told you which land to play, you lost information.
- **Casting a creature before combat for no reason.** It reveals a card, taps your mana and removes the threat of tricks.
- **Using sorcery-speed removal after combat.** If the blocker was the problem, it needed to go before attacks.
- **Playing a tapped land on the wrong turn.** Look ahead and play it when the mana wasn't needed.
- **Fetching after scrying.** Shuffle first, then set up the top of your library.
- **Casting payoffs after their triggers.** Get the engine down before the fuel.
- **Tapping the wrong lands.** Leave up the colors you actually need for instants.
- **Overextending into a board wipe.** Against control, sequencing across turns matters too. Commit enough threats to pressure them, and hold the rest.

## The takeaway

Before every turn, decide what you need to learn, what your opponent can learn, and what mana you need this turn and next. Default to playing lands early and spells after combat, then break those defaults only when you have a reason. A few seconds of planning each turn will win you more games than most card swaps.
