A combo deck wins by putting together a specific set of cards whose interaction ends the game, usually in a single turn, regardless of what the opponent has on the battlefield. Most of the deck isn't the combo itself. It's the cards that find the pieces, protect them, and keep you alive until you're ready.

This guide goes deeper than the overview in [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained). We'll cover the different kinds of combos, how combo decks are built, how to play one well, and the classic examples that show the shapes combos take.

## What makes a deck "combo"

Every deck has synergies. An elf deck wants lots of elves, and a burn deck wants its spells to point at the opponent's face. What separates combo from the rest is how much the deck depends on one specific interaction.

A useful test: **if you removed the combo, would the deck still have a plan?** A midrange deck that happens to contain a two-card combo still wins plenty of games by attacking. A dedicated combo deck without its combo is a pile of card draw and lands. Most real decks sit somewhere on that spectrum, and where your deck sits changes how you should play it.

Combo decks usually share a few traits:

- **Lots of card selection.** Cheap spells that look at or draw cards, so the deck sees more of itself each turn.
- **Few traditional threats.** Creatures or spells that win through damage or board presence are often a backup plan, if they exist at all.
- **Little interaction with the opponent.** The deck is mostly busy with itself. Any interaction it plays is often there to protect the combo rather than to stop the opponent's plan.
- **A specific "go" turn.** The deck aims to win on a particular turn, and everything before it is setup.

## Deterministic combos vs value combos

Combos fall into two broad groups, and they play quite differently.

### Deterministic combos

A deterministic combo wins the game, or locks it, once the pieces are together and it isn't disrupted. The result doesn't depend on luck. Many of these are infinite loops.

Some long-standing examples:

- **Kiki-Jiki, Mirror Breaker with Pestermite.** Kiki-Jiki can tap to create a token copy of a nonlegendary creature you control, with haste. The copy of Pestermite enters and untaps Kiki-Jiki, which makes another copy, which untaps Kiki-Jiki again. The result is as many hasty Pestermites as you want in a single turn. Deceiver Exarch and Zealous Conscripts fill the same role.
- **Splinter Twin with Deceiver Exarch.** The same loop in a different package: the enchantment gives the creature the copy ability, and each copy untaps the original.
- **Sanguine Bond with Exquisite Blood.** Whenever you gain life, the opponent loses that much; whenever an opponent loses life, you gain that much. Once both are out, any life gain or life loss starts a loop that drains the opponent to zero.
- **Painter's Servant with Grindstone.** Painter's Servant makes every card the same color, so Grindstone's "repeat if they share a color" clause never stops. One activation mills the opponent's entire library.
- **Isochron Scepter with Dramatic Reversal.** With enough mana rocks out to produce at least three mana, copying Dramatic Reversal untaps them all for a net gain each time, giving you as much mana as you want. This is a well-known Commander engine.

### Value combos and engines

A value combo doesn't win on the spot. It produces a large, repeating advantage that snowballs. Think of a sacrifice outlet combined with creatures that drain the opponent when they die, or a card that rewards you for casting spells combined with many cheap spells.

Storm decks sit between the two. Grapeshot deals 1 damage and gets copied for every spell cast before it that turn, so a deck that chains rituals and card draw into one big turn can win with it. It isn't a two-card combo, but the deck is still built around one explosive turn.

Value engines are more forgiving because you can win without assembling every piece, but they are also easier to beat with ordinary removal and pressure.

## How combo decks find their pieces

If your deck needs two specific cards, you need a plan to find them reliably. Combo decks use three tools.

### Redundancy

The simplest answer is to play more copies of each role. If Pestermite, Deceiver Exarch and Zealous Conscripts all go infinite with Kiki-Jiki, Mirror Breaker, you have many more ways to complete the combo than you would with a single partner card. Redundancy also makes the deck harder to disrupt, since removing one piece doesn't remove the whole plan.

### Card selection

Cheap spells that dig through the deck increase how many cards you see. Brainstorm, Ponder, Preordain and Serum Visions are classic examples. Each one costs a single mana and lets you see two or three cards to pick the one you need. Over several turns, a deck full of these sees far more of itself than a deck that only draws one card a turn.

### Tutors

A tutor searches your library for a card and puts it somewhere useful, usually your hand or the top of your library. Demonic Tutor finds any card. More restricted tutors, like Enlightened Tutor (artifact or enchantment) or Mystical Tutor (instant or sorcery), are cheaper or faster but narrower. Tutors effectively turn every copy into an extra copy of your most important piece.

In 60-card formats, where you can play four copies of each card, redundancy and card selection usually matter most. In singleton formats like Commander, where you get one copy of each card, tutors carry more of the load.

## Protecting the combo

Opponents will try to stop you. A combo deck needs a plan for that, and there are a few approaches.

- **Counter their answer.** Holding a counterspell when you try to go off lets you stop the removal or counter aimed at your combo.
- **Clear the way first.** Discard like Duress or Thoughtseize takes the opponent's answer before your combo turn and shows you what they're holding.
- **Stop them from responding.** Silence stops your opponents from casting spells for the rest of the turn. Resolve it first on the turn you go off, and they can't cast instant-speed answers to your combo.
- **Wait for the window.** If the opponent taps out on their own turn, they often can't answer you on yours. Patient combo players go off when the opponent's mana is down.
- **Present a backup plan.** Many combo pieces are reasonable cards on their own. A Pestermite can still attack. Combo decks that can win a different way force opponents to respect both plans.

Choosing between "go now" and "wait for protection" is the hardest skill in playing combo. Going off into open mana can lose to one counterspell. Waiting too long gives the opponent time to pressure you or find more answers.

## How to play a combo deck well

**Know your deck's goldfish speed.** Playing alone against no opponent ("goldfishing") shows you which turn your deck typically wins. That number tells you how much time you have and whether you should race or wait.

**Mulligan for function.** A combo deck's hand needs mana, card selection and a realistic path to the pieces. A hand with one combo piece and no way to find the other may be weaker than it looks. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) explains the general process, and combo decks often apply it aggressively.

**Sequence to hide information.** Play your setup cards in an order that reveals as little as possible. An opponent who doesn't know you're a combo deck is less likely to hold up the right answer.

**Practice the loop.** In tournament play, you need to demonstrate a loop and then shortcut it by announcing how many times you repeat it. If a loop is optional, you pick a number; you can't just say "infinite." Know exactly how your combo works so you can explain it cleanly.

**Know what beats you.** Learn the cards that shut your deck down so you recognize them and can sideboard around them.

## How opponents beat combo

Combo decks are vulnerable to well-timed interaction, cards that stop a key part of the engine, and fast pressure that forces them to go off before they're ready. Graveyard hate, discard, counterspells and cards that limit how many spells a player can cast each turn are all common answers.

That's a whole guide of its own: [how to beat combo decks](/blog/how-to-play-against-combo) covers identifying the combo, timing your disruption and sideboarding for the matchup.

## The takeaway

A combo deck is a plan built around one interaction, supported by redundancy, card selection and tutors to find it, and by discard, counters or patience to protect it. Deterministic combos win on the spot; value engines win by snowballing. To play combo well, learn your deck's speed, mulligan for hands that can actually assemble the pieces, and choose your moment to go off.
