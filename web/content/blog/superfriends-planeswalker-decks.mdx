In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), a superfriends deck is built around playing several planeswalkers and keeping them alive. Instead of relying on creatures, it uses planeswalkers as its main source of card advantage, removal and threats, then protects them with board wipes, blockers and effects that discourage attacks. Proliferate and other loyalty-boosting cards push the planeswalkers to their ultimate abilities faster. The name comes from the idea of assembling a team of planeswalker heroes on one battlefield.

This guide covers why the archetype works, how to protect a board of planeswalkers, how proliferate fits in, and how to build superfriends in Commander. If you're new to loyalty abilities and attacking planeswalkers, read [how planeswalkers work](/blog/mtg-planeswalkers-explained) first.

## Why build around planeswalkers

A planeswalker is a repeatable source of value. Each turn, it uses one loyalty ability, which might draw a card, create a creature token or remove a threat. Unlike a sorcery, it does that again next turn, and the turn after. A single planeswalker left unchecked for several turns generates more value than almost any other card.

Superfriends multiplies that. With three planeswalkers on the battlefield, you get three activations every turn, and your opponent has to deal with all of them. Each one demands an answer, so the deck creates a problem the opponent may not have enough resources to solve.

The archetype has three strengths:

- **Resilience to creature removal.** Removal spells that only hit creatures, and board wipes like Wrath of God, don't touch planeswalkers. Your opponent's answers may be dead cards.
- **Steady card advantage.** Many planeswalkers draw cards, make tokens, or both. That [stream of value](/blog/card-advantage-explained) wins long games.
- **A built-in finish.** Most planeswalkers have an ultimate, a powerful ability with a large loyalty cost. Getting one or two of those to resolve often ends the game outright.

## Choosing planeswalkers

Not every planeswalker suits a superfriends deck. Look for ones that do one of these jobs well:

- **Protect themselves.** Planeswalkers that create creature tokens or remove creatures can defend themselves the turn they come down. Elspeth, Sun's Champion makes three 1/1 Soldier tokens with its plus ability, and its minus ability destroys every creature with power 4 or greater. Gideon, Ally of Zendikar can make a 2/2 Knight Ally token every turn.
- **Generate card advantage.** Teferi, Hero of Dominaria draws a card with its plus ability and untaps two lands at the next end step, so you can play it and still hold up mana.
- **Control the opponent.** Liliana of the Veil makes each player discard with its plus ability and forces a sacrifice with its minus ability. Jace, the Mind Sculptor can return a creature to its owner's hand or manipulate the top of a library.

A planeswalker that starts with high loyalty and can use a plus ability immediately is the safest kind to deploy. Ones that need to minus immediately to have an effect are less reliable as part of a superfriends board, because they're left at low loyalty.

### The legend rule

Planeswalkers are legendary, and the legend rule applies by name. You can't keep two copies of Teferi, Hero of Dominaria, but you can have two different Teferi planeswalkers at once, since their names are different. In a singleton format like Commander, this rarely matters. In 60-card decks, it means a second copy of the same planeswalker can be stuck in your hand while the first is on the battlefield, which is a reason to mix different planeswalkers.

## Protecting your planeswalkers

A planeswalker without protection is a target. Your opponent can attack it with creatures, and burn spells can target it directly. The superfriends deck's whole plan depends on keeping planeswalkers alive, so protection takes up a large share of the deck.

### Board wipes

Board wipes are the best friend of a planeswalker deck. Wrath of God and similar effects destroy all creatures, and planeswalkers aren't creatures, so they're untouched. A common sequence: wipe the board, then play a planeswalker on the empty battlefield, and it starts ticking up with nothing able to attack it.

Because superfriends decks usually don't rely on creatures of their own, board wipes are almost free for them. This is why the archetype tends to look like a [control deck](/blog/mtg-control-deck-guide) with planeswalkers as its threats.

### Blockers and tokens

Token-making planeswalkers act as their own defense, and a few cheap creatures with high toughness can protect the whole team. In multiplayer, tokens are especially useful, since they block attacks from several opponents.

### Attack taxes and fogs

Ghostly Prison and Propaganda make opponents pay extra mana for each creature that attacks you. Read the current Oracle text of any tax effect to see whether it also covers your planeswalkers, but even when it doesn't, taxing every attack slows creature decks enough for your planeswalkers to take over. Fog effects buy a turn when a big attack is coming.

### Spot removal

Keep removal for the creatures that threaten your planeswalkers, especially fliers and hasty attackers. Damage dealt to a planeswalker removes that much loyalty, so a single evasive creature can pick them off one by one.

## Proliferate and loyalty boosters

Proliferate is a keyword action: you choose any number of permanents and players that already have counters on them, and give each one more counter of each kind it already has. For planeswalkers, that means one extra loyalty counter on each one you choose.

With several planeswalkers out, a single proliferate effect adds loyalty to all of them at once. That pushes them closer to their ultimates and makes them harder to attack down. The [counters guide](/blog/mtg-counters-explained) covers how proliferate works on other counters too, including poison and +1/+1 counters.

Good proliferate sources for superfriends include:

- **Atraxa, Praetors' Voice:** proliferates at the beginning of your end step, every turn.
- **Contagion Clasp:** an artifact that proliferates for four mana and a tap, and puts a -1/-1 counter on a creature when it enters.
- **Inexorable Tide:** proliferates whenever you cast a spell.
- **Flux Channeler:** proliferates whenever you cast a noncreature spell, which is most of a superfriends deck.
- **Karn's Bastion:** a land that proliferates for four mana and a tap.

Other cards boost loyalty directly. Oath of Gideon makes your planeswalkers enter with an additional loyalty counter. Doubling Season doubles the counters that effects put on your permanents, so planeswalkers enter with twice their printed loyalty, often enough to use an ultimate right away. Deepglow Skate doubles the counters already on any number of permanents when it enters.

### Extra activations

Normally you can activate one loyalty ability per planeswalker per turn. Oath of Teferi lets you activate the loyalty abilities of your planeswalkers twice each turn instead of once. With a board of planeswalkers, that doubles your output.

## Superfriends in Commander

Commander is where superfriends shines. Games are long, which gives planeswalkers time to accumulate value, and the singleton deck has room for many different planeswalkers.

### Commander choices

Two common approaches:

- **A proliferate commander.** Atraxa, Praetors' Voice is the classic superfriends commander. It's four colors, giving access to most planeswalkers, and it proliferates every turn on its own. As a 4/4 flier with vigilance, deathtouch and lifelink, it also defends your planeswalkers well.
- **A planeswalker commander.** A small number of planeswalkers say they can be your commander, such as Teferi, Temporal Archmage and Freyalise, Llanowar's Fury. Using one gives you a planeswalker every game, though in a three-color or four-color deck the commander's colors limit your options.

### Threat assessment and the table

In multiplayer, a board of planeswalkers draws attention. Every opponent can attack your planeswalkers, and if the table sees you as the biggest threat, they'll team up. The [threat assessment guide](/blog/commander-threat-assessment) covers how players decide who to target, and that cuts both ways for you.

A few practical tips:

- **Don't deploy every planeswalker at once.** Two well-protected planeswalkers are better than five unprotected ones.
- **Use plus abilities early.** High loyalty makes planeswalkers less attractive targets.
- **Offer removal to the table.** A planeswalker that removes another player's threat makes you useful to the table, not just a target.
- **Hold a board wipe for the moment several opponents commit creatures at your planeswalkers.**

## Weaknesses to plan for

- **Fast aggression.** An aggressive opponent can attack your planeswalkers down before they generate value. Cheap interaction and early blockers are essential.
- **Planeswalker removal.** Hero's Downfall destroys a creature or planeswalker, and other cards hit any permanent. You can't stop all of them, which is why redundancy matters.
- **Running out of protection.** A deck full of planeswalkers and nothing else loses to creature swarms. Keep the protection count high.

## The takeaway

Superfriends wins by putting several planeswalkers on the battlefield, protecting them with board wipes, blockers and attack taxes, and using proliferate to push them toward their ultimates. Choose planeswalkers that protect themselves or generate card advantage, keep removal for the creatures that threaten them, and in Commander, build around a proliferate engine like Atraxa, Praetors' Voice. Planeswalkers only win the game if they survive.
