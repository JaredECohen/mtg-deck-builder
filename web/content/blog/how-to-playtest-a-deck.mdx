To playtest a Magic: The Gathering (MTG) deck, start by goldfishing (playing it alone against an imaginary opponent) to check that it does what it's supposed to, then draw dozens of sample hands to test your mana and mulligans, and finally play real games against the decks you expect to face. Write down what happens, and change only one or two things at a time so you can tell which change made the difference.

Most players skip straight to the last step, play a few games, and change half the deck based on how they felt. This guide lays out a more reliable process.

## Why playtesting matters

A decklist on paper always looks better than it plays. Testing reveals the problems a list hides:

- **Mana trouble.** Too few lands, the wrong colors, or spells that cost too much for your curve.
- **Clunky draws.** Cards that are strong alone but awkward together, or too many expensive cards in one hand.
- **Missing pieces.** A plan that needs three specific cards at once and rarely gets them.
- **Bad matchups.** Strategies your deck just can't beat without changes.

The goal of testing isn't to win games. It's to learn things about your deck you couldn't see on paper. If you don't have a list yet, the [Deck Lab builder](/) can draft a starting version for you to test.

## Step 1: Goldfishing

Goldfishing means playing your deck against no opponent. You shuffle, draw an opening hand, and play turns as if nothing will interfere. The name comes from the idea of playing against a goldfish that never does anything.

Goldfishing answers basic questions quickly:

- **How fast does the deck win?** Count the turn you would have dealt lethal damage, or assembled your combo, if nothing stopped you. Over a few games, you'll learn your average "kill turn."
- **Does the curve work?** You should usually have something to do with your mana each turn. If you're often stuck with expensive cards early and nothing to do late, your curve needs work. The [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) explains how to shape it.
- **Do the cards work together?** Goldfishing shows you whether your synergies show up often enough to matter.

### Goldfishing tips

- **Play by the real rules.** Draw on the right turns, follow the land-per-turn rule, and track summoning sickness.
- **Make real mulligan decisions.** Don't keep hands you wouldn't keep in a real game.
- **Play enough games.** Five games tell you little. Fifteen or twenty start to show patterns.
- **Note the turn you'd win.** This number is the backbone of later decisions. If your aggro deck averages a late kill turn while goldfishing, it will be slower against a real opponent.

Goldfishing has limits. It's best for aggressive and combo decks, which mostly try to execute a plan. Control and midrange decks depend on interacting with an opponent, so goldfishing only tells you whether their mana and curve work.

## Step 2: Sample hands

Drawing sample hands is the fastest kind of testing. You shuffle, draw seven cards, decide whether you'd keep or mulligan, and then shuffle again. You don't play the game out.

Draw at least 20 to 30 hands. For each one, ask:

- **Would I keep this?** If you find yourself mulliganing often, your land count or curve may be off.
- **How many lands are there?** Too many hands with one land or five lands is a warning sign.
- **Can I cast my spells?** If you have the right number of lands but the wrong colors, your mana base needs adjusting.
- **Does it have a plan?** A keepable hand usually has a way to use mana in the first few turns.

For help deciding which hands to keep, the [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) covers keep-or-mull logic in detail.

### Draw a few turns

After deciding on a keep, draw the next two or three cards. That shows you whether a borderline hand tends to recover or fall apart, which is useful information when you're deciding whether to keep hands like that in the future.

## Step 3: Testing matchups

Once the deck works on its own, test it against real opponents. This is where you learn the most, and where you need to be most careful.

### Pick the right opponents

Test against the decks you actually expect to face. For a tournament, that's the decks you see most often in your local playgroup or event. For casual play, it's your friends' decks. Testing against a random pile of cards teaches you less.

It helps to test against at least one deck from each major style:

- **Aggro**, to see if you can survive the early turns. The [guide to playing against aggro](/blog/how-to-play-against-aggro) covers what to look for.
- **Control**, to see if your threats can get past counterspells and removal.
- **Combo**, to see if you can interact fast enough or race them.

### Play enough games

A handful of games can't tell you much about a matchup. Variance, like drawing too many lands or not enough, swamps everything else. Play at least five to ten games in each matchup before you draw any conclusions, and more if the results are close.

### Play both sides seriously

If a friend is piloting the opposing deck, make sure they're playing it well. Testing against an opponent who makes mistakes inflates your win rate. If you're playing both decks yourself, play each side as if you wanted it to win.

### Test with the sideboard

If your format uses sideboards, test post-sideboard games too. The matchup after sideboarding can look very different from game one. The [sideboarding guide](/blog/sideboarding-101) explains how to plan which cards come in and out.

## Step 4: Tracking results

Human memory is unreliable. A single dramatic loss sticks with you more than three quiet wins. Writing results down keeps you honest.

A simple log can be a spreadsheet or a notebook. For each game, record:

| What to record | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Opponent's deck | Separates results by matchup |
| Win or loss | The basic result |
| On the play or draw | Going first changes many matchups |
| Mulligans | Shows if your mana or curve is causing problems |
| Why you won or lost | The most important part: "flooded," "couldn't answer a flyer," "combo went off turn four" |
| Cards that felt strong or weak | Points you toward changes |

The "why" column is where you learn. Over time, patterns appear. If many losses say "stuck on two lands," your mana needs work. If many say "couldn't beat a big creature," you need more removal.

### Be careful with small numbers

Ten games won't give you a precise win rate. Treat results as rough signals. A matchup where you win eight out of ten is probably good. A matchup where you win five out of ten could be anything. Look for consistent patterns in the reasons you win and lose, not just the final numbers.

## Step 5: Change one thing at a time

When you find a problem, it's tempting to swap out six cards at once. Don't. If you change many things and the deck improves, you won't know which change helped, and if it gets worse, you won't know which change hurt.

Instead:

1. **Identify one problem.** "I lose to fast aggro because I can't block early."
2. **Make one small change.** Swap two expensive cards for two cheaper creatures.
3. **Test again.** Play another set of games against the same matchup.
4. **Keep or revert.** If the problem improved without causing a new one, keep the change. If not, go back and try something else.

This takes longer than rebuilding the deck all at once, but it actually teaches you what each card does for your deck.

### Watch for side effects

Every change has a cost. Adding more removal means cutting threats. Adding more lands means cutting spells. After each change, check that you didn't fix one matchup by breaking another.

## Common playtesting mistakes

- **Testing with the cards you want to be good.** Be honest when a pet card underperforms.
- **Only testing against easy opponents.** Losing in testing is better than losing in a tournament.
- **Ignoring your mulligans.** Frequent mulligans are a deck problem, not bad luck.
- **Drawing conclusions from three games.** Variance is huge in small samples.
- **Changing too much at once.** You'll never learn what's working.

## The takeaway

Playtest in stages: goldfish to check your speed and curve, draw sample hands to check your mana and mulligans, then play enough games against real matchups to see patterns. Write down why you win and lose, change one thing at a time, and retest after every change. A slow, careful process will improve your deck more than a hundred untracked games.
