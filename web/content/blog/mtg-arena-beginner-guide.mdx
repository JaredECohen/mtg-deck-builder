MTG Arena is Wizards of the Coast's free-to-play digital version of Magic: The Gathering. It plays real Magic with the real rules, handles all the bookkeeping for you, and matches you against other players online. To get started you download the client, play through the tutorial, use the decks the game gives you, and then grow your collection by opening packs and spending wildcards on the specific cards you want.

This guide covers what Arena is, how a new account usually starts, the main kinds of games you can play, how deckbuilding works in the client, and how wildcards let you build the decks you actually want. It sticks to the parts of Arena that have stayed stable for years. Event names, reward structures and menus change often, so check the client itself for those details.

## What MTG Arena is

Arena is a digital client for playing Magic, available on computers and mobile devices. Everything you'd do at a kitchen table happens on screen: you draw, play lands, cast spells, attack and block. The difference is that the game engine enforces the rules. It tracks life totals, knows when an ability triggers, puts things on the stack in the right order and stops you from making illegal plays.

That makes Arena one of the easiest ways to learn. You don't need to own cards, find an opponent or remember every rule before you start. If you've never played Magic at all, the [beginner's guide to playing Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) explains the turn, mana and combat in plain language, and everything in it applies on Arena.

A few terms you'll see right away:

- **Collection:** every card your account owns. You build decks only from cards in your collection.
- **Booster pack:** a random bundle of cards you open to add to your collection.
- **Currency:** Arena has an in-game currency you earn by playing and a premium currency you can also buy. Both can be spent on packs and event entries.
- **Wildcard:** a special item that turns into any card of its rarity. More on these below.

## Getting started

### The tutorial

A new account starts with a short guided tutorial. It teaches the basics one at a time: playing a land, casting a creature, attacking, blocking and using spells. It's simple, but don't skip it if you're new. It also teaches you how the client shows you information, like where the stack appears and how you choose targets.

After the tutorial, Arena typically walks new players through a set of introductory games against computer opponents and other new players. These help you try out different colors before you commit to one.

### Starter decks

New accounts receive a handful of ready-to-play decks, each built around a color or color pair. They're designed to be easy to understand rather than strong, but they're fine for your first few dozen games. Play each one a few times. You'll learn which colors feel natural to you, and that tells you where to put your first wildcards.

Each color has its own strengths, so knowing what you enjoy is a useful way to pick a starting direction.

### How Arena handles the game for you

Arena automates a lot. It taps the right lands for a spell when it can, passes priority when you have nothing relevant to do, and orders simple triggers for you. That's convenient, but it can hide what's happening. A few habits help:

- **Watch the stack.** When a spell or ability is waiting to resolve, Arena shows it. That's your chance to respond with an instant.
- **Slow down on key turns.** The client may move past a step quickly if it thinks you have no plays. If you're holding a combat trick or a removal spell, make sure you stop where you want to use it.
- **Read the cards.** Tapping or hovering on a card shows its full text. Do it for every unfamiliar card your opponent plays.

## Game modes on Arena

Arena's menus and event names change over time, but every game falls into one of two big families: Constructed and Limited.

### Constructed

In Constructed, you bring a deck you built ahead of time from your own collection. Arena supports several formats, each with its own list of legal cards. The most common for new players is Standard, which uses cards from recent sets. Because new sets keep arriving, Standard's card pool changes over time.

Constructed decks follow the familiar rules: at least 60 cards, and no more than four copies of any card except basic lands. Arena also offers a Brawl mode, a singleton format built around a legendary commander.

Constructed games come in casual and ranked versions. Casual queues are a low-pressure place to test a deck. Ranked play tracks your results and matches you against players near your level.

### Limited

In Limited, you don't bring a deck. You build one on the spot from cards you open in the event.

- **Draft:** you open packs and pick one card at a time, passing the rest. Arena offers drafts against other players and drafts where the other "drafters" are computer players.
- **Sealed:** you open a set number of packs and build a deck from everything inside.

Limited decks are usually 40 cards, and you keep the cards you draft or open. That makes Limited a way to play and grow your collection at the same time. If you're deciding between the two, the [draft vs sealed guide](/blog/draft-vs-sealed-limited-guide) compares them in detail.

### Best-of-one and best-of-three

Many queues come in two versions:

- **Best-of-one (Bo1):** a single game decides the match. Matches are fast, and there's no sideboard.
- **Best-of-three (Bo3):** the first player to win two games takes the match. Between games you can swap cards in from a sideboard of up to 15 cards.

Best-of-one is quicker and good for short sessions. Best-of-three rewards preparation and is closer to how tabletop tournaments run. Arena has also used an opening-hand system in some best-of-one queues that nudges hands toward a more typical mix of lands and spells. The trade-offs are covered in [best-of-one vs best-of-three](/blog/best-of-one-vs-best-of-three).

## Building decks on Arena

The deck builder shows your collection and lets you filter by color, card type, mana value and format. When you add cards, it keeps a running count and a mana curve chart.

Some principles that carry over from tabletop Magic:

1. **Start with a plan.** Decide whether you're the aggressive deck or the deck that wins late. Every card should help that plan.
2. **Play enough lands.** Around 24 lands in a 60-card deck is a common starting point for decks with higher-cost spells, fewer for low-curve aggro decks. Around 17 is typical for a 40-card Limited deck.
3. **Watch your curve.** Too many expensive cards means doing nothing early. Spread your spells across mana values so you have plays on each turn.
4. **Use four copies of your best cards.** In a 60-card deck, four copies of a key card means you'll see it far more often than one copy.
5. **Stick to one or two colors at first.** Fewer colors means fewer games lost to the wrong lands.

Arena also lets you import and export decklists as text. If you find a list you like, you can paste it in, and the client tells you which cards you're missing. Reading a list well before you copy it is its own skill. You can also sketch a deck idea in the [Deck Lab builder](/) and then assemble it on Arena from the cards you own.

## The wildcard concept

Wildcards are what make Arena's economy work. Instead of trading with other players, you collect wildcards and turn them into the exact cards you need.

### How wildcards work

Each wildcard has a rarity: common, uncommon, rare or mythic rare. You can turn a wildcard into any card of the same rarity. A rare wildcard becomes any rare, a mythic wildcard becomes any mythic, and so on. You can't trade a lower-rarity wildcard for a higher-rarity card.

You mostly earn wildcards by opening booster packs, where they sometimes appear in place of a normal card. Common and uncommon wildcards show up often. Rare and mythic wildcards are much scarcer, and they're the ones that limit what you can build. The [card rarity guide](/blog/mtg-card-rarity-explained) explains what each rarity means and why higher rarity doesn't always mean a stronger card.

### Spending wildcards well

New players often spend their first rare wildcards on whatever card looked cool, then run out before finishing a deck. A better approach:

- **Pick one deck and finish it.** A complete deck you can win with is worth more than four half-built ones.
- **Check what you already own.** Use the client's import feature to see exactly which cards you're missing before crafting anything.
- **Prioritize cards that go in many decks.** Dual lands and flexible removal spells tend to stay useful across several decks in the same format.
- **Be careful near rotation.** In formats like Standard, cards eventually leave. Crafting a card that is about to rotate gives you a short window of use.
- **Don't craft playsets of experimental cards.** Try new ideas with cards you own first.

### Commons and uncommons are cheap

Many strong decks lean heavily on commons and uncommons. Because those wildcards are plentiful, the real cost of a deck on Arena is mostly its rares and mythics. When you compare two decks, count the rares and mythics you're missing. That number tells you how far away each deck is.

## Tips for your first weeks

- **Play the free modes first.** Learn the client and your starter decks before spending currency on events.
- **Try Limited once you know the basics.** Drafting teaches card evaluation faster than almost anything else, and you keep the cards.
- **Don't chase every new deck.** Pick a deck that fits how you like to play and get good with it.
- **Learn from your losses.** Arena makes it easy to queue again immediately. Take a moment after a loss to ask what you'd do differently.

<Callout title="Tip">If you can't follow what your opponent just did, stop and read the card before you respond. Taking a few seconds to understand a play is almost always worth it.</Callout>

## The takeaway

MTG Arena is the easiest on-ramp to Magic: free to start, and the client enforces the rules so you can focus on decisions. Play the tutorial, try every starter deck, then pick one direction. Learn the difference between Constructed and Limited and between best-of-one and best-of-three, and spend your rare and mythic wildcards on one complete deck rather than scattered favorites.
