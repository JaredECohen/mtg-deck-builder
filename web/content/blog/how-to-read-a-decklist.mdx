A Magic: The Gathering (MTG) decklist is a list of every card in a deck with its quantity, usually grouped into sections like creatures, spells and lands, followed by a separate sideboard. Reading one well means going beyond the names: count the lands and the mana curve, figure out how the deck intends to win, and look at the sideboard to see which matchups the builder was worried about. A few minutes with a list can tell you how a deck plays before you've shuffled it once.

This guide covers the standard layout of a list, then walks through a practice list to show how to count the curve, identify the plan and read the sideboard. It finishes with how to learn from lists you find, rather than just copying them.

## The structure of a decklist

Most decklists follow the same basic format.

- **Each line is a quantity and a card name.** "4 Lightning Bolt" means four copies of Lightning Bolt.
- **The main deck comes first.** In 60-card Constructed formats, it has at least 60 cards.
- **Cards are often grouped by type.** Creatures, instants, sorceries, artifacts, enchantments, planeswalkers and lands, sometimes with a count for each group.
- **The sideboard comes last.** In most Constructed formats it holds up to 15 cards that can be swapped in between games.

Some lists don't group by type and just put everything in one block, often the way a digital client exports them. The information is the same, but you'll want to sort it in your head before you start analyzing.

### Commander lists

Commander lists look a bit different. The commander is usually listed first, on its own, followed by 99 other cards. Because Commander is a singleton format, almost every line is a quantity of one. The quantity worth counting is how many cards fill each role: ramp, card draw, removal, board wipes and so on. Commander games also usually have no sideboard.

## A practice list

Here's a made-up, mono-red aggressive list. It's meant to practice reading, not to recommend a deck for any particular format or event.

```
Creatures (12)
4 Goblin Guide
4 Monastery Swiftspear
4 Eidolon of the Great Revel

Instants and Sorceries (28)
4 Lightning Bolt
4 Chain Lightning
4 Lava Spike
4 Rift Bolt
4 Skewer the Critics
4 Light Up the Stage
4 Fireblast

Lands (20)
20 Mountain

Sideboard (15)
3 Smash to Smithereens
2 Pyroclasm
4 Searing Blood
3 Exquisite Firecraft
3 Pyroblast
```

We'll use this list for the rest of the guide.

## Step 1: Count the lands

Start with the land count, because it tells you a lot right away. This list has 20 lands in 60 cards, a third of the deck. That's low compared to decks that play expensive spells, and it signals a deck that wants to do everything with one to three lands on the battlefield.

As a rough guide:

- **Around 16 to 20 lands** in 60 cards points toward an aggressive, low-cost deck.
- **Around 22 to 24 lands** is common for midrange decks.
- **25 or more lands** often means a control or ramp deck that wants to reach the late game.

Also look at what kind of lands they are. A deck with all basics is focused on one color. A deck with many dual lands is managing multiple colors, and the mix tells you which color is the main one.

## Step 2: Count the curve

The mana curve is the number of spells at each mana value. Counting it shows when the deck wants to act. The [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) explains the concept in depth. Here's how to count it from a list.

For the practice list:

| Mana value | Cards |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 20 (Goblin Guide, Monastery Swiftspear, Lightning Bolt, Chain Lightning, Lava Spike) |
| 2 | 4 (Eidolon of the Great Revel) |
| 3 | 12 (Rift Bolt, Skewer the Critics, Light Up the Stage) |
| 6 | 4 (Fireblast) |

That looks like a deck with a strange gap and some expensive cards. But read the cards and the picture changes:

- **Rift Bolt** has suspend, so you can pay one red mana to cast it a turn later.
- **Skewer the Critics** and **Light Up the Stage** have spectacle, which lets you pay one red mana instead of their normal cost if an opponent lost life this turn.
- **Fireblast** lets you sacrifice two Mountains instead of paying its mana cost.

So the real curve is almost entirely one-mana plays. That's the first big lesson in reading lists: **mana value and the cost you actually pay are not always the same.** Count the curve, then adjust it for alternative costs, cost reductions and cards that are usually cast late.

## Step 3: Spot the plan

Once you know the land count and the curve, ask how the deck wins.

### Look at the threats

Which cards actually win the game? In the practice list, the creatures are cheap, aggressive bodies. Most of the damage comes from burn spells that hit the opponent directly. The plan is to deal 20 damage as fast as possible, mostly without combat.

### Look at the interaction

What does the deck do about the opponent's cards? Here, the burn doubles as removal, since several spells can hit creatures too. There's no card draw in the traditional sense, but Light Up the Stage helps keep the hand full.

### Look at the patterns

Repeated effects reveal priorities:

- **Seven different burn spells** mean the deck wants damage from any card it draws.
- **Eidolon of the Great Revel** hurts both players whenever they cast cheap spells. The deck accepts that because it plans to win the damage race.
- **No expensive threats** means the deck has no plan for a long game. If it doesn't win early, it's in trouble.

From there you can usually place a deck into a broad archetype like aggro, midrange, control or combo. Our practice list is a classic burn deck.

### Count copies

Quantities tell you how much the builder wants to see each card.

- **Four copies** means a core card the deck wants in most games.
- **Three copies** often means important, but slightly less wanted, or a card that's bad in multiples.
- **One or two copies** usually means a situational card, a legendary card, or a late-game card you only need to draw occasionally.

## Step 4: Read the sideboard

The sideboard tells you what the builder expected to face. Each card is a response to a particular kind of opponent. For the practice list:

- **Smash to Smithereens** destroys an artifact and deals 3 damage to its controller. It's for artifact-heavy opponents.
- **Pyroclasm** deals 2 damage to each creature. It's for decks that go wide with small creatures.
- **Searing Blood** kills a small creature and can deal extra damage to the opponent. It's for other creature decks.
- **Exquisite Firecraft** deals 4 damage and can't be countered once you have enough instants and sorceries in your graveyard. It's mostly for counterspell decks.
- **Pyroblast** answers blue spells and permanents. It's for blue decks.

A sideboard is a map of the matchups the builder worried about. When you see several cards aimed at the same type of opponent, that's a matchup they expected to see often or found difficult. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers how to plan the swaps themselves.

Also notice what's missing. If a sideboard has no answers to a common strategy, the builder either thinks the main deck already handles it or has accepted it as a bad matchup.

## Learning from lists

Reading lists is one of the fastest ways to improve, but only if you do more than copy them.

- **Ask why each card is there.** If you can't explain a card's role, look it up and figure it out.
- **Compare similar lists.** When several versions of the same deck exist, the cards they all share are the core. The cards that differ are the flexible slots, where builders made different choices.
- **Note the unusual choices.** A single copy of an odd card often reflects a specific concern the builder had. Think about what it's for.
- **Test before you trust.** A list that looks good on paper may not fit how you play. Goldfish it and play some games. The [playtesting guide](/blog/how-to-playtest-a-deck) covers how to test a deck efficiently.
- **Adapt for your environment.** A list built for one group of opponents may need changes for yours.

If you're preparing for an event, reading lists also helps you understand what opponents might be playing. The guide to [your first MTG tournament](/blog/mtg-tournament-guide-first-event) covers what to expect when you bring your own list.

## The takeaway

Read a decklist in four passes: count the lands, count the curve and adjust it for alternative costs, identify how the deck wins and what it does about the opponent, then read the sideboard as a map of the matchups the builder cared about.
