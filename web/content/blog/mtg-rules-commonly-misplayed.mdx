Most Magic: The Gathering rules mistakes come from a handful of situations that feel intuitive but work differently under the Comprehensive Rules. A blocked creature stays blocked even if its blocker disappears, tokens stop existing once they leave the battlefield, unused mana vanishes between steps, and summoning sickness never stops a creature from blocking. Get these right and you'll avoid the arguments that stall casual games.

Below are ten rules that experienced players and beginners alike regularly get wrong, each with the correct explanation and an example. They're listed roughly in the order they come up during a turn.

## 1. A blocked creature stays blocked

Once a creature has been blocked, it remains blocked for the rest of combat, even if every creature blocking it leaves the battlefield.

Say your 4/4 attacks and your opponent blocks with a 2/2. You cast a removal spell on the 2/2 before damage. Many players assume the 4/4 now hits the opponent. It doesn't. It's still a blocked creature with nothing to deal damage to, so it deals no combat damage at all.

The exception is **trample**. A blocked trampling creature with no blockers left assigns all its damage to the player or planeswalker it's attacking. For the full order of combat steps, see [MTG combat explained](/blog/mtg-combat-explained).

**The practical lesson:** removing a blocker saves your attacker, but it doesn't push damage through. If you want damage, remove the potential blocker before blockers are declared.

## 2. Tokens that leave the battlefield cease to exist

A token is a permanent created by an effect, not a card. When a token leaves the battlefield, whether it's destroyed, exiled or returned to its owner's hand, it moves to that zone briefly and then ceases to exist the next time the game checks state-based actions.

Two consequences follow:

- **"Dies" triggers still work.** A token creature that's destroyed does go to the graveyard for a moment, so abilities that trigger when a creature dies will trigger.
- **You can't get it back.** A token bounced to your hand can't be recast, and a token that went to the graveyard can't be returned by a reanimation spell. It's gone.

This is also why bounce spells are so good against tokens: returning a token to hand is effectively the same as exiling it.

## 3. Mana empties between steps and phases

Unused mana doesn't last for the whole turn. When a step or phase ends, any mana left in your mana pool empties.

So you can't tap your lands in your first main phase, move to combat and then use that mana to cast an instant during the declare blockers step. The mana disappears as the main phase ends. Tap mana when you're about to spend it.

There's no penalty for losing unused mana. An old rule called mana burn made players lose life for unspent mana, but it was removed from the game many years ago. A few cards change how mana empties, but unless a card on the battlefield says so, assume your pool clears at the end of every step and phase.

## 4. The legend rule only checks your own permanents

The legend rule says that if a player controls two or more legendary permanents with the same name, that player chooses one and puts the rest into their owners' graveyards.

The key word is **controls**. The rule looks at each player separately. If you control a legendary creature and your opponent controls a copy of the same legendary creature, both stay on the battlefield. There's no conflict, because neither of you controls two.

Two more details people miss:

- **It isn't destruction.** The extra copies are put into the graveyard as a state-based action, so indestructible doesn't save them.
- **It only cares about the exact name.** Two different legendary cards that depict the same character but have different names don't interact with each other under this rule.

The [legendary rule guide](/blog/mtg-legendary-rule-explained) covers the history and edge cases.

## 5. Summoning sickness doesn't stop blocking

A creature you haven't controlled continuously since the start of your most recent turn can't attack, and it can't use abilities that have the tap symbol or untap symbol in their cost. That's what players call summoning sickness.

What it **doesn't** stop:

- **Blocking.** A creature you cast on your turn can block on your opponent's next turn, and a creature you flash in during your opponent's turn can block right away.
- **Other abilities.** Activated abilities without the tap or untap symbol in their cost work immediately.
- **Being tapped by other effects.** Summoning sickness only limits what you can choose to do with the creature, not what other cards do to it.

It also applies to any permanent that's a creature, such as a land that becomes a creature, while it hasn't been under your control since your turn began. Haste removes the restriction. [Summoning sickness explained](/blog/summoning-sickness-explained) goes deeper.

## 6. "Dies" means put into a graveyard from the battlefield

In Magic, "dies" has one specific meaning: a creature or planeswalker is put into a graveyard from the battlefield.

It doesn't matter how it gets there. Destruction, lethal damage, sacrifice, the legend rule, zero toughness and zero loyalty all count, as long as the permanent goes from the battlefield to the graveyard.

Things that **don't** count as dying:

- **Exile.** A creature exiled from the battlefield doesn't die, so "when this creature dies" abilities don't trigger.
- **Bouncing.** A creature returned to its owner's hand doesn't die.
- **Discard or mill.** A creature card put into your graveyard from your hand or library never died, because it was never on the battlefield.

This is why exile-based removal is so valuable against creatures with death triggers: it gets around the trigger entirely.

## 7. Damage wears off at end of turn, not right away

Damage dealt to a creature stays marked on it until the cleanup step at the end of the turn. It doesn't heal between combat and the second main phase.

That has real consequences. If your 3/3 takes 2 damage in combat, it's effectively a 3/3 with one toughness remaining for the rest of the turn. A 1-damage effect afterward will finish it.

A related detail: in the cleanup step, damage is removed at the same time that "until end of turn" effects end. If a pump spell gave your 2/2 +2/+2 and it took 3 damage, it survives the turn. The damage and the pump go away together, so it never becomes a 2/2 with 3 damage marked on it.

## 8. Abilities on the stack don't need their source

Once an activated or triggered ability is on the stack, it exists independently of the permanent that created it. Removing the source in response doesn't stop the ability.

For example, if a creature has an ability that deals damage when it's activated, killing that creature in response won't cancel the damage. The ability still resolves. The same goes for triggered abilities: if a creature's enters-the-battlefield trigger is on the stack, destroying the creature doesn't stop the trigger.

What you can do is respond with something that changes the outcome, such as a spell that counters abilities, or making the ability's target illegal. If every target is illegal when the ability tries to resolve, it doesn't resolve. For how responses resolve in order, see [how the stack works](/blog/how-the-stack-works-mtg).

## 9. Hexproof and shroud only stop targeting

Hexproof means a permanent or player can't be the target of spells or abilities your opponents control. Shroud is stricter: it can't be the target of any spells or abilities, including your own.

Neither one protects against effects that don't target. A creature with hexproof is still destroyed by Wrath of God, which destroys all creatures without targeting. It can still be forced into a sacrifice by an effect that makes a player sacrifice a creature, and it still takes damage from an effect that hits each creature.

Read removal carefully: if a spell says "target," hexproof stops your opponent from aiming it at your creature. If it says "each" or "all," hexproof doesn't help.

## 10. Maximum hand size only matters at your own cleanup

Your maximum hand size is normally seven, but it only matters at one point: the cleanup step of your own turn. At that point, if you have more than seven cards in hand, you discard down to seven.

You can hold more than seven cards at any other time. Drawing to nine cards during your opponent's turn is fine, and you won't discard until the end of your next turn, by which point you may have played some of them. Discarding during your opponent's cleanup step is a common mistake, and it gives away cards for no reason.

## The takeaway

Most rules arguments come from a few situations that look simple but follow precise wording. Blocked stays blocked, tokens disappear once they leave the battlefield, mana empties between steps, the legend rule checks each player separately, summoning sickness never stops blocking, and "dies" means battlefield to graveyard. When a situation is unclear, the Comprehensive Rules are the final authority, and the exact wording on the card comes first.
