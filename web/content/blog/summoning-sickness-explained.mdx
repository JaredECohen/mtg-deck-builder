Summoning sickness is the rule that a creature can't attack, and can't use abilities with the tap symbol in their cost, until you've controlled it continuously since the start of your most recent turn. In practice, a creature you cast this turn has to wait until your next turn to attack or tap. It can still block, and it can still use any ability that doesn't require tapping it.

That's the whole rule. The rest of this guide covers the cases that trip players up: mana creatures, haste, stolen creatures, Vehicles, flicker effects and the many things summoning sickness doesn't touch.

## The rule in plain words

"Summoning sickness" is an informal nickname. The Comprehensive Rules don't treat it as a status or a counter you put on a card. Instead, they ask one question whenever a creature tries to attack or tap for an ability: **has this creature been under your control continuously since your most recent turn began?**

- If yes, it can attack and use its tap abilities normally.
- If no, it can't attack, and it can't activate abilities whose cost includes the tap symbol (`{T}`) or the untap symbol (`{Q}`).

Notice what that question doesn't ask. It doesn't ask when the card was cast, whether it's been a creature the whole time, or how long it's been on the battlefield in total. It only cares about **continuous control since the start of your turn**. Almost every tricky case comes down to that wording.

The rule exists for a simple reason: without it, every creature would hit the turn it arrived, and games would swing far too fast. Summoning sickness gives your opponent one turn to see a creature coming and respond to it. If you're still learning how a turn flows, [your first game of Magic](/blog/your-first-game-of-magic) walks through one start to finish.

## What summoning sickness stops

### Attacking

A creature that just entered the battlefield under your control can't be declared as an attacker this turn. You cast Grizzly Bears in your main phase, move to combat, and the Bears stay home. Next turn, once your turn has begun with the Bears already under your control, they can attack.

### Tap abilities, including mana abilities

Any activated ability with `{T}` in its cost is off limits on a creature with summoning sickness. This is the case new players hit most often with mana creatures.

**Llanowar Elves** reads "`{T}`: Add `{G}`." If you cast it on turn one, you can't tap it for mana that turn. On turn two it's been under your control since your turn began, so you can tap it, and that's when it starts accelerating you. The same goes for **Birds of Paradise** and every other creature that taps for mana.

Creatures with tap abilities that deal damage, draw cards or do anything else follow the same rule. **Prodigal Pyromancer**, which taps to deal 1 damage to any target, has to wait a turn before it can ping anything.

## What summoning sickness doesn't stop

This is where most of the confusion lives. Summoning sickness is narrow: it blocks attacking and tap or untap costs, and nothing else.

### Blocking

A creature with summoning sickness **can block**. Blocking doesn't require tapping, and it isn't attacking, so the rule doesn't apply. If you cast a creature on your turn, it's ready to block on your opponent's very next turn. This is one of the most common beginner mistakes in both directions: players forget their new creature can block, or attack into an opponent's fresh creature assuming it can't.

### Abilities without the tap symbol

A creature can activate any ability whose cost doesn't include `{T}` or `{Q}`, even the turn it arrives. **Shivan Dragon** can pump itself with its "`{R}`: +1/+0" ability right away, which matters if it blocks. Abilities that cost only mana, life, discarding a card or sacrificing a permanent are all fine.

Sacrifice abilities are a good example. **Sakura-Tribe Elder** has "Sacrifice Sakura-Tribe Elder: Search your library for a basic land card, put that card onto the battlefield tapped, then shuffle." No tap symbol, so you can sacrifice it the turn it comes down.

### Triggered and static abilities

Triggered abilities ("when," "whenever," "at") and static abilities (effects that are simply always true) work as soon as the creature is on the battlefield. An enters-the-battlefield trigger happens immediately. A lord that gives your other creatures +1/+1 does so the moment it lands. Summoning sickness has nothing to say about either.

### Tapping a creature to pay for something else

Some costs ask you to tap creatures without using the tap symbol on those creatures. These aren't the creature's own `{T}` abilities, so summoning-sick creatures can help pay them:

- **Crew** on Vehicles taps any number of untapped creatures you control with enough total power. A creature you cast this turn can crew.
- **Convoke** lets you tap creatures to help pay for a spell. Fresh creatures can convoke.

### Noncreature permanents

Summoning sickness only applies to creatures. Artifacts, enchantments, lands and planeswalkers that aren't creatures can use their tap abilities the turn they enter. **Sol Ring** taps for two colorless mana immediately. A land you play this turn taps for mana right away. A planeswalker can activate a loyalty ability the turn you cast it.

## Haste: the exception

Haste is a keyword that switches summoning sickness off. A creature with haste can attack and use tap abilities the turn it comes under your control.

Haste can be printed on the creature, like **Goblin Guide**, **Monastery Swiftspear** and **Raging Goblin**, or granted by another card. **Lightning Greaves**, for example, is Equipment that gives the equipped creature haste and shroud, with an equip cost of zero, so it can make a new creature ready to swing immediately.

A few points about haste:

- Haste matters only while the creature would otherwise be summoning sick. On a creature you've controlled since your turn began, it does nothing extra.
- Haste works for tap abilities too, not just attacking. A hasty creature that taps for mana can tap the turn it arrives.
- Haste is a staple of red and of aggressive decks in general, because damage the turn a creature lands is damage the opponent never got a turn to prevent.

## Control changes

Because the rule is about continuous control, gaining control of a creature you didn't control at the start of your turn makes it summoning sick for you, no matter how long it sat on your opponent's side.

**Control Magic** is an Aura that says you control the enchanted creature. If you steal a creature with it on your turn, you can't attack with that creature or use its tap abilities until your next turn.

That's why "threaten" effects come with haste built in. **Act of Treason** gains control of target creature until end of turn, untaps it and gives it haste until end of turn. Without the haste, stealing a creature for one turn would accomplish almost nothing, because it couldn't attack. With it, you borrow their best blocker, swing with it, and hand it back at end of turn.

The reverse also applies. If your opponent steals your creature and you later get it back, it's summoning sick again for you until your next turn begins under your control.

## Flicker, tokens and other ways to become "new"

When a permanent leaves the battlefield and comes back, the game treats it as a brand-new object with no memory of its past. That includes summoning sickness.

- **Flicker effects.** If you exile your creature and return it, as **Cloudshift** does, the creature that returns is summoning sick. It can't attack that turn even if the original had been on the battlefield for ages. Flickering a creature on your opponent's turn avoids the problem, since it will be under your control when your next turn begins.
- **Tokens.** Creature tokens follow exactly the same rule. Tokens you make during your main phase can't attack that turn unless they have haste.
- **Recast commanders.** In Commander, a commander that died and was recast is a new creature and has summoning sickness again.

## Flash and creatures that arrive on the opponent's turn

The rule checks the start of **your** turn, so a creature that enters during your opponent's turn is ready to go on yours. If you cast a creature with flash at the end of your opponent's turn, it's under your control when your next turn begins, so it can attack and tap normally. This is a big part of why flash creatures are strong: they dodge sorcery-speed removal and still attack on schedule.

The same logic applies to creatures put onto the battlefield on the opponent's turn by any effect, such as a creature token created at instant speed during their end step.

## Things that become creatures

The rule cares whether a permanent has been under your control continuously, not whether it has been a creature continuously. That produces two useful results:

- **A Vehicle you controlled since your turn began** can attack the turn you crew it, because it's been under your control the whole time, even though it wasn't a creature until a moment ago.
- **A Vehicle you cast this turn** can't attack even after being crewed, because it hasn't been under your control since your turn started.

Lands that turn into creatures work the same way. **Mutavault** can become a 2/2 creature until end of turn. If you played it this turn and animate it, it can't attack. If it's been on your battlefield since before your turn began, it can.

## Common confusions, answered

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Can a summoning-sick creature block? | Yes. |
| Can it use an ability that costs only mana? | Yes, as long as there's no `{T}` or `{Q}` in the cost. |
| Can Llanowar Elves tap for mana the turn I cast it? | No, unless something gives it haste. |
| Can I tap Sol Ring the turn I cast it? | Yes. It's not a creature. |
| Can a fresh creature crew a Vehicle or help convoke? | Yes. Those costs don't use the creature's own tap symbol. |
| Does my creature with flash, cast on their turn, attack on mine? | Yes. |
| Does a stolen creature have summoning sickness? | Yes, until your next turn, unless it has haste. |
| Do triggered abilities wait a turn? | No. They work immediately. |

## Why it matters for deckbuilding

Summoning sickness is part of why mana creatures cost what they do and why the [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) matters. A one-mana creature that taps for mana is really a turn-two investment: you pay on turn one and collect starting on turn two. Aggressive decks value haste because it removes the turn of warning, and flash creatures get value from arriving at the end of the opponent's turn. Knowing the rule well also helps you read cards correctly; [how to read a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) explains where the tap symbol and other costs appear.

## The short version

A creature can't attack or pay a tap-symbol cost until you've controlled it since the start of your turn. It can block, trigger, use non-tap abilities and help crew or convoke right away. Haste removes the restriction, stolen and flickered creatures get it again, and noncreature permanents never have it at all.
