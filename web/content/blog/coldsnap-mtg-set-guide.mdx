Coldsnap is a Magic: The Gathering expansion released in July 2006 as the third set of the Ice Age block, a full ten years after Alliances. It returns to the frozen continent of Terisiare on Dominaria just as the long winter is ending, brings back cumulative upkeep and pitch spells, and expands snow from lands to every kind of permanent. It's also the home of Legacy mainstays Counterbalance, Dark Depths and Mishra's Bauble.

## The story

Coldsnap is set at the very end of the Ice Age. Centuries earlier, the World Spell had been cast to break the endless winter that followed the Brothers' War, and now the ice is finally melting in a period called the Thaw. For most people on Terisiare that's good news. For a few, it's a threat.

The villains are the Cult of Rimewind, a reclusive order of ice wizards who had grown powerful by using the eternal ice to amplify their magic. As the Thaw brought flooding, plague and refugees north, a cultist named Heidar became convinced the world had to be frozen again. He killed his master, Gendrin, and took control of the order. In the ruins beneath the ice he found Phyrexian war machines buried since the Brothers' War, and he set about waking them with ice crystals infused with magic. You can see those machines in cards like Phyrexian Ironfoot and Phyrexian Snowcrusher, and the crystals in Coldsteel Heart.

To get what he needed, Heidar made alliances with two of the continent's darkest powers: Haakon, the undead leader of the Order of Stromgald, and Garza Zol, the vampire queen of the plague-ridden city of Krov. When a Balduvian army under Lovisa Coldeyes stormed Rimewind's keep, Heidar unleashed the Phyrexians, and the battle became a massacre. Lovisa was killed, and the machines rampaged across the north.

Heidar's own power undid him. Twisted by Phyrexian magic, he began attacking allies as readily as enemies, even killing Lindra, the woman he loved. Garza Zol had him assassinated. Without Heidar, the Phyrexians were destroyed by shamans from Yavimaya, and the forces of Kjeldor and Yavimaya routed what remained of the cult. The story was told mostly through card flavor, web stories and a short piece by Jeff Grubb, *Keeping the Cold*, included in the set's Player's Guide.

## Mechanics

### Snow

In the original Ice Age, only lands could be snow-covered. Coldsnap made snow a supertype that any permanent can have, and introduced the snow mana symbol, which can only be paid with mana from a snow source. Squall Drifter, Frost Raptor and Rimescale Dragon are snow creatures, and cards like Skred, Rimefeather Owl and Scrying Sheets care about how many snow permanents you control. To support all of this, Coldsnap printed snow-covered basic lands, snow dual lands like Boreal Shelf, and Into the North to fetch them.

### Ripple

When you cast a spell with ripple 4, you may reveal the top four cards of your library. You can then cast any copies of that same spell among them for free, and the rest go to the bottom. Surging Flame, a two-mana shock, can chain into more Surging Flames. Ripple was designed to make playing four copies of a common worthwhile in Coldsnap-only Limited.

### Recover

A card with recover sits in your graveyard waiting for a creature to die. When a creature goes to your graveyard from the battlefield, you can pay the recover cost to return the recover card to your hand. If you don't pay, it's exiled. Grim Harvest, Krovikan Rot and Garza's Assassin all have it. Recover was a way to capture the old Ice Age feeling that graveyard order mattered without any bookkeeping.

### Cumulative upkeep and pitch spells

Cumulative upkeep returned from Ice Age (our guide to [reading a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) explains how to parse reminder text like this). Each upkeep, the permanent gets an age counter and you pay the cost once per counter or sacrifice it. Coldsnap had fun with nonmana costs: Jötun Grunt makes you put two cards from a graveyard on the bottom of their library each time, and Phyrexian Etchings draws you more cards the longer you keep paying.

Pitch spells, from Alliances, also came back. Five rares, including Commandeer, Soul Spike and Allosaurus Rider, let you exile two cards of their color from your hand instead of paying mana. Coldsnap also had cycles of "Martyr" commons, like Martyr of Sands, which sacrifice themselves and scale with cards of their color revealed from your hand, and "kindle"-style commons like Rite of Flame, Rune Snag and Sound the Call that get stronger for each copy in graveyards.

## Limited

Coldsnap was drafted on its own: three packs of Coldsnap, since Ice Age and Alliances boosters were long gone by 2006. That's exactly why the set has so many commons that reward multiple copies. Ripple cards like Surging Flame and Surging Sentinels, and kindle-style cards like Rune Snag and Sound the Call, are much better as the second or third copy, so drafting duplicates was a real strategy.

The other big Limited theme was snow. A deck with plenty of snow-covered basics and snow creatures turned on Skred, Frost Raptor's shroud, Boreal Centaur's pump and Rimebound Dead's regeneration. Snow basics came in packs, so picking them up mattered more than in any normal format. Removal at common included Skred, Chill to the Bone (which kills only nonsnow creatures), Gelid Shackles and Krovikan Rot at uncommon.

Coldsnap had no gold signpost cards for each color pair the way modern sets do. Most of its multicolor cards are allied-color uncommons and rares, which nudged drafters toward allied pairs, but the format was mostly about snow density, duplicates and removal. ## The meta: how it played in Constructed

### Standard (2006 to 2008)

Coldsnap was legal in Standard based on its release date, not its block, so it stayed in Standard for a little over two years and rotated out with Time Spiral block when Shards of Alara arrived in October 2008. The Ice Age and Alliances reprints in its theme decks were not legal in Standard.

Its first Standard season sat alongside Kamigawa block, Ravnica block and Ninth Edition. Counterbalance found an immediate partner in Kamigawa's Sensei's Divining Top, and "Counter-Top" decks were part of that summer's metagame. Rite of Flame became the key ritual in Dragonstorm, the combo deck Makihito Mihara used in the Standard portion of his 2006 World Championship win. Later, snow decks used Skred, snow lands and Scrying Sheets, and Ohran Viper also saw Constructed play. No Coldsnap card was ever banned in Standard. For how rotation works, see our [rotation explainer](/blog/mtg-rotation-explained).

### Modern, Legacy, Vintage, Pauper and Commander (as of October 2026)

Coldsnap is old enough to feel vintage, but new enough to be legal in Modern.

- **Counterbalance** became a pillar of Legacy, paired with Sensei's Divining Top in "Counter-Top" control decks for years until Top was banned there in 2017.
- **Dark Depths** is a Legacy combo piece, assembled quickly with Vampire Hexmage or Thespian's Stage. It's banned in Modern.
- **Mishra's Bauble** is a free artifact that fuels artifact counts, delirium and prowess in Modern and Legacy.
- **Rite of Flame** is banned in Modern but legal in Legacy and Vintage.
- **Skred** is a strong Pauper removal spell in snow decks, and Into the North helps those decks find their lands.
- **Commander** players lean on Zur the Enchanter, Adarkar Valkyrie, Coldsteel Heart and the snow duals, especially after Kaldheim revived snow.

Coldsnap has no Reserved List cards. Our [Legacy format guide](/blog/mtg-legacy-format-guide) explains where Counterbalance and Dark Depths fit today.

## Cards from Coldsnap worth owning

- **Counterbalance**: a defining Legacy control card.
- **Dark Depths**: an iconic Legacy combo land.
- **Mishra's Bauble**: a free cantrip artifact played across Modern and Legacy.
- **Skred**: efficient Pauper and Modern snow removal.
- **Zur the Enchanter**: a long-running Esper-colored Commander.
- **Adarkar Valkyrie**: a value engine in Commander.
- **Coldsteel Heart** and **Into the North**: easy pickups for snow-themed Commander decks.
