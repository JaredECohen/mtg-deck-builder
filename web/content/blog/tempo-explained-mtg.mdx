Tempo in MTG is how efficiently you turn your mana and your turns into progress on the board compared with your opponent. You gain tempo when you spend less mana than your opponent to change the game by the same amount, such as killing their three-mana creature with a one-mana spell. You lose tempo when you waste mana, skip a play or let them undo your turn for cheap.

Every deck deals in tempo, not just the decks named after it. This guide covers the concept: how to measure it, which cards gain it, and how it trades off against card advantage. If you're looking for the archetype built around it, see [tempo decks explained](/blog/mtg-tempo-decks-explained).

## Tempo is a resource, like cards and life

Magic has a few resources you spend to win: **cards** in hand, **life**, and **time**, which shows up as mana and turns. Tempo is the time resource. It's harder to see than a hand size or a life total, but it's just as real.

A simple way to think about it: each turn you get a certain amount of mana. Any mana you don't use, and any mana you spend on something that doesn't help, is gone forever. The player who uses more of their mana well, turn after turn, is usually ahead even if the card counts are equal.

That's why the [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) matters so much. A curve is a plan for spending your mana every turn. A hand that casts a two-drop on turn two, a three-drop on turn three and a four-drop on turn four is using its mana fully. A hand with the same cards that does nothing until turn four has wasted three turns of tempo.

## How to measure a tempo swing

The easiest measure is mana spent versus mana undone.

- You cast Lightning Bolt (one mana) on the opponent's three-mana creature. You spent one mana, they lost three mana's worth of development. You gained roughly two mana of tempo.
- They cast a four-mana creature, and you bounce it with Unsummon (one mana). They spent four mana and now have to spend it again. You gained about three mana of tempo, but they kept the card.
- You cast a five-mana spell that kills one two-mana creature. You spent more than they did. That's a tempo loss, even though it was a one-for-one trade of cards.

This isn't exact math. A one-mana creature that's carrying the game is worth more than its mana value, and a big creature that isn't doing anything is worth less. But comparing mana spent on each side is a good first check.

## Ways to gain tempo

### Cheap removal

Removal that costs less than the threat it answers is the most common tempo gain in Magic. **Swords to Plowshares** exiles a creature for one mana (its controller gains life equal to its power). **Lightning Bolt** deals 3 damage for one mana. **Doom Blade** destroys a nonblack creature for two. Each one can answer something that cost the opponent more.

### Bounce

Bounce returns a permanent to its owner's hand. It doesn't remove the card, so it's card-neutral at best for you, but it erases the mana the opponent spent and forces them to spend it again. Bounce is a pure tempo play: you're buying time, not cards.

Bounce is at its best in specific situations:

- **On a token.** A token that leaves the battlefield stops existing, so bouncing it is real removal.
- **On a creature with an Aura, Equipment attached or counters.** The Aura goes to the graveyard, the Equipment stays behind unattached, and counters are lost. You undo several investments at once.
- **On a blocker during your attack**, letting your creatures connect while the opponent's turn of mana is spent replaying it.
- **In response to an opponent's investment**, like bouncing a creature after they spend their turn pumping it.

Bounce is at its worst on a cheap creature with a strong enters-the-battlefield ability, because they get to use the ability again.

### Counterspells

A counterspell that costs less than the spell it counters is a tempo gain. **Counterspell** for two mana against a five-mana spell is a big swing. The opponent spent their turn and has nothing to show for it.

### Efficient threats

A creature that does more than its mana cost suggests is a tempo gain every turn it stays on the battlefield. A cheap evasive creature that attacks for 3 each turn forces the opponent to spend resources answering it, and every turn they don't, you're ahead.

### Using all your mana

The simplest tempo gain is just spending your mana well. Casting two spells in one turn when you can, making your land drop every turn, and having a play at every point on the curve all keep you ahead of an opponent who stumbles.

### Haste and flash

Haste gets damage in before the opponent has a turn to answer, which is tempo. Flash lets you spend mana on the opponent's turn instead of leaving it unused when you hold up an answer you didn't need.

## Ways to lose tempo

- **Missing land drops** or keeping a hand that can't use its mana early. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) covers how to avoid keeping slow hands.
- **Casting a spell that does nothing this turn** when you had a play that affected the board.
- **Holding up mana you never use.** Passing with counterspell mana open is fine when it pays off. Doing it every turn while the opponent develops freely is a slow loss.
- **Getting bounced or countered**, especially after investing extra mana in a creature with Auras or Equipment.
- **Overpaying for answers**, like using a five-mana removal spell on a one-drop when a cheaper answer was available.

## Tempo versus card advantage

Card advantage means having more useful cards than your opponent. Tempo means using your time and mana better. The two often pull in opposite directions, and the most important strategic decisions come from weighing them.

### Trading cards for time

Many tempo plays cost you card advantage:

- **Bounce** is often a card for no card. You spend Unsummon, they keep their creature.
- **Daze**, which can be cast by returning an Island to your hand, saves mana but costs you a land drop.
- **Chump blocking** sacrifices a creature to save life and buy a turn.

These plays are correct when the time they buy is worth more than the card. If you're about to win in two turns, a card-for-time trade is excellent. If the game will go long, it's often a mistake.

### Trading time for cards

The opposite trade is spending mana and turns to get ahead on cards:

- **Card draw spells** that do nothing to the board, like a turn spent casting a draw-three.
- **Board wipes** like Wrath of God, which spend a whole turn's mana but can trade one card for several of the opponent's.
- **Expensive value creatures** that generate cards over time.

These plays are correct when you can survive the tempo loss. Tapping out for a draw spell while facing a lethal attack is not.

### How to decide

Ask two questions:

1. **Who's ahead, and who's the faster deck?** If you're the one pressuring, tempo is usually worth more than cards. If you're the one defending, cards and stabilizing matter more.
2. **How long will the game last?** In a short game, extra cards rarely get used. In a long game, small tempo gains get washed away and the player with more resources wins.

A card that gains both at once, like a two-for-one that's also cheap, is the best of all. Snapcaster Mage, for example, adds a body and a second use of a cheap spell for two mana. Cards like that are why some cards are rated so highly.

## Tempo in different archetypes

Every archetype cares about tempo, but in different ways:

- **Aggro** lives on tempo. It plays cheap threats every turn and wants the opponent to fall behind and stay behind.
- **Tempo decks** pair cheap threats with cheap interaction, so they can attack and disrupt in the same turn.
- **Midrange** tries to win individual trades, gaining small tempo and card edges with efficient creatures and removal.
- **Control** often loses tempo early on purpose, then catches up with board wipes and card advantage.
- **Ramp** spends early turns on mana development, then jumps ahead with threats that cost far more than the opponent can answer efficiently.
- **Combo** cares about tempo in a narrow sense: having enough mana and time to assemble its pieces before the opponent wins or disrupts it.

For more on how these plans match up, see [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained).

## Tempo in Limited

Tempo is especially visible in Limited, where decks are less powerful and boards matter more. A cheap bounce spell used on a creature that just received an Aura, or a combat trick that kills a blocker in a key turn, can decide the game. When you're [evaluating cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited), ask how much mana a card costs compared with what it does on the turn you cast it.

## Practical habits for gaining tempo

- **Plan your mana for the next few turns.** Know what you'll cast on turns two, three and four.
- **Use cheap answers on expensive threats** when you can, and save expensive answers for the cards only they can stop.
- **Bounce at the right moment**, ideally on tokens, enchanted creatures or blockers in your key attack.
- **Don't hold up mana you won't use.** If the opponent has nothing worth countering, develop instead.
- **Know when to trade the other way.** Tempo matters most when one player is racing. When the game settles down, card advantage takes over.

## The takeaway

Tempo is about using your mana and turns better than your opponent. Cheap removal, bounce, counterspells and efficient threats gain it; wasted mana, missed land drops and overpaying lose it. Tempo and card advantage trade against each other, and the right choice depends on who's ahead and how long the game will last.
