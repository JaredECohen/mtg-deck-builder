To protect your Magic: The Gathering cards, sleeve the ones you play with, keep decks in a sturdy deck box, store your collection upright in a cool, dry, dark place, and handle cards with clean, dry hands. The biggest threats to cards are moisture, sunlight, bending and wear from shuffling, and all four are easy to avoid with a little care.

This guide covers each part of that: picking sleeves, double sleeving, deck boxes, binders, bulk storage and the everyday habits that keep cards in good shape. None of it requires expensive gear. The goal is simple: cards that stay flat, clean and shuffle well for years.

## Why card care matters

Magic cards are made of layered card stock with a printed surface. They wear in predictable ways:

- **Edges and corners** get rough or white from shuffling and handling.
- **Surfaces** pick up scratches, scuffs and oils from hands and tables.
- **Moisture** makes cards warp, curl or stick together.
- **Sunlight** fades the ink over time.
- **Pressure** bends or creases cards, especially in tight boxes or binder rings.

A worn card still plays, but in tournaments, visibly worn or bent cards can count as marked cards if they're distinguishable from the rest of your deck while sleeved. Protecting your cards also keeps your options open if you ever want to trade them.

## Choosing sleeves

Sleeves are the single most useful protection for cards you play with. They protect the surface and edges, make shuffling easier and keep the whole deck uniform.

### Size

Magic cards use the standard card size, so look for sleeves labeled for standard-size cards. Smaller sleeves, often called Japanese size or small size, are made for other card games and won't fit a Magic card.

### Finish and color

- **Matte backs** are less reflective and often shuffle more smoothly. Many players prefer them for long games.
- **Glossy backs** look bright but can stick together a little more, especially when new.
- **Opaque colored backs** hide the card's back completely, so wear on an individual card can't show through. Most tournament players use them.
- **Clear sleeves** show the card back. They're fine for casual play and for storage.

### Quality

Cheap sleeves tend to split at the seams, scuff quickly and vary in size within a pack. Mid-range sleeves usually last much longer. Whatever you buy, check a pack for consistency: sleeves of slightly different sizes or with visible defects can make individual cards identifiable.

### When to replace them

Replace sleeves when they show splits, dirt, cloudy fronts or worn corners, or when one sleeve looks different from the others. In a tournament, a single damaged sleeve is enough to make one card distinguishable from the rest, which is a problem even if you didn't mean anything by it. Replace the whole deck's sleeves at once so they match.

## Double sleeving

Double sleeving means putting each card in a thin, tight inner sleeve before the regular outer sleeve. Inner sleeves are often called perfect-fit sleeves.

How to do it:

1. **Slide the card into the inner sleeve from the top**, so the inner sleeve's opening is at the card's top edge.
2. **Turn it upside down** and slide it into the outer sleeve, so the inner sleeve's opening faces the bottom of the outer sleeve.

This way, both openings are sealed by the other sleeve, and dust and spills have a much harder time reaching the card.

Why players do it:

- **Spill protection.** A drink knocked over on the table is far less likely to soak through two sleeves.
- **Longer outer sleeve life.** The inner sleeve absorbs some of the pressure, and outer sleeves stay firmer.
- **Valuable cards.** It's common for cards you care about most.

The downside is thickness. A double-sleeved Commander deck of 100 cards takes up noticeably more room than a single-sleeved one, so check that your deck box can fit it.

## Deck boxes

A good deck box keeps your sleeved deck from bending and protects it in a bag. Look for:

- **Capacity that matches your deck.** A 60-card deck with a sideboard needs less room than a double-sleeved Commander deck. A box that's too tight bends cards when you close it; a box that's too loose lets cards slide around.
- **A sturdy closure** that won't pop open in a bag.
- **A separate tray or compartment** for dice, tokens or a sideboard, if you need one.

Keep the deck box closed when you're not playing, and don't leave it in a hot car. Heat can warp sleeves and cards alike.

## Binders

Binders are great for showing off a collection or keeping trade cards organized. A few things to watch for:

- **Side-loading pages** hold cards more securely than top-loading pages, because cards can't slide out when the binder is turned upside down.
- **Zipper binders** keep dust out and cards in.
- **Avoid overfilling.** Two cards in one pocket or a binder stuffed past capacity puts pressure on the cards.
- **Watch ring binders.** Cards in the pocket closest to the rings can get dented when the binder closes. Many collectors prefer binders with pages bound directly into the spine.
- **Choose pages labeled acid-free and PVC-free.** Some older plastic pages can release chemicals over time that damage cards.

Store binders upright on a shelf, like books, rather than stacked flat under heavy objects.

## Long-term storage for your collection

Most players end up with far more cards than fit in decks or binders. Bulk storage keeps them organized and safe.

- **Cardboard storage boxes** sized for cards are the standard choice. Long rows let you sort by set, color or card type.
- **Store cards upright,** standing on their bottom edge, in a box that's full but not tight. A half-empty box lets cards lean and curve; an overstuffed one bends them.
- **Use dividers** to keep sections separate and to support cards when a row isn't full.
- **Penny sleeves and top loaders** add extra protection for valuable singles. A penny sleeve is a thin clear sleeve; a top loader is a rigid plastic holder that keeps a card flat.

### Where to store it

The location matters more than the box:

- **Cool and dry.** Avoid basements, attics, garages and outer walls, where temperature and humidity swing.
- **Off the floor.** A small leak or spill can ruin a whole box.
- **Away from sunlight.** Direct sun fades card fronts surprisingly fast, even through a window.
- **Away from heat sources** like radiators and vents.

If you live somewhere humid, a few desiccant packs in your storage boxes help absorb moisture. Replace or recharge them as directed.

## Humidity, curling and foils

Foil cards are especially sensitive to humidity. The foil layer and the paper layers absorb moisture at different rates, so foils can curl when the air is humid or when a card moves between environments. Sleeving foils, storing them in a stable environment and keeping them in tight stacks or binders helps. A mildly curled foil often flattens again over time once it's stored in steadier conditions; forcing it flat with heat or weight risks damaging it.

Nonfoil cards curl less, but they can still warp in damp conditions or stick together if they get wet. If cards do get wet, separate them gently, let them dry flat on a clean surface and resist the urge to use a hairdryer.

## Handling and shuffling

Good habits at the table prevent most wear:

- **Wash and dry your hands** before playing, especially after eating.
- **Keep food and drinks off the play area,** or at least well away from your cards.
- **Use a playmat.** It gives cards a clean, soft surface instead of a rough or dirty table.
- **Shuffle gently.** Riffle shuffling unsleeved cards bends them. With sleeves, a careful riffle or a mash shuffle is fine. Overhand shuffles are gentle but take longer to randomize.
- **Don't bend cards to shuffle.** A bridge that bows the cards puts a curve in them over time.

Pile shuffling, dealing cards into several piles, is handy for counting your deck but doesn't randomize it on its own. Follow it with a real shuffle.

## Proxies and playtest cards

Many players test new decks with stand-in cards before buying real copies or before risking their valuable cards in a new group. If you do, mark them clearly so they're never mixed up with the real thing. ## The takeaway

Sleeve the cards you play, double sleeve the ones you care about most, keep decks in a box that fits, and store everything else upright in a cool, dry, dark place. Handle cards with clean hands on a clean surface, shuffle gently, and replace worn sleeves before they become a problem. Good care is cheap, and it keeps your cards playable and tradable for years.
