Cards get banned in Magic: The Gathering when they make a format worse to play. That usually means a card or deck is winning too often, pushing other strategies out, or creating games that aren't fun or interactive. Wizards of the Coast manages the banned lists for its formats and updates them in public announcements. A banned card can't be played in that format at all, while Vintage also uses a restricted list that limits a card to one copy.

This guide explains the reasons behind bans, how banning differs from restriction, how announcements generally work, and how Commander approaches its own list. It doesn't list which cards are banned today, because those lists change. For the current lists, check the official Banned and Restricted page on the Wizards of the Coast website before you build or play in an event.

## What a ban actually does

Each constructed format has its own legal card pool. A banned list removes specific cards from that pool. If a card is banned in a format, you can't have any copies of it in your main deck or sideboard for that format.

Bans are format by format. A card banned in one format can be perfectly legal in another. That's normal, because formats have different card pools and different power levels. A card that's fine among the most powerful cards ever printed can be overwhelming in a format where the competition is weaker. The [formats overview](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) covers how each format's card pool is defined.

Bans matter most at sanctioned events, like store tournaments and larger competitive play. At a casual kitchen table, players can agree to use whatever rules they like. That's a choice the group makes, not something the official lists control.

## Why Wizards bans cards

Wizards has explained its reasoning many times over the years, and the same few themes come up again and again.

### Format health and dominance

The most common reason is that one card, or the deck built around it, is too strong. If a single deck wins far more than its share of games and makes up a large portion of the competitive field, the format starts to feel solved. Players either play that deck or play something built to beat it, and everything else disappears.

Wizards looks at data such as how often decks appear, how they perform against the field, and how they perform in specific matchups, along with feedback from players. A deck can be strong and popular without being a problem. It becomes a ban candidate when it's crowding out the rest of the format.

### Diversity

Closely related is diversity. A healthy format has several viable strategies, so players can choose decks they enjoy and still have a fair chance. When one card makes a whole category of decks unplayable, such as a card that punishes slow decks so hard that control can't exist, that can justify a ban even if the card's own deck isn't the single best one.

### Play patterns

Some bans aren't about win rates at all. They're about what games feel like. Wizards has banned cards for creating patterns like:

- **Games that end too early,** before the opponent has a real chance to interact.
- **Non-interactive turns,** where one player takes a very long turn while the other watches.
- **Too little counterplay,** where a card can't reasonably be answered once it resolves.
- **Repetitive games,** where the same opening decides the result over and over.

Fast mana is a frequent theme here. Cards that give a large burst of extra mana early in the game make explosive, lopsided starts more common. Free spells, which can be cast without paying mana, raise similar concerns because they let players act without tapping lands, which removes the usual tradeoffs.

### Logistics and other reasons

A smaller set of bans is about practical issues rather than power. In eternal formats, cards that require gambling for ownership of cards, known as ante, and cards designed only for special draft formats are not legal in sanctioned constructed play. A few old cards that require physical dexterity, like flipping a card onto the table, are banned in Vintage for the same practical reason. Wizards has also removed some cards from play because of offensive content.

## Bans in formats with rotation

In Standard, the card pool rotates: older sets leave the format on a regular schedule as new sets come in. Because problem cards eventually rotate out on their own, Standard bans are less common, but they do happen when a card is distorting the format and rotation is too far away.

Non-rotating formats like Pioneer, Modern and Legacy rely more on their banned lists, because nothing ever leaves the pool otherwise. As new sets are added, new interactions appear, and the banned list is the main tool for keeping the format in shape.

## Bans versus restrictions

Vintage is the one major format that uses a restricted list. A restricted card can be played, but only one copy across your main deck and sideboard combined.

The logic is that Vintage is meant to be the format where you can play almost every card ever printed. Instead of removing the most powerful cards entirely, Vintage keeps them legal and limits how often you can draw them. The famous Power Nine, including Black Lotus, Ancestral Recall and Time Walk, have been restricted in Vintage for decades, which is why the format still feels like Magic rather than a contest to draw the right card on turn one.

Vintage's banned list is small and mostly covers the practical cases above, like ante and dexterity cards. Legacy, by contrast, doesn't use a restricted list. Cards are either legal or banned. The [Vintage guide](/blog/mtg-vintage-format-guide) explains how restriction shapes that format's decks.

## How announcements work

Wizards updates banned and restricted lists through official announcements, which explain each change and the reasoning behind it. Some general patterns:

- **Announcements are usually scheduled.** They tend to be tied to the release calendar, so players know when changes might come.
- **Emergency changes are possible.** If a format becomes badly broken, Wizards can make changes outside the usual schedule.
- **There's an effective date.** Changes for tabletop play generally take effect on a stated date. Digital platforms like MTG Arena can apply changes on their own timeline.
- **Unbans happen too.** When a format has changed enough that a banned card no longer seems dangerous, it can come off the list.

Digital-only formats also have another tool: suspension. A suspended card is temporarily not playable in that format, which gives Wizards a softer option than a permanent ban.

If you're preparing for an event, check the official list close to the event date. A deck that was legal last month might not be today.

## Commander's own approach

Commander has always worked a little differently. The format was created by players, and for most of its history its banned list was maintained by a group separate from Wizards, the Commander Rules Committee. Wizards has since taken over management of the format, working with input from community members. Either way, Commander's list follows its own philosophy, because the format's goals are different.

Commander is mainly a social, multiplayer format. Its banned list has historically aimed at cards that cause problems in casual multiplayer play, such as cards that are too strong in a 100-card singleton deck, cards that make games miserable for the whole table, or cards that create problems specifically when used as a commander. It isn't built around tournament win rates.

The format also relies on conversation more than on the list alone. Players are expected to talk about power level before a game, and the official [Commander brackets](/blog/commander-brackets-explained) give groups a shared way to describe how strong their decks are. Many cards that would be too much at a casual table are legal, with the understanding that players will match their decks to the group. The [Commander overview](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) covers the format's rules if you're new to it.

## What bans mean for you

- **Check legality before an event.** The Deck Lab builder checks format legality as you build, but always confirm against the official list for sanctioned play.
- **Don't build around guesses.** Bans are hard to predict. Build decks you enjoy rather than trying to guess what the next announcement will change.
- **Learn from the reasons.** Ban announcements explain why a card was a problem. Reading them is a good way to understand what makes a format healthy, and what kinds of cards are powerful in general.

## The takeaway

Cards are banned when they hurt a format's health, shrink its diversity, or create games that aren't fun to play. Vintage limits its most powerful cards to one copy instead of banning them, Commander follows a casual, multiplayer-focused philosophy, and every format's list can change. Before you sleeve up for an event, check the official Banned and Restricted page.
