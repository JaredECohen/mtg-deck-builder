In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), an artifact deck is built around a simple idea: fill the battlefield with cheap artifacts, then play cards that get stronger, cheaper or more efficient for every artifact you control. Each artifact is weak alone, but together they turn cost-reduction spells into free plays and make a single payoff card enormous. The price is a shared weakness: cards that destroy artifacts, especially the ones that hit all of them at once, can wreck the whole plan.

This guide covers how artifact synergy works, the main mechanics that reward artifacts, the payoffs that close games, how to build the deck, and how to plan around artifact removal. For where artifacts fit among aggro, control, combo and the other strategies, the [deck archetypes overview](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) has the short version.

## What makes an artifact deck work

Artifacts are a card type, and they're mostly colorless. That makes them easy to play in any deck, but an artifact deck isn't just a deck with artifacts in it. It's a deck where the **count** of artifacts matters.

Most artifact strategies have three layers:

1. **Enablers.** Cheap artifacts whose main job is to be an artifact. These include zero- and one-mana artifact creatures, mana rocks, and artifact lands.
2. **Synergy engines.** Cards that care how many artifacts you control, such as cost reducers and abilities that check for a minimum number.
3. **Payoffs.** Cards that turn a wide artifact board into damage, card advantage or a win.

The deck works when all three layers show up together. A hand full of enablers with no payoff does very little, and a payoff with no artifacts to count is a weak card.

## Enablers: the cheap artifacts

The enablers are what make the deck fast. Classic examples:

- **Ornithopter:** a 0/2 flying artifact creature that costs nothing.
- **Memnite:** a 1/1 artifact creature that also costs nothing.
- **Artifact lands:** Seat of the Synod, Vault of Whispers, Ancient Den, Great Furnace and Tree of Tales are lands that are also artifacts. Each one taps for a single color of mana and counts toward your artifact total the moment you play it.
- **Mana rocks:** artifacts like Mind Stone or, in Commander, Sol Ring that produce mana and count as artifacts.
- **Artifact tokens:** many modern cards create Treasure, Clue, Food or Thopter tokens. These are all artifacts, so a deck that makes tokens builds its artifact count without spending cards on it.

Zero-cost creatures do almost nothing on their own. They're in the deck because they let you play several artifacts on turn one, which powers up everything that comes after.

## Cost reduction: affinity, improvise and friends

The signature mechanic of artifact decks is cost reduction. The more artifacts you have, the less your spells cost.

### Affinity for artifacts

**Affinity for artifacts** means a spell costs `{1}` less to cast for each artifact you control. It only reduces generic mana, the plain number in the cost, not colored mana.

- **Frogmite** costs `{4}` and is a 2/2. With four artifacts on the battlefield, it costs nothing.
- **Myr Enforcer** costs `{7}` and is a 4/4. With a fast start, you can cast it on turn two or three.
- **Thoughtcast** costs `{4}{U}` and draws two cards. With enough artifacts, it costs just `{U}`.

Affinity is why the classic artifact aggro deck in older formats is often called "Affinity." It could dump most of its hand onto the battlefield in the first two or three turns.

### Improvise

**Improvise** works differently. When you cast a spell with improvise, you can tap your untapped artifacts to help pay for it, and each artifact you tap pays for `{1}` of the generic cost. Like affinity, it doesn't pay colored mana. Unlike affinity, it uses up your artifacts for the turn, which matters if they're creatures you wanted to attack with.

### Metalcraft and other thresholds

**Metalcraft** abilities turn on as long as you control three or more artifacts. Galvanic Blast deals 2 damage to any target for `{R}`, or 4 damage instead with metalcraft. Etched Champion gains protection from all colors with metalcraft, which makes it nearly impossible for colored creatures to block. Threshold cards like these push you to keep your artifact count high rather than sacrificing it carelessly.

## Payoffs: how artifact decks win

Payoffs are the cards that convert all those cheap artifacts into a win.

### Scaling threats

- **Cranial Plating** is an Equipment that gives the equipped creature +1/+0 for each artifact you control. It also has an ability to attach it to a creature you control for `{B}{B}`, at instant speed, which lets you move it to an unblocked attacker after blocks.
- **Steel Overseer** taps to put a +1/+1 counter on each artifact creature you control. Left alone for a few turns, it grows your whole team.

### Sacrifice engines

- **Arcbound Ravager** lets you sacrifice an artifact to put a +1/+1 counter on it. It also has modular, so when it dies, you can move its counters onto another artifact creature. In response to removal, you can sacrifice artifacts that would be lost anyway and keep their value.
- **Atog** lets you sacrifice an artifact to give it +2/+2 until end of turn. A big artifact board can turn Atog into a lethal attacker in one swing.

Sacrifice payoffs give you a way to get value from your artifacts before artifact removal takes them, which is one of the best defenses the archetype has. The same sacrifice-for-value idea drives other strategies too, and some artifact decks lean far enough into engines and loops that they play like [combo decks](/blog/mtg-combo-decks-explained).

## Building an artifact deck

The exact cards depend on your format, but the structure is similar across most versions.

| Role | What to look for | Rough share of the nonland cards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enablers | Zero- and one-mana artifacts, artifact tokens | A large share, especially in aggro builds |
| Engines | Affinity, improvise and metalcraft cards | A solid core |
| Payoffs | Cards that scale with artifact count | Several, so you draw one most games |
| Interaction | Removal or protection in your colors | A few slots |

A few principles help:

- **Count your artifacts.** Before you finalize the list, count every card that is an artifact or makes one. If the number is low, your affinity and metalcraft cards will often be weak.
- **Play artifact lands when the format allows it.** They let you add artifacts to the battlefield without spending a spell. The trade-off is that they're vulnerable to artifact removal like everything else.
- **Don't overload on payoffs.** A hand with three payoffs and nothing to count is a bad hand. Enablers should outnumber payoffs.
- **Plan your colors around what the artifacts need.** Most artifact cards are colorless, so your colors are usually chosen for the colored payoffs and interaction you want, such as blue for card draw or red for burn.

### Artifacts in Commander

In Commander, artifacts are everywhere, because mana rocks are colorless and fit in any deck. A dedicated artifact Commander deck uses the same structure, just slower: more mana rocks and token makers, fewer zero-cost creatures, and payoffs that generate cards or mana from each artifact rather than a fast clock. Artifact token strategies also overlap a lot with the go-wide plans in the [tokens deck guide](/blog/mtg-tokens-deck-guide).

## The weakness: artifact removal

Every card in an artifact deck shares a card type, so every artifact removal spell has a target. That's the archetype's biggest weakness.

### Single-target artifact removal

Cheap cards like Shatter, Naturalize, Disenchant and Ancient Grudge destroy one artifact. Against an artifact deck, they're always live, and they're often aimed at your best payoff, such as Cranial Plating or Steel Overseer.

### Mass artifact removal

The real danger is cards that hit all your artifacts at once.

- **Shatterstorm** destroys all artifacts.
- **Vandalblast** destroys one artifact you don't control, or, with its overload cost, every artifact you don't control.
- **Hurkyl's Recall** returns every artifact a player owns to their hand, which undoes several turns of work.

### Hate that shuts off abilities

**Stony Silence** stops activated abilities of artifacts from being activated. That turns off mana rocks, Equipment's equip abilities, and sacrifice engines like Arcbound Ravager in one card.

### Playing around artifact hate

You can't make your deck immune, but you can make removal less devastating:

- **Use sacrifice outlets in response.** If your artifacts are about to be destroyed, sacrifice them to Arcbound Ravager or Atog first and get something for them.
- **Don't overextend into a sweeper.** If you suspect mass removal, keep a few artifacts in hand. You'll lose a little speed but keep enough to rebuild.
- **Sideboard for it.** In best-of-three, bring in threats that aren't artifacts, protection, or counterspells after game one. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers how to plan those swaps.
- **Diversify your threats.** Payoffs that aren't artifacts themselves survive artifact-only removal.

Also expect opponents to have artifact removal in their sideboard even if they don't main-deck it. Games two and three are usually harder than game one.

## The takeaway

An artifact deck plays cheap enablers to build its artifact count, uses affinity, improvise and metalcraft to make its spells cheaper and stronger, then wins with payoffs that scale with every artifact on the battlefield. Keep the enabler count high and the payoff count reasonable, use sacrifice outlets to get value before removal lands, and plan for opponents to bring artifact hate after game one.
