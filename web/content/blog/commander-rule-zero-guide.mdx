Rule Zero in Commander is the idea that the players at a table can talk before a game and agree on what kind of game they want, including any changes to the usual rules. In practice it means a short pregame conversation about how strong everyone's decks are, what's off limits and whether the group uses any house rules. Done well, it takes two minutes and saves an hour of a lopsided game nobody enjoyed.

If you're new to the format itself, start with [what Commander is and how it works](/blog/what-is-commander-edh). This guide focuses on the conversation: what to say, what to ask and what to do when decks don't line up.

## What Rule Zero actually means

The name is borrowed from tabletop role-playing games, where "rule zero" traditionally means the group can change any rule to suit the table. Commander picked up the same idea because it started as a casual, player-run format built for multiplayer fun rather than tournaments.

Two things are worth being clear about:

- **Rule Zero isn't a numbered rule in the Comprehensive Rules.** It's a community term for agreement between players. The official rules of Commander still apply unless your group agrees otherwise.
- **It's about agreement, not permission.** Rule Zero doesn't mean one player can insist on something. It means the table decides together. If someone doesn't want to play against a house rule, that's part of the conversation too.

Rule Zero matters most in casual play. At a store event or tournament, the organizer's rules apply, and you shouldn't expect to renegotiate them at the table.

## Why the pregame talk matters

Commander decks vary enormously. A preconstructed deck right out of the box and a tuned deck with fast mana and tutors are technically playing the same format, but they're playing very different games. When those two meet, one player usually spends the evening watching.

Unlike a 1v1 format, a Commander game is four people sharing a couple of hours. One mismatched deck affects everyone. The pregame talk is how the table makes sure the game it sits down to play is the game everyone wanted.

## How to have the conversation

You don't need a script, but covering the same few points every time keeps it short and comfortable.

### 1. Describe your deck's plan

Start with what your deck does and how it usually wins. "It's a Golgari graveyard deck that grinds value and wins with big creatures" tells the table far more than a power number does.

### 2. Give a sense of power

This is where the official Commander brackets help. Saying "it's a bracket 2" or "a solid bracket 3" gives everyone the same reference point. If you aren't familiar with them, [Commander brackets explained](/blog/commander-brackets-explained) walks through all five.

Add detail the bracket doesn't capture:

- How many Game Changers it runs, if any.
- Whether it has any combos, and how early they tend to come together.
- How quickly it usually threatens to win.

### 3. Flag anything that might surprise people

Some strategies are fine in one group and unwelcome in another. Mention them up front:

- Mass land destruction, such as Armageddon.
- Extra-turn chains.
- Two-card infinite combos.
- Heavy stax: cards that tax or lock out the whole table.
- Very long turns with lots of shuffling, tokens or triggers.

You're not apologizing. You're giving people the chance to say "sounds good" or "can you play something else?"

### 4. Ask about house rules

Every group has its own habits. Ask before shuffling up, especially in a new group. A quick "anything I should know about how you play?" covers it.

### 5. Ask, don't just tell

The best pregame talks are two-way. Ask what others are playing and whether anyone wants a particular kind of game. Sometimes a player brought a casual deck specifically because they want a relaxed night.

## Common house rules

House rules are changes a group agrees on for its own games. None of these are official, and none are required. They're just ones you'll often run into.

| House rule | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Proxies allowed | Stand-in cards for ones a player doesn't own. Usually marked clearly so they aren't mistaken for real cards. |
| Extra free mulligan for bad hands | Some groups allow a redraw for a hand with no lands or all lands, beyond the usual free first mulligan. |
| Group ban list | A few cards or strategies the group would rather not see, such as specific combos or land destruction. |
| No infinite combos | Combos are allowed only if they don't loop forever, or not at all. |
| Random seat or turn order | A die roll or similar to decide who goes first, sometimes with the last game's winner going last. |
| Variant rules | Optional add-ons like Planechase or Archenemy that the group enjoys. |

When a group uses a house rule, the important part is that everyone knows it before the game starts. A rule announced mid-game, after someone has already been hit by it, feels unfair even if it's reasonable.

<Callout title="Tip">If you're the host or the regular in a group, mention your house rules to newcomers before they pick a deck, not after they've shuffled.</Callout>

## Using brackets well

The bracket system was designed partly to make Rule Zero easier. A few habits make it work better:

- **Be honest about the top of your bracket.** If your deck is right on the edge of the next bracket, say so. "It's a bracket 3 but a strong one" is useful information.
- **Remember brackets describe intent, not just cards.** A deck can avoid every Game Changer and still be built to crush a casual table. The [brackets guide](/blog/commander-brackets-explained) covers why intent matters.
- **Use the bracket to start the talk, not end it.** "Bracket 2" plus a sentence about the deck's plan is ideal. A number alone leaves out too much.

## Handling mismatches gracefully

Even with a good conversation, sometimes decks just don't line up. Here are ways to handle it without making anyone feel bad.

### Before the game

- **Swap decks.** If you brought more than one deck, offer to switch to one that fits the table better. Carrying a casual deck and a stronger one makes this easy.
- **Lend a deck.** If someone's deck is much weaker, offering them one of yours can even out the game.
- **Agree on a quick game.** Sometimes the table decides to play the mismatch anyway and see what happens. That's fine if everyone agrees.

### During the game

- **Talk about it if it's clearly lopsided.** If one deck is running away with the game on turn four, it's okay to say so and offer to restart with different decks.
- **Spread out your attention.** If your deck is stronger, avoid ganging up on the weakest player. Proper [threat assessment](/blog/commander-threat-assessment) usually points at the strongest board anyway.

### After the game

- **Debrief briefly.** "That felt a bit too fast for this group, I'll bring something slower next time" goes a long way.
- **Don't blame people for misjudging.** Judging your own deck's power is hard. Most mismatches are honest mistakes, not attempts to get an edge.

### When someone keeps misrepresenting their deck

Occasionally a player repeatedly calls a strong deck casual. Bring it up calmly and away from the table if you can. If it keeps happening, the group can ask that player to bring a different deck, or simply play at a different table. The point of Rule Zero is that everyone gets the game they agreed to.

## What Rule Zero isn't

- **It isn't a way to ban a card mid-game** because it just beat you.
- **It isn't a license to break rules on the fly.** Changes happen before the game, by agreement.
- **It isn't only for casual decks.** Competitive groups have the conversation too, often just confirming that everyone is bringing their strongest deck. The [cEDH guide](/blog/cedh-competitive-commander-guide) covers what that environment expects.

## The takeaway

Rule Zero is a short, honest conversation before a Commander game: describe your deck's plan, give a bracket and the details behind it, flag strategies that might surprise people and ask about house rules. Agree on changes before the game starts, and when decks don't line up, swap, lend or restart without blame. A table that talks first plays better games.
