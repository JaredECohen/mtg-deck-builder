Casual Magic variants change how a game is set up or played to make it more chaotic, more cooperative or more social. Planechase adds a shared deck of plane cards that alter the rules as players travel between them. Archenemy pits one player, armed with a deck of powerful schemes, against everyone else. Team variants like Emperor and Two-Headed Giant change who your allies are. None of them need a special deck of regular Magic cards: you bring the decks you already own and add a few rules on top.

This guide explains how each variant works, what kind of group it suits, and how to set one up at your table. If you're looking for the main competitive formats instead, the [formats overview](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) covers those.

## Why play a variant at all

Most Magic games are one player against another, or a free-for-all among four Commander players. Variants change the experience in a few ways:

- **They shake up stale matchups.** If your group always plays the same decks against each other, a variant makes familiar decks behave differently.
- **They reward adaptation.** When the rules shift mid-game, the player who adjusts fastest often wins, not just the one with the strongest deck.
- **They balance uneven decks.** Team and one-versus-many games let a strong deck face a group, which can make a lopsided pod fun again.
- **They're memorable.** The strange moments, a plane that doubles all damage or a scheme that wipes the board, are what people talk about after game night.

Most variants work with any decks, including Commander decks. If your group has a pre-game talk about power level and house rules, which the [rule zero guide](/blog/commander-rule-zero-guide) covers, include the variant in that conversation.

## Planechase

Planechase is a free-for-all variant where the game takes place on a series of planes. Each plane is an oversized card with an ongoing effect that changes the rules for everyone while it's face up, and a chaos ability that triggers when a player rolls the chaos symbol.

### Setting up Planechase

Each player brings a planar deck: a small deck of plane cards, at least ten of them, each with a different name. Alternatively, the whole group can share a single planar deck, which is easier when only one person owns plane cards. Some planar decks also include phenomenon cards, one-time events that resolve when encountered and then move the game on to the next plane. The rules cap how many phenomena a planar deck can contain.

At the start of the game, the first player's top plane card is turned face up. That's where everyone starts. Planes and phenomena aren't permanents and can't be targeted or destroyed; they just sit in the command zone and affect the game.

### The planar die

The planar die is a six-sided die with one face showing the planeswalker symbol, one face showing the chaos symbol, and four blank faces. On your turn, during your main phase, any time you could cast a sorcery, you may roll it:

- **Planeswalker symbol:** you planeswalk. The current plane goes to the bottom of its owner's planar deck, and the next plane is turned face up. This is how the group travels.
- **Chaos symbol:** the current plane's chaos ability triggers.
- **Blank:** nothing happens.

Your first roll each turn is free. Each additional roll that turn costs one more generic mana than the last: the second costs one mana, the third costs two, and so on. You can keep rolling as long as you can afford it.

### How Planechase plays

Planechase is chaotic by design. A plane might help one deck enormously and hurt another, so the player who benefits wants to stay and everyone else wants to roll. That tension creates decisions you never face in normal Magic: do you spend mana rolling to escape a plane that's killing you, or do you develop your board and hope someone else rolls? Do you roll to trigger a chaos ability that helps you, at the risk of planeswalking away from a plane you like?

The active player controls the current plane's effects, so the same plane's abilities shift from player to player as turns pass. Read every new plane out loud when it's revealed. Half the fun is the table reacting to what just changed.

Planechase suits groups who like big swings and don't mind randomness. It works especially well with Commander decks, since longer games mean more planes visited.

## Archenemy

Archenemy is one player against a team. The archenemy plays a normal deck plus a scheme deck, a stack of oversized cards with powerful effects. The rest of the players form a team and work together to defeat them.

### Setting up Archenemy

The archenemy needs a scheme deck of at least twenty schemes, with no more than two copies of any one scheme. The archenemy starts at 40 life to stand up to several opponents, while the team members start at their normal life total. The archenemy takes the first turn.

### Setting schemes in motion

At the beginning of the archenemy's first main phase each turn, they turn over the top card of their scheme deck. That's called setting the scheme in motion, and its effect happens right away. Schemes are things like dealing damage to each opponent, creating a group of creatures, or destroying something the team relies on.

There are two kinds:

- **One-shot schemes** happen once and go to the bottom of the scheme deck.
- **Ongoing schemes** stay face up and keep working until a condition on the card tells the archenemy to abandon them.

Like planes, schemes live in the command zone. The team can't counter or destroy them, only deal with what they create.

### How Archenemy plays

Archenemy is cooperative for the team, which makes it one of the best variants for mixed groups. Newer players can team up with experienced ones, discuss plays openly and learn by doing. The archenemy, meanwhile, gets to play the villain: a free scheme every turn means their deck can be modest and still feel powerful.

The team's main challenge is coordination. Because the archenemy gets an effect every turn, the team can't afford a long game. Decide early who's the main damage dealer and who's protecting, and focus attacks on the archenemy instead of spreading resources.

A popular twist gives every player a scheme deck and makes the game a free-for-all, sometimes called Supervillain Rumble. It's even more chaotic than Planechase.

## Team variants

Several variants use ordinary decks and change only who you're playing with and against.

### Two-Headed Giant

Two teams of two share a life total and take their turns together. It's the most widely played team variant and works for both Constructed and Limited. The [Two-Headed Giant guide](/blog/two-headed-giant-mtg) covers its rules and strategy in full.

### Emperor

Emperor is played by two teams of three. Each team sits in a row with its emperor in the middle and a general on each side. A team wins when the opposing emperor loses the game.

The key rule is range of influence: how far away you can affect other players with spells and abilities. In Emperor, generals usually have a range of one, so they can only reach the player sitting next to them, while the emperor has a range of two. The generals protect their emperor and attack the opposing generals, and the emperor plays a support role until the way to the other emperor is open.

Emperor rewards deck roles. A defensive deck makes a good emperor, while aggressive decks suit the general seats that do the early fighting.

### Star

Star is a five-player variant common in casual groups. Players sit in a circle, and each player's enemies are the two players not sitting next to them, while the players beside them are allies. You win when both of your enemies have lost. Because every alliance overlaps with someone else's, players cooperate one turn and compete the next. The structure suits five-color themes, with each player choosing one color and opposing its enemy colors on the color pie.

### Free-for-all and its tweaks

Standard multiplayer free-for-all, the format most Commander games use, is a variant too. Many groups add small modifications, such as only being allowed to attack the player to your left, which makes games faster and reduces pile-ons. [What is Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) covers the most popular way to play free-for-all.

## Choosing the right variant for your group

| Variant | Players | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planechase | 2 or more, usually 3-6 | Groups who enjoy randomness and big swings |
| Archenemy | 3 or more (one vs. team) | Mixed experience levels, one powerful deck |
| Two-Headed Giant | 4 | Friends who want to team up |
| Emperor | 6 | Large groups who like deck roles |
| Star | 5 | Five friends, especially with color themes |

Variants can also be combined. Planechase and Archenemy work together, and both work fine with Commander decks. Agree on the setup before the game begins, and keep a copy of the rules handy for the first game, since the edge cases come up quickly.

## Tips for a smooth variant night

- **Explain the variant before decks are chosen.** A deck that's fine in a free-for-all may be miserable in Archenemy.
- **Read every plane and scheme aloud.** Not everyone can see the card across the table.
- **Use a clear turn marker** in team games so everyone knows whose turn it is.
- **Keep it casual.** These variants are about the shared experience, not winning at all costs.

## The takeaway

Planechase adds a traveling set of rule-changing planes and a planar die. Archenemy gives one player a stack of schemes to battle a team. Emperor, Star and Two-Headed Giant reshape alliances using ordinary decks. Pick the variant that suits your group's size and taste, bring the decks you already have, and expect the most memorable games of the year.
