To build a 60-card Magic: The Gathering deck, start with a clear plan for how the deck wins, then choose the cards that do that job best, play four copies of the ones you most want to draw, shape a mana curve that lets you use your mana every turn, add enough lands to cast your spells on time, and balance threats with removal. Then test the deck and adjust it. The steps below walk through each of those decisions in order.

This guide is for constructed formats like Standard, Pioneer and Modern, where decks have at least 60 cards and up to four copies of each card other than basic lands. If you're building for Commander, the 100-card singleton rules change the approach enough that it needs its own guide.

## The rules you're building within

Before choosing cards, know the constraints that apply to most 60-card constructed formats:

- **At least 60 cards in the main deck.** There's no maximum, but you should almost always play exactly 60.
- **No more than four copies of any card,** except basic lands, which you can play in any number. Some cards have text that lets you break this rule, but they're rare.
- **An optional sideboard of up to 15 cards,** which you can swap with main deck cards between games of a match.
- **Only cards legal in your format.** Each format has its own card pool and banned list, so check the official list before you buy or build.

Why exactly 60? Every extra card makes it slightly less likely you'll draw your best cards. A 61st card is almost always weaker than the cards you already have, so it dilutes the deck.

## Step 1: Decide how the deck wins

Every good deck starts with a plan. Ask yourself: how does this deck win, and roughly when?

- **Aggro** wins fast by deploying cheap creatures and attacking before the opponent can stabilize.
- **Midrange** plays efficient threats and removal, aiming to trade well and win with stronger cards.
- **Control** answers everything the opponent does, then wins late with a few powerful threats.
- **Combo** assembles a specific combination of cards that wins the game or creates an overwhelming advantage.

If these terms are unfamiliar, the [deck archetypes guide](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) explains each one. Your plan guides every later decision: how many lands you need, what your curve looks like and how much removal you play.

Write down your plan in one sentence before you choose cards. "A red deck that wins with cheap creatures and burn spells by turn five" is a useful plan. "A deck with cool cards" isn't.

## Step 2: Choose your core cards

With a plan in mind, pick the cards that are central to it. These are the cards that do the most to advance your plan, and they're usually the ones you want in every game.

A good way to start:

1. **Pick your key cards.** These are the cards the deck is built around. In a burn deck, that might be Lightning Bolt and other efficient damage spells. In a control deck, it might be Counterspell and Wrath of God.
2. **Add support.** Cards that help your key cards work, protect them, or fill in the gaps.
3. **Cut anything off-plan.** If a card doesn't advance your plan or answer a threat that stops your plan, it probably doesn't belong.

Resist the urge to include every card you like. A focused deck that does one thing well beats a deck that does many things badly.

## Step 3: Decide your 4-ofs and singletons

In a 60-card deck, the number of copies of a card controls how often you'll see it. Four copies is the maximum, and it's the default for cards you want in most games.

A rough guide:

- **Four copies:** your most important cards, the ones you want in your opening hand or early in most games. Cheap, efficient spells that do your plan's main job are almost always four-ofs.
- **Three copies:** strong cards you want often, but not every game, or ones that get worse in multiples.
- **Two copies:** situational cards, expensive cards you don't want to draw early, or legendary cards where drawing a second copy is often a dead draw.
- **One copy:** a very specific answer, a single big finisher, or a card you can find with a search effect.

Beginners often build decks full of single copies, which makes every game play out differently and makes the deck less reliable. More copies of fewer cards make a deck more consistent. Consistency wins more games than variety.

The legend rule is worth remembering here. If you control two legendary permanents with the same name, you have to choose one and put the other into the graveyard, so playing four copies of a legendary card means duplicates can be dead draws. That's why many legendary cards are run as two- or three-ofs.

## Step 4: Shape your mana curve

Your mana curve is the number of cards at each mana value. A good curve lets you spend your mana efficiently every turn. The [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) covers this in depth, but the basic principles are:

- **Aggro decks** want most of their cards at one and two mana, with a few at three and very little above that.
- **Midrange decks** cluster around two to four mana.
- **Control decks** have a flatter curve with more cards at higher mana values, plus cheap interaction early.

A common mistake is too many expensive cards. A hand full of five- and six-drops can't do anything in the early turns, and you'll fall behind against faster decks. If your curve is top-heavy, cut some of the expensive cards for cheaper ones.

### Make sure you have early plays

Count how many cards you can play on turns one and two. If the answer is very low, your deck will often do nothing early. Even control decks want some cheap spells to interact with the opponent's early plays.

## Step 5: Set your land count

Lands are the most important cards in your deck. Without enough, you can't cast your spells. With too many, you draw lands instead of spells.

For a 60-card deck, typical land counts are:

- **Aggro and low-curve decks:** roughly 16 to 20 lands.
- **Midrange:** around 22 to 25.
- **Control and decks with a high curve:** 24 to 26.

These are starting points. Adjust based on your curve and how badly you need to hit land drops. If your deck has lots of cheap card draw, you can sometimes play slightly fewer lands. If it has many expensive spells, play more.

### Colours and mana base

If your deck uses more than one colour, think about how many sources of each colour you need. A card that costs `{U}{U}` needs more blue sources than a card that costs `{2}{U}`. As a rule of thumb, the more coloured mana symbols your early spells have, the more sources of that colour you need. One- and two-colour decks are much more reliable than three- or more-colour decks for beginners.

## Step 6: Balance threats and removal

A deck needs both ways to win and ways to stop the opponent from winning.

- **Threats** are the cards that win the game: creatures, planeswalkers, burn spells, or combo pieces.
- **Removal and interaction** are the cards that answer your opponent's threats: removal spells, counterspells, and effects that disrupt their plan.

The balance depends on your plan. An aggro deck plays mostly threats with a little removal to clear blockers. A control deck plays mostly removal and interaction with a few strong threats. A midrange deck is somewhere in between.

A common beginner mistake is building a deck with no removal at all. Even an aggressive creature deck needs a few ways to deal with an opposing creature it can't attack past.

### A sample framework

Here's a rough breakdown for a midrange 60-card deck:

| Category | Approximate count |
| --- | --- |
| Lands | 23 to 24 |
| Creatures and other threats | 18 to 22 |
| Removal and interaction | 8 to 12 |
| Card advantage and utility | 4 to 8 |

Adjust these to fit your plan. An aggro deck would push threats higher and lands lower. A control deck would push removal and lands higher.

## Step 7: Test the deck

Building a deck is only half the work. The real test is playing it.

- **Draw sample hands.** Shuffle and draw seven cards a few dozen times. Would you keep most of these hands? If not, your land count or curve may need work. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) explains how to judge opening hands.
- **Goldfish.** Play the deck against an imaginary opponent who does nothing. This shows how fast your deck can execute its plan.
- **Play real games.** Test against decks you expect to face. Note what goes well and what goes badly.

The [playtesting guide](/blog/how-to-playtest-a-deck) covers each of these methods in detail, including how to track results and avoid drawing conclusions from small samples.

### Change one thing at a time

When you adjust your deck, change a few cards at a time and test again. If you change twenty cards at once, you won't know which changes helped and which didn't.

### Watch for dead cards

If a card often sits in your hand doing nothing, it may be too expensive, too situational or simply not good enough. Replace it with something that advances your plan more directly.

## Common deck-building mistakes

- **Too many single copies.** Reduces consistency.
- **Too few lands.** One of the most common reasons beginner decks fail.
- **A top-heavy curve.** Leaves you without plays in the early turns.
- **No removal.** Leaves you unable to deal with opposing threats.
- **Too many colours.** Makes your mana unreliable.
- **No clear plan.** Makes every card choice harder and the deck less focused.

## The takeaway

Build a 60-card deck by starting with a clear plan, choosing the cards that best support it, and playing four copies of the ones you most want to draw. Shape a curve with plenty of early plays, set a land count that fits that curve, and balance threats with removal. Then test, adjust a few cards at a time, and repeat until the deck feels consistent.
