A prerelease is a casual Sealed event held at local game stores shortly before a new Magic: The Gathering set goes on sale. You buy into the event, open a kit of brand-new booster packs, build a 40-card deck from what you opened, and play a few rounds against other people doing the same thing. You don't need a collection, a deck, or tournament experience. You only need to know how to play.

This guide covers what happens at a prerelease, what to pack, how to build your deck in the time you're given, and the etiquette that keeps the event friendly. If Sealed itself is new to you, [Draft vs Sealed](/blog/draft-vs-sealed-limited-guide) explains how the format works before you go.

## What a prerelease actually is

Every new set gets a prerelease window, usually the weekend or so before the set's official release. Stores run events during that window, often several on different days and times, so you can pick one that fits your schedule.

The format is almost always Sealed. In Sealed, you open a fixed number of packs yourself and build only from those cards plus basic lands. Nobody passes you cards or takes them from you. That makes it a relaxed way to see a new set: you get to read every card you open, spread them out on the table, and think.

A few things make prereleases different from other events:

- **It's casual.** Prereleases are run as friendly events, not high-stakes tournaments. Judges and store staff expect new players and help them.
- **Everyone is new to the set.** Nobody has had months to learn the cards, so experienced players are figuring things out alongside you. That levels the field more than almost any other event.
- **You keep your cards.** Everything you open is yours to take home, whether you play it or not.
- **There's usually a prize structure.** Stores commonly hand out extra packs based on your record or simply for taking part. The details vary by store, so ask when you sign up.

Some prereleases use a variant, such as Two-Headed Giant, where you team up with a partner. The store's listing will say. This guide assumes standard one-on-one Sealed, which is the most common version.

## What to bring

The store provides the packs and, in most cases, basic lands for deckbuilding. Everything else is up to you. A small bag with the following covers almost every situation:

- **Card sleeves.** Brand-new cards scuff fast. One pack of sleeves, at least 45 or so, protects your deck and makes shuffling easier.
- **A deck box.** Somewhere to keep your deck and the rest of your pool separate.
- **Dice or counters.** You'll need to track +1/+1 counters, loyalty, and other markers. A handful of small dice works.
- **Life tracking.** A pen and paper, a spindown die, or a phone app. Pen and paper is the most reliable because you can check the history if there's a disagreement.
- **Token cards or markers.** Many sets make creature tokens. The kit sometimes includes some, but a few blank cards or spare dice help.
- **A playmat (optional).** Not required, but it keeps your cards off a sticky table.
- **Water and a snack.** Prereleases can run several hours with short breaks between rounds.

Check the store's page or social media before you go. Some stores ask you to preregister, some sell out, and some have their own rules about what time deckbuilding starts.

### Basic lands

Most stores have a box of basic lands for players to borrow or keep during the event. If you have your own basics, bring 20 or so of each color you might play. You won't know your colors until you open your packs, so bringing a few of every basic type is the safe choice.

## Building your deck at the event

When the event starts, you open your kit and have a set amount of time to build, usually somewhere around half an hour. That sounds short, but it's enough if you follow a simple process.

1. **Open everything and sort by color.** Make a pile for each color, one for multicolored cards, one for artifacts and colorless cards, and one for lands.
2. **Find your best cards.** Look for bombs, the rare or powerful cards that win the game on their own, and removal, the cards that kill or neutralize opposing creatures. These two categories usually decide your colors.
3. **Pick two colors.** Choose the two colors with the most good, playable cards, not the two colors with the single flashiest card.
4. **Count to 23.** Pick roughly 23 nonland cards from those two colors, leaning on creatures, and fill out a sensible [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) so you have plays on turns two, three, and four.
5. **Add 17 lands.** Split them by how many cards of each color you're playing.

That's the core of it. For judging individual cards you've never seen before, [how to evaluate cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) gives you a quick framework.

### Don't rush the registration

Some stores ask you to note which cards are in your deck, especially at larger events. Most prereleases don't, but if yours does, fill it in carefully. Ask staff if you're unsure what they need.

### Your sideboard is the rest of your pool

In Sealed, every card you opened but didn't put in your deck is your sideboard. Between games, you can swap cards in and out. If your opponent has a lot of flyers, you can bring in a creature with reach. If your deck isn't working at all, you can often switch colors entirely between matches. Check with the store about what's allowed, since rules can differ slightly by event.

## How the rounds work

After deckbuilding, the store pairs players for the first round. Pairings are often posted on a sheet or sent through an app the store uses. Each round is a match, typically best-of-three games, with a time limit. When the round ends, you report your result and wait for the next pairing.

A few basics:

- **Start by greeting your opponent and deciding who goes first.** A die roll or coin flip settles it for the first game.
- **Shuffle well and present your deck.** After shuffling, offer your deck to your opponent so they can cut or shuffle it. This is normal and not an accusation.
- **Keep track of time.** Rounds have a limit. Play at a steady pace and don't spend minutes on routine decisions.
- **Report results honestly.** If you win 2-1, report 2-1. Mistakes in reporting are easy to make, so double-check with your opponent.

If your first hand is weak, the [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) explains how to decide whether to keep it. In a 40-card deck, a hand with two to four lands is usually fine.

## Prerelease etiquette

Prereleases are social events. The players who have the best time are the ones who make the event pleasant for everyone else, too.

### At the table

- **Announce what you're doing.** Say what you're casting, what you're targeting, and when you're moving to combat. Clear communication prevents most disputes.
- **Let your opponent read your cards.** Everyone is seeing the set for the first time. If they ask to read a card, hand it over or turn it toward them.
- **Keep your play area tidy.** Lands in one row, creatures in front, graveyard in a clear pile. It helps both players see the board.
- **Don't pressure people.** If your opponent is new, give them time. If they make a mistake that hurts them, you're allowed to win, but there's no need to make them feel bad about it.

### Calling a judge

Calling a judge is normal and not rude. If you and your opponent disagree about a rule or something goes wrong, raise your hand and call for a judge or store staff. At a casual event, the goal is to fix the situation and keep playing, not to punish anyone.

### Trading and the store

Many players trade cards between rounds. Trade fairly, don't pressure anyone into a trade, and never handle someone else's cards without asking. Clean up your area when you leave, and thank the store staff. Prereleases are a lot of work to run.

## Having fun, win or lose

Your record at a prerelease matters a lot less than you might think. Here's how to get the most out of the day:

- **Treat it as a preview.** You're seeing the set before most players. Pay attention to which mechanics feel strong and which cards surprise you.
- **Learn from your losses.** After a game, ask your opponent what they thought of a play, or look at what beat you. Most players are happy to talk about it.
- **Try something.** Sealed rewards solid, consistent decks, but a prerelease is also a fine place to test a strange idea. The stakes are low.
- **Meet people.** The players you sit across from are your local Magic community. Many regular playgroups start at prereleases.

## The takeaway

Show up with sleeves, dice, and a pen. Open your packs, sort by color, build around your bombs and removal in two colors, and play 17 lands. Communicate clearly, let your opponent read your cards, and call a judge without hesitation if something goes wrong. Then enjoy being one of the first people to play with a brand-new set.
