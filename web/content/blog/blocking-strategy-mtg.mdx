In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), good blocking comes down to one question: is this block worth more than the life you'd lose by not making it? Block when the trade favors you or when the damage would really hurt, take the hit when your life total can afford it and your creatures are worth more on offense, and chump block only when the alternative is losing. Everything else in this guide is about how to weigh those choices in a real game.

This is a decision guide, so it assumes you know how combat works. If you need a refresher on the steps, how multiple blockers split damage or how first strike changes damage, start with [MTG combat explained](/blog/mtg-combat-explained). This guide is the mirror image of [when to attack](/blog/when-to-attack-mtg): the same math, seen from the defending side.

## Your life total is a resource

New players often block everything they can, as if every point of life were precious. It isn't. Life is a resource you spend to get something better, and at 20 life a few points of damage rarely matter.

Before you block, ask:

- **How fast is the opponent's clock?** Count how many turns it would take their board to kill you if you never blocked. If the answer is five or more turns, you have room to take hits.
- **What's my plan?** If you're the deck that wins the long game, protecting your life matters more. If you're the faster deck, keeping creatures alive to attack back may matter more.
- **What could happen next turn?** Burn spells, haste creatures and pump effects can turn a comfortable life total into a lethal one. Leave yourself a cushion against what the opponent's deck can do.

Understanding which role you're playing in the matchup makes these choices clearer. The guide to [who's the beatdown](/blog/whos-the-beatdown-mtg) explains how to figure that out.

## The basic block decisions

Every blocking choice falls into one of a few patterns.

### Good trades

A block is a good trade when your creature kills the attacker and survives, or when you trade a weaker card for a stronger one. Blocking a 3/3 attacker with a 4/4 is usually great, because your creature survives and theirs dies. Trading a 2/2 that cost two mana for a 3/2 that cost three is also a fine deal.

### Even trades

When both creatures die, the block is even on the board. Whether it's good depends on context:

- **Trade when you're behind** on the board or racing. Removing an attacker slows their clock.
- **Trade when you're the control player.** Your plan is to run them out of threats.
- **Hold back when your creature is more valuable** than theirs, for example a creature with a strong ability or one that's key to your plan.

### No block

Sometimes the best block is no block. Skip blocking when:

- The damage is small and your life total is healthy.
- Your creature is more useful attacking on your next turn.
- The attack looks like bait for a combat trick, covered below.
- Blocking would cost you a creature you need to block a bigger threat later.

## Chump blocks

A chump block is blocking with a creature that will die without killing the attacker, just to prevent the damage. Throwing a 1/1 in front of a 6/6 is a chump block.

Chump blocking trades a card for a turn of life. That's a bad deal most of the time, so save it for when it really matters:

- **When you'd otherwise die.** This is the clearest case. A chump block that keeps you alive is always worth it.
- **When you're about to win.** If you need one more turn to finish the game, a chump block buys that turn.
- **When the damage would put you in burn range.** Taking 6 and going to 3 against a red deck might be the same as losing.
- **When the chump blocker has value on dying.** A creature with a death trigger, or a token, loses you less when it chumps.

Tokens make the best chump blockers because they cost you the least. A deck that makes many small creatures can absorb a huge attacker for several turns.

### Trample makes chump blocks weaker

A trampler only has to assign lethal damage to the blocker, and the rest goes through. Chumping a 6/6 trampler with a 1/1 still lets 5 damage through, so you save just 1 life. Against trample, chump blocking rarely helps unless you're exactly at lethal.

## Double blocks

Double blocking means putting two or more creatures in front of one attacker so their combined damage kills it. Two 2/2s can block and kill a 4/4, and the attacker can kill at most one of them in return, sometimes two if its power is high enough.

Double blocks are strong but risky:

- **The upside:** you kill a creature that none of your creatures could kill alone, usually losing only one of yours.
- **The risk:** a combat trick can wreck it. If the opponent pumps the attacker or gives it first strike, you can lose both blockers and kill nothing. That's a two-for-one in their favor.

Under the current rules, the attacking player splits damage among multiple blockers however they like when damage is assigned, so assume they'll kill the blocker that matters most to you. Before double blocking, check the opponent's untapped mana and whether they have cards in hand. The more open mana they have, the more dangerous a double block becomes.

A double block is safest when you have a trick of your own, or when you're happy if the opponent uses their trick to save their creature rather than on something worse later.

## Deathtouch and first strike blockers

Some abilities change blocking math completely.

### Deathtouch

A creature with deathtouch destroys any creature it deals damage to. That makes even a tiny deathtouch creature a wall against huge attackers. A 1/1 with deathtouch blocking a 7/7 kills it.

- **On defense,** deathtouch creatures make opponents reluctant to attack at all. Leave one untapped and many attacks stop being worth it.
- **Against a deathtouch attacker,** any blocker you put in front of it dies. Double blocking a deathtouch attacker is especially bad, because 1 damage is lethal for each blocker, so it can kill several.
- **Deathtouch plus trample** is brutal: the attacker only needs to assign 1 damage to each blocker, and the rest tramples over.

Indestructible creatures ignore deathtouch, since deathtouch destroys and indestructible creatures can't be destroyed.

### First strike

A first-strike creature deals its damage before creatures without first strike. If that's enough to kill the attacker, the attacker never deals damage back.

- **A first-strike blocker** can block and kill attackers with toughness equal to or less than its power, and survive, as long as those attackers don't also have first strike.
- **First strike beats deathtouch** when the first striker kills the deathtouch creature first. A 2/2 first striker blocking a 1/1 deathtouch attacker kills it before the deathtouch damage happens.
- **Double strike** deals damage in both the first-strike step and the regular step, so it's effectively double power against blockers it doesn't kill in the first step.

## Reading combat tricks

When an opponent attacks into a board where the attack looks bad for them, they often have a reason. Learning to read those reasons is a big part of blocking well. The [combat tricks guide](/blog/combat-tricks-guide) covers the tricks themselves; here's how to spot them from the other side.

Warning signs:

- **A bad attack.** If they swing a 2/2 into your untapped 3/3, they probably have a pump spell, a removal spell or a reason to want you to block.
- **Untapped mana.** Count the mana they left open. Two open mana in a green deck often means a pump spell.
- **Cards in hand.** An opponent with no cards in hand can't have a trick, aside from abilities already on the battlefield.
- **They played nothing before combat.** Players who hold their mana through their main phase often plan to use it during combat.

When you read a trick, you have options:

- **Don't block,** and take the damage if you can afford it. The trick stays in their hand, but you keep your creature.
- **Block with a creature you don't mind losing,** so the trick is spent on a lesser target.
- **Block and respond** with your own instant if you have one.

None of this is certain. Sometimes a bad attack is just a bluff or a mistake. But if you call it wrong and lose a key creature, that's often worse than taking a few points.

## Blocking in multiplayer

In Commander and other multiplayer games, blocking has an extra layer. Every creature you lose blocking one opponent is a creature that can't defend you against the others. Chump blocks are more costly, and taking damage is often fine when other players are also under pressure. Attackers usually target whoever looks weakest, so a strong untapped defense discourages attacks before they happen.

## The takeaway

Treat your life total as a resource, and block only when the block is worth more than the damage. Take good trades, think about even trades in the context of who's ahead, and save chump blocks for when you'd otherwise lose. Be careful double blocking into open mana, respect deathtouch and first strike, and when an attack looks too good to be true, assume a trick. Blocking well wins as many games as attacking well.
