Oathbreaker is a casual, multiplayer Magic: The Gathering format where a planeswalker leads your deck instead of a legendary creature. Alongside that planeswalker, called your Oathbreaker, you choose a signature spell: an instant or sorcery that sits in the command zone with it and can be cast whenever your Oathbreaker is on the battlefield. Decks are singleton, follow colour identity rules much like Commander, and are smaller and faster than a typical Commander deck.

This guide explains how the format works, how the signature spell and command zone rules play out, and how to approach building your first Oathbreaker deck. Oathbreaker is a community-run format with its own rules committee and its own banned list, separate from Wizards of the Coast's formats, so treat the official Oathbreaker website as the final word on exact rules and banned cards.

## Oathbreaker at a glance

If you know [Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh), Oathbreaker will feel familiar. The big differences are who leads the deck and how much smaller everything is.

| | Commander | Oathbreaker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leader | A legendary creature (or a card that says it can be your commander) | Any planeswalker |
| Second command zone card | None by default | A signature spell (instant or sorcery) |
| Deck size | 100 cards | 60 cards, counting the Oathbreaker and signature spell |
| Starting life | 40 | 20 |
| Copies | One of each card, except basic lands | One of each card, except basic lands |
| Colour identity | Set by the commander | Set by the Oathbreaker |
| Typical players | Three to four | Three to four |

Those numbers reflect the format as its organizers have long published it. Because the format is maintained by its own committee, check the official site before a tournament or a new playgroup in case anything has been updated.

## The Oathbreaker

Your Oathbreaker is a planeswalker card that starts the game in the command zone. You can cast it from there whenever you could normally cast it, which for most planeswalkers means during your main phase when the stack is empty.

Once it's on the battlefield, it works like any planeswalker. It enters with loyalty, you can activate one of its loyalty abilities each turn at sorcery speed, and it can be attacked. Damage dealt to it removes loyalty counters, and it goes to the graveyard when its loyalty reaches zero. If you're not comfortable with how planeswalkers work, [the planeswalkers guide](/blog/mtg-planeswalkers-explained) covers loyalty, attacking and abilities.

When your Oathbreaker would leave the battlefield, it can return to the command zone instead, just like a commander. Any planeswalker can be an Oathbreaker. The card doesn't need special text giving it permission, which is one of the format's appeals: almost every planeswalker ever printed is a possible leader.

## The signature spell

The signature spell is what makes Oathbreaker distinct. When you build your deck, you choose one instant or sorcery card to be your signature spell. It starts the game in the command zone alongside your Oathbreaker.

The rules that govern it:

- **It must be an instant or sorcery** within your Oathbreaker's colour identity.
- **You can only cast it while your Oathbreaker is on the battlefield.** If your planeswalker is in the command zone or has been removed, the signature spell is locked away.
- **It returns to the command zone.** When the signature spell resolves, or would go to another zone such as the graveyard or exile, you can put it back into the command zone, ready to cast again.
- **It has its own tax.** Each time you cast it from the command zone, it costs an additional `{2}` for each previous time you cast it from there this game. That tax is tracked separately from your Oathbreaker's.

This creates the format's core tension. Your signature spell is a reusable resource, but only while your planeswalker survives. Opponents who want to shut down your best spell have a clear target: attack or remove your Oathbreaker.

### Choosing a signature spell

A good signature spell does one of three things:

- **Protects your Oathbreaker.** Spells that remove attackers, counter removal or wipe the board help keep your planeswalker alive, which keeps the spell available.
- **Feeds your Oathbreaker's plan.** A spell that draws cards, makes tokens or deals damage can combine with your planeswalker's abilities.
- **Does something powerful on repeat.** Even a simple effect gets strong when you can cast it again and again.

For example, a blue Oathbreaker might pair with Counterspell to protect itself, or a red one with Lightning Bolt for cheap, reusable removal. Because the tax rises by `{2}` each cast, cheap spells usually work best. A one-mana spell is still affordable on its third or fourth cast. An expensive one quickly isn't.

## Commander tax for both cards

Your Oathbreaker follows the same tax rule as the signature spell. Each time you cast it from the command zone, it costs `{2}` more for each previous time you cast it from there. The two taxes are separate. Recasting your Oathbreaker doesn't raise the cost of your signature spell, and vice versa.

In practice, this means the first time your Oathbreaker is removed, recasting it is a reasonable investment. By the third time, it may be better to spend your mana elsewhere and win another way.

## Singleton and colour identity

Like Commander, Oathbreaker is a singleton format: apart from basic lands, you can only include one copy of each card in your deck, counting your Oathbreaker and signature spell.

Your deck's colour identity is set by your Oathbreaker. A card's colour identity includes the coloured mana symbols in its cost and in its rules text, so a card can be outside your identity even if it would be castable with your lands. Every card in your deck, including the signature spell, must fit within that identity. A mono-red Oathbreaker means a mono-red deck. A planeswalker with both white and blue mana symbols allows white, blue and colourless cards.

This makes your choice of planeswalker the most important deck-building decision, more so than in Commander, because there are relatively few multicoloured planeswalkers compared with multicoloured legendary creatures.

## How games play out

Oathbreaker games are usually shorter than Commander games. Smaller decks, lower starting life and cheaper leaders mean the format moves quickly, which is a big part of its appeal for people who want multiplayer games without a long time commitment.

A few patterns show up often:

- **Early Oathbreakers draw attention.** A planeswalker cast on turn three or four becomes a target right away. Players who let one keep activating for several turns tend to regret it.
- **Attacking planeswalkers matters.** Creature combat is often aimed at Oathbreakers rather than players, because removing a planeswalker also turns off its signature spell.
- **Politics still matter.** It's a multiplayer format, so assessing who is the biggest threat is as important as in Commander. Threat assessment from Commander carries over directly.
- **Removal for planeswalkers is premium.** Spells that can hit any permanent, or that specifically handle planeswalkers, are much better than in creature-focused formats.

## Building your first Oathbreaker deck

### Start with the planeswalker

Pick a planeswalker whose abilities you want to use every game. Planeswalkers that protect themselves, for example by making creature tokens or removing threats with their minus abilities, tend to survive longer. Ones with powerful plus abilities can grind value over a long game.

### Pick a signature spell that fits

Choose a cheap instant or sorcery that either protects your Oathbreaker or advances its plan. Think about what you'd want to cast multiple times in one game.

### Fill out the rest

With a 60-card deck, a rough starting framework looks like this:

- **About 20 to 24 lands.** The format is low-curve, but missing land drops is still a quick way to lose.
- **Some ramp and card draw.** A few cheap mana rocks or ramp spells, and a handful of ways to draw cards.
- **Removal and protection.** Plenty of ways to handle creatures attacking your planeswalker, plus answers to opposing Oathbreakers.
- **Cards that support your plan.** Spells and creatures that work with your planeswalker's abilities.

These are starting points, not rules. Adjust them as you play, and keep your mana curve low. Smaller decks and lower life totals reward efficiency.

### Mind the banned list

Oathbreaker has its own banned list, maintained by its rules committee and distinct from Commander's. Check the official site for the current version before building, since cards that are fine in Commander can be banned in Oathbreaker and vice versa.

## Oathbreaker vs other casual formats

Oathbreaker sits between Commander and smaller formats. Compared with Commander, it's faster, quicker to build and centred on planeswalkers. Compared with [Brawl](/blog/mtg-brawl-format-guide), a singleton format played mostly on MTG Arena and led by a legendary creature or planeswalker, Oathbreaker adds the signature spell mechanic, requires a planeswalker as the leader, and is designed mainly for paper multiplayer tables. For a broader comparison of every major format, see [the MTG formats guide](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).

## The takeaway

Oathbreaker is Commander's quicker cousin: a planeswalker leads your singleton deck, a reusable signature spell sits beside it, and the whole game revolves around keeping your planeswalker alive. Pick a planeswalker you love, pair it with a cheap spell worth casting again and again, build a lean deck around them, and check the official Oathbreaker site for the latest rules before you sit down.
