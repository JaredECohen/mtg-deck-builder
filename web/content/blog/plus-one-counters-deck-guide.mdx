In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), a +1/+1 counters deck is built around putting counters on creatures over and over, then multiplying and rewarding them. Each counter makes a creature permanently bigger, and the deck stacks cards that add extra counters, copy counters onto more permanents, or pay you off for having them. The result is a board that grows faster than the opponent can keep up with, and creatures that each demand an answer.

The archetype is a synergy deck at heart. Individually, many of its cards are modest. Together, a two-mana creature that would normally stay small can be a 6/6 by the midgame. This guide covers how the pieces fit, the key cards, and how the deck changes between Commander and 60-card formats.

## How +1/+1 counters work, briefly

A +1/+1 counter is a marker placed on a creature that gives it +1/+1 for as long as it stays there. Counters aren't "until end of turn" effects. They stay until something removes them or the creature leaves the battlefield.

A few rules shape the archetype:

- **Counters leave with the permanent.** If a creature dies, is exiled or returns to hand, its counters are gone. Coming back later, it starts fresh.
- **+1/+1 and -1/-1 counters cancel out.** If a creature has both, pairs of them are removed as a state-based action. That makes -1/-1 effects a natural enemy of this deck.
- **Counters are tracked per creature.** Moving counters around or copying them is what many of the deck's synergy cards do.

That's enough to build with. The [counters rules guide](/blog/mtg-counters-explained) covers the full rules for every counter type; the rest of this guide is strategy.

## The building blocks

Counters decks usually have four kinds of cards.

| Role | What it does | Classic examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sources | Put +1/+1 counters on creatures | Creatures with evolve, Walking Ballista, Ajani's Pridemate |
| Multipliers | Increase how many counters you get | Hardened Scales, Winding Constrictor, Corpsejack Menace, Doubling Season |
| Spreaders | Add counters to more permanents | Proliferate cards like Atraxa, Praetors' Voice |
| Payoffs | Reward you for having counters | Card draw for creatures with counters, protection, evasion |

### Sources: getting counters in the first place

Anything that puts a counter on a creature is a source. Some of the most common:

- **Evolve.** A creature with evolve gets a +1/+1 counter whenever a creature with greater power or toughness enters under your control. A curve of cheap evolve creatures grows steadily as you play bigger ones.
- **Creatures that enter with counters.** Walking Ballista enters with X +1/+1 counters, and you can pay `{4}` to add another. Its other ability removes a counter to deal 1 damage to any target, which makes extra counters directly useful.
- **Repeatable triggers.** Ajani's Pridemate gets a counter whenever you gain life, which is why counters and [lifegain decks](/blog/lifegain-deck-guide) overlap so often.
- **Modular.** Arcbound Ravager and other modular creatures enter with counters and can pass them to another artifact creature when they die.

The best sources are cheap and repeatable. A source you can trigger every turn does far more over a game than one that adds a single large burst.

### Multipliers: the engine

Multipliers are the cards that make the archetype explosive. They change how many counters you get each time a source fires.

- **Hardened Scales** is a one-mana enchantment: whenever one or more +1/+1 counters would be put on a creature you control, that many plus one are put on it instead.
- **Winding Constrictor** does something similar for any kind of counter on your artifacts and creatures, and also for counters you'd get yourself.
- **Corpsejack Menace** doubles the +1/+1 counters put on your creatures.
- **Doubling Season** doubles counters placed on permanents you control and also doubles tokens you'd create.

"Plus one" effects are best with sources that add counters one at a time, because each event gets the bonus. Doubling effects are best with sources that add many at once. Two multipliers together compound: with both Hardened Scales and a doubler, the order in which the effects apply affects the final number, and you choose that order as the controller of the affected creature.

Multipliers on their own don't do anything, so don't overload on them. A deck that draws three multipliers and no sources has a hand full of blanks.

### Spreaders: proliferate

Proliferate is a keyword action: you choose any number of permanents and players that have counters, and give each one another counter of each kind already there. One proliferate can add a +1/+1 counter to every creature you control that already has one, which in a developed board is a big swing.

A few things to know about proliferate:

- **You choose what to proliferate.** You can skip your opponent's permanents or players you don't want to help.
- **It works with every kind of counter.** Loyalty counters on planeswalkers, poison counters on opponents and charge counters on artifacts all count. That's why proliferate decks often mix counter types.
- **It only adds to things that already have counters.** You need sources first.

Atraxa, Praetors' Voice is the best-known proliferate card. It has flying, vigilance, deathtouch and lifelink, and proliferates at the beginning of your end step. It's also one of the most popular Commander picks for the archetype.

### Payoffs: turning counters into wins

Once your creatures have counters, payoffs reward that. Common payoff effects include:

- **Card draw** for each creature with a +1/+1 counter, which refills your hand after a big turn.
- **Evasion or trample** for creatures with counters, so your bigger creatures connect.
- **Protection** for creatures with counters, which helps against removal and board wipes.
- **Damage** from removing counters, as Walking Ballista does.

Big creatures alone can be a payoff. If your board outgrows the opponent's, you can attack through it. But a card or two that turns counters into card draw or evasion makes the deck much harder to stop.

## The archetype's weaknesses

Counters decks put a lot of resources into a few creatures, which creates predictable problems.

1. **Board wipes.** A single Wrath of God destroys every counter you've accumulated. Plan for it with protection, recursion, or by not overcommitting.
2. **Exile and bounce.** Returning a creature to hand or exiling it removes its counters for good. Even cheap bounce can be a large tempo loss.
3. **-1/-1 counters.** These cancel your +1/+1 counters, and some cards add them to many creatures at once.
4. **Chump blocks.** A huge creature without evasion can be blocked by a 1/1 token every turn. Trample and evasion matter.
5. **Thin, clunky draws.** Multipliers and payoffs without sources do nothing. Build with a high source count.

## Commander vs. 60-card versions

The archetype exists in almost every format, but it plays quite differently depending on the deck size and the number of opponents.

### Commander

In Commander, counters decks are usually built around a commander that adds or spreads counters, which guarantees access to the engine every game. Atraxa, Praetors' Voice and Ezuri, Claw of Progress are long-standing choices, and Vorinclex, Monstrous Raider, which doubles counters you put on permanents and players, is a powerful multiplier in green.

Commander versions tend to:

- **Play more multipliers and spreaders,** because games are longer and there's time to set up.
- **Mix counter types.** Proliferate works on loyalty and poison counters too, so many lists include planeswalkers or other counter themes.
- **Run more protection and recursion,** since four-player games feature more board wipes.

Singleton rules mean you'll only have one copy of each card, so redundancy comes from playing many cards that do similar things. The [first Commander deck guide](/blog/how-to-build-your-first-commander-deck) covers the template for slots like ramp, removal and draw.

### 60-card formats

In 60-card decks, the archetype is leaner and faster. Four copies of key cards mean you can count on drawing a multiplier, so the deck can be more focused.

60-card versions tend to:

- **Run a low curve of cheap sources** that grow quickly.
- **Use only the best multipliers.** Hardened Scales and Winding Constrictor are both cheap, which matters in faster formats.
- **Pair counters with another theme.** Artifacts with modular, lifegain with Ajani's Pridemate, or tokens all combine well with counters. Hardened Scales has been paired with Arcbound Ravager and Walking Ballista in Modern for years.

Counters also pair naturally with [token decks](/blog/mtg-tokens-deck-guide): proliferate and "each creature you control" effects get more value from a wide board, and a doubler like Doubling Season works for both.

## Building a counters deck

A practical outline for a 60-card counters deck:

- **14-18 sources,** mostly creatures that cost one to three mana.
- **4-8 multipliers,** cheap ones first.
- **2-6 spreaders or payoffs** that reward a developed board.
- **4-8 interaction spells** so you don't lose to a faster deck.
- **A land count that matches your low curve.**

For Commander, scale the same idea up: plenty of sources, a deep bench of multipliers and proliferate, then the usual ramp, removal and card draw. Build in protection so a single board wipe doesn't end your game.

## The takeaway

A +1/+1 counters deck wins by stacking small advantages until they're overwhelming: sources put counters down, multipliers increase them, proliferate spreads them, and payoffs turn a big board into cards and damage. Keep your source count high, respect board wipes and bounce, and give your big creatures a way through blockers.
