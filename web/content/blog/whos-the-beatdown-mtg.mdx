In almost every game of Magic: The Gathering, one player should be the beatdown, pushing damage and trying to end the game quickly, and the other should be the control, trading resources and trying to win the long game. The beatdown is the player whose deck gets worse as the game goes on. Working out which role is yours, and playing it on purpose, is one of the biggest skill jumps a player can make.

The idea was popularized by a classic strategy article from the late 1990s, and it has held up because it explains a mistake every player makes: trying to do both jobs at once and doing neither well.

## What "beatdown" and "control" mean here

These are roles in a specific matchup, not deck labels. Your deck has an archetype, like aggro, midrange or control (the [deck archetypes guide](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) covers those). Your role is what that deck should be doing against the particular opponent in front of you.

- **The beatdown** wants the game to be short. It spends cards and life to deal damage, races instead of trading, and treats every turn the game goes on as a turn its opponent gets closer to taking over.
- **The control** wants the game to be long. It trades off threats, blocks, protects its life total and waits for the point where its better cards, more powerful late game or card advantage take over.

The key question is simple: **if this game goes long, who wins?** The player who wins a long game is the control. The other player is the beatdown, because their only path to victory is to win before the long game arrives.

## How to assign roles in a matchup

You can usually answer the question before the first turn by comparing the two decks on a few axes.

### Compare the late games

Which deck has the more powerful things to do at six or seven mana? Which one draws more cards, or has threats that are hard to answer? The deck with the stronger late game is usually the control. A deck full of two-mana creatures and burn has a weak late game no matter what it faces, so it's almost always the beatdown.

### Compare the early games

Which deck has the faster, more aggressive start? If your opponent can deal damage faster than you, and your cards are individually bigger, you're likely the control even if your deck is "aggressive" on paper.

### Look at card quality versus card quantity

Decks that win by getting more cards than the opponent, through card draw or two-for-one effects, want long games. Decks that win by using each card efficiently on tempo want short ones.

### Account for interaction

A deck with lots of removal and counterspells can play control against creature decks. Against a deck with few targets for that removal, the same cards are weak, and the interactive deck may need to become the beatdown.

## Examples of role assignment

### Aggro vs control

The easy case. A red deck with Goblin Guide and Lightning Bolt against a blue-white deck with Counterspell and Wrath of God: red is the beatdown, blue-white is the control. Red wants to deal 20 damage before the sweepers and card advantage take over. Blue-white wants to survive the early turns and grind red out of cards.

Nobody gets this one wrong. The interesting cases are the ones in the middle.

### Midrange vs aggro

A green-black midrange deck with efficient removal and four-mana threats plays against a fast aggro deck. The midrange deck's creatures are bigger, its removal lines up well, and its late game is better. So against aggro, midrange is the **control**. It should block, trade, use removal on early creatures and stabilize, not race.

### Midrange vs control

Take the same green-black deck and put it across from a deck full of counterspells, sweepers and card draw. Now the opponent has the better late game. Midrange becomes the **beatdown**. It should deploy threats early, apply pressure and force the control player to have answers on time, because every extra turn favors the control deck.

That's the heart of role assignment. The same 60 cards play opposite roles depending on the opponent.

### The mirror match

When two similar decks meet, roles often come down to small differences. Who's on the play? Who has the faster draw this game? Who has more card advantage in their list? In aggro mirrors, the player on the draw with a slower hand often becomes the control by default, blocking and trading to blunt the faster start. In control mirrors, the player with more threats or more ways to pressure is often the beatdown, since a game where both players just hold up counterspells tends to favor whoever has more of them.

### Combo matchups

Combo decks usually want to execute their plan before the opponent can stop it, so they often behave as the beatdown, even if they never attack. A slow, interactive deck facing combo can't afford a passive game either: if the combo deck will eventually assemble its pieces, the interactive deck has to pressure it while holding up disruption. That's why disruptive aggro decks are often good against combo. Clocking them while interfering with them is a role both decks feel.

## How roles change mid-game

Roles are assigned before the game, but they're reassessed every turn. A few things flip them.

### The board changes

If the control player falls far behind early, they may not survive to the late game they were counting on. At that point, racing might be their best chance. Likewise, an aggressive player who gets a big creature stuck in play against an opponent with no answer can switch to holding back and grinding.

### Life totals change

Life is a resource for the beatdown and a lifeline for the control. If you're at 4 life against a deck with burn spells, you're the control in that moment no matter what you planned. Every point of life matters, and attacking with a creature you need as a blocker is how you lose. If your opponent is at 3 and you have reach, you're the beatdown, even if you started the game as the control.

### Hands change

A control player who draws a sweeper and three more lands is in great shape to play the long game. A control player who keeps a hand of answers and then draws only lands while the opponent plays threats has to adapt. Count your cards and your opponent's cards. The player who is going to run out first usually wants a shorter game.

### Sideboarding changes the matchup

After game one, both players can sideboard. The control deck might bring in more removal and cheap blockers to beat aggro more reliably. The aggro deck might bring in cards that punish control's sweepers or counters. That can shift roles: a midrange deck that was the beatdown in game one can bring in its own late-game cards and become the control. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers how to plan those changes, and role assignment is a good lens for them. Ask what role you want in post-board games and choose cards that support it.

## Playing your role well

Once you know your role, many individual decisions become easier.

### As the beatdown

- **Point burn at the face** when the race is close. Lightning Bolt aimed at the opponent is 3 of the 20 damage you need. Aim it at a blocker only when that creature stops more than 3 damage over the next few turns.
- **Attack when trades are acceptable.** Trading a two-drop for a blocker is fine if it clears the way for more damage.
- **Commit to the board.** Holding creatures back for fear of a sweeper only helps the control player if you give them time. Sometimes you should play around a sweeper by deploying just enough threats; often you should just make them have it.
- **Mulligan for speed.** Against control, a hand with early pressure is worth more than a hand of expensive cards. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) shows how matchup changes what you keep.

### As the control

- **Trade early and often.** One-for-one trades favor you because your later cards are better.
- **Protect your life total.** It's your resource for buying time. Block when it preserves enough life to reach your late game.
- **Don't race.** Attacking with your only blocker to deal 3 damage when your opponent is threatening 5 is how controls lose to beatdown decks.
- **Use removal on the creatures that threaten you most,** not the ones that look scariest in the long run. Survive first, then win.

## Common role-assignment mistakes

1. **Playing to your archetype instead of your matchup.** "My deck is aggro, so I attack." Not if your opponent's deck is faster than yours. Ask who wins the long game, not what your deck is called.
2. **Trying to do both jobs.** Attacking with half your creatures and holding back the other half often means you deal too little damage to pressure your opponent and keep too few blockers to stop theirs. Commit to a role.
3. **Not updating after the board changes.** You planned to be the control, but you're at 5 life and your opponent has two cards in hand. Or you planned to be the beatdown, but your opponent just stabilized with a creature you can't get through. Re-ask the question each turn.
4. **Racing when you can't win the race.** Count damage. If they kill you a turn before you kill them, racing loses. Block or remove something instead.
5. **Chump blocking too early as the control.** Life is a resource. Spending a creature to prevent 2 damage when you're at 18 trades a card for something you didn't need yet.
6. **Holding back as the beatdown because of "value."** The beatdown doesn't care about value. Every turn you hold back is a turn the control player gets closer to winning.

## Roles in Commander and multiplayer

In multiplayer games, the beatdown and control idea still applies, but there's more than one opponent and every player's role relative to the others shifts. A player with a fast, aggressive start may be the beatdown against the whole table, while a player with a slow, powerful deck wants everyone to fight each other. Deciding whom to pressure and when to hold back is the core of [Commander threat assessment](/blog/commander-threat-assessment).

## The takeaway

Before each game, and on every important turn, ask: if this goes long, who wins? If it's you, play the control: trade, block, protect your life and wait. If it's your opponent, play the beatdown: pressure, race and end it before their late game arrives. The same deck can play either role, and playing the right one usually matters more than any single card.
