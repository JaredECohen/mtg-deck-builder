A Magic: The Gathering (MTG) mana curve is the count of cards in your deck at each mana value: how many cost one, how many cost two, and so on. A good curve lets you spend all or most of your mana every turn, which is one of the biggest reasons some decks feel smooth and others feel clunky. Building a sensible curve, and pairing it with the right number of lands, is one of the simplest ways to make any deck better.

## What a mana curve is

Every card has a **mana value**: the total amount of mana in its cost, counting colored and generic symbols alike. Lightning Bolt (`{R}`) has a mana value of 1. Counterspell (`{U}{U}`) has a mana value of 2. Wrath of God (`{2}{W}{W}`) has a mana value of 4. Lands have a mana value of 0.

If you sort the nonland cards in your deck into piles by mana value and line them up, you get a bar chart. That's the curve. Most decks have a hump shape: few cards at the very top, most cards in the low-to-middle range. The position of the hump tells you a lot about the deck. An aggro deck's hump sits at 1 and 2. A control deck's is flatter and further right.

Two related terms:

- **Curving out** means playing a card that uses all your mana on each early turn: a one-drop on turn one, a two-drop on turn two, a three-drop on turn three.
- **Average mana value** is the total mana value of your nonland cards divided by how many there are. It's a rough single-number summary of how fast your deck is.

## Why the curve matters

You usually get one land per turn, which means one more mana per turn. Mana you don't spend on a turn is gone. A deck that can't use its mana early falls behind on board, and a deck with too many cheap cards can run out of things to do later.

Picture two decks facing off. One plays a two-drop on turn two, a three-drop on turn three and a four-drop on turn four. The other has a hand full of five- and six-mana cards and does nothing until turn five. Even if the second deck's cards are individually stronger, it may already be too far behind to use them.

That's the core trade-off: cheap cards come down early but are usually less powerful; expensive cards are powerful but come down late. Your curve is how you balance the two to match your deck's plan.

## Typical curves by archetype

These ranges are for 60-card constructed decks and are meant as starting points. Exact numbers vary with format and card choices. The [archetypes guide](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) explains each game plan in more depth.

| Archetype | Where the curve peaks | Highest common cards | Lands (60 cards) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aggro | 1 and 2 mana | Few or no cards above 3 | 16-20 |
| Midrange | 2 and 3 mana | Some 4s and a few 5s | 22-25 |
| Control | Flat across 2-4 | A few finishers at 5+ | 24-26 |

**Aggro** wants to deploy threats from turn one and keep spending all its mana. A typical aggro deck has the majority of its spells at one and two mana.

**Midrange** wants a play every turn from two through four or five. Its curve is fuller in the middle, with a few powerful cards at the top.

**Control** cares less about using mana on turns one and two, and more about hitting every land drop so it can cast answers and then its expensive finishers. Its curve is flatter, and its land count is higher.

## How many lands should you play?

Land count and curve go together. The higher your curve, the more lands you need to cast your spells on time.

- **60-card constructed:** roughly 16 to 20 lands for aggro and lower-curve decks, around 22 to 25 for midrange, and 24 to 26 for control.
- **40-card Limited** (draft and sealed): usually 17 lands. Fast, low-curve decks sometimes go to 16, and decks with lots of expensive cards sometimes go to 18.
- **100-card Commander:** around 36 to 38 lands, plus additional ramp. The [Commander deck guide](/blog/how-to-build-your-first-commander-deck) covers that template in full.

A common beginner error is cutting lands to fit more spells. Missing land drops loses more games than drawing an extra land, especially in slower decks. If you're unsure, err a land toward more rather than fewer. The [beginner mistakes guide](/blog/mtg-beginner-mistakes) covers this and other common traps.

## Smoothing your draws: cheap card draw and mana sinks

Two kinds of cards make your curve more forgiving.

**Cheap card selection** helps you find lands when you need them and spells when you have enough. One-mana spells like **Opt** and **Preordain** let you look at the top of your library and draw a card. Decks that play several of these can often run a land or two fewer, because they dig past bad draws.

**Mana sinks** are cards that turn extra mana into value. A creature with an activated ability you can use repeatedly, a spell with kicker, or a card with cycling all give you something to do with excess lands. **Figure of Destiny** is a classic example: a one-mana creature that can spend more and more mana to grow into a large threat. Mana sinks make flooding (drawing too many lands) much less painful, which lets you safely play a slightly higher land count.

## Colored mana: the curve's hidden half

The curve counts *how much* mana your cards cost. You also have to check *which colors* that mana must be.

A card with two colored symbols, like Counterspell (`{U}{U}`), is harder to cast on curve than a card with one, like Mana Leak (`{1}{U}`). In a two-color deck, a turn-two spell with two symbols of the same color needs that color from both of your first two lands. Some things to check:

- **Count your colored sources.** For each color, count how many lands (and other mana sources) produce it. Your main color should have most of your sources.
- **Watch early double-pip cards.** Cheap cards with two or more colored symbols want a deck heavily weighted toward that color.
- **Put the demanding colors early.** If your splash color only appears on a couple of expensive cards, you have more turns to find a source for it.
- **Use dual lands where you can.** Lands that produce two colors make every color requirement easier to meet.

A deck can have a perfect curve and still stumble if half its two-drops need a color it rarely draws on turn two.

## A worked example: counting a curve

Let's count a curve for a 60-card mono-red aggro deck. It has 20 lands and 40 nonland cards:

| Mana value | Number of cards | Mana value total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 14 | 14 |
| 2 | 14 | 28 |
| 3 | 10 | 30 |
| 4 | 2 | 8 |
| **Total** | **40** | **80** |

**Step 1: Sort.** Ignore lands and put each nonland card in a pile by mana value.

**Step 2: Count each pile.** Here: 14 one-drops, 14 two-drops, 10 three-drops, 2 four-drops.

**Step 3: Find the average.** Multiply each mana value by its card count and add them up: 14 + 28 + 30 + 8 = 80. Divide by 40 nonland cards: an average mana value of 2.0.

**Step 4: Read it.** The hump sits squarely at 1 and 2, with almost nothing above 3. That's a classic aggro curve, and an average of 2.0 supports a lower land count. If this deck had six or seven four-drops, it would need more lands and would play more like midrange.

Now compare a control deck with 26 lands and 34 nonland cards: perhaps 4 one-drops, 10 two-drops, 8 three-drops, 6 four-drops, 3 five-drops and 3 six-drops. That's 4 + 20 + 24 + 24 + 15 + 18 = 105, divided by 34 is about 3.1. The higher average is exactly why it needs more lands.

## Practical rules of thumb

- **Have early plays.** Most decks want a meaningful number of cards they can cast on turn two.
- **Cap the top end.** Every expensive card is one you can't cast early. Keep only the ones that win games.
- **Match lands to curve.** The higher your average mana value, the more lands you need.
- **Count colored sources, not just lands.**
- **Use selection and sinks** to make floods and screws hurt less.
