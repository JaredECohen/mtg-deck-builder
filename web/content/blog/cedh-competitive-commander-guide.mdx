cEDH, short for competitive EDH, is Commander, the Magic: The Gathering multiplayer format, played with one goal: win the game as efficiently as possible. It uses the same rules and card pool as any other Commander game, but every player builds the strongest deck they can and plays to win, so games are fast, full of interaction, and often end with a combo rather than combat. In the official Commander bracket system, cEDH is bracket 5.

This guide explains what makes cEDH different from casual Commander, why fast mana and interaction define it, what games actually feel like, and the table etiquette competitive players expect. If you're new to Commander itself, read [what is Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) first.

## Where cEDH fits in the bracket system

The official Commander brackets sort decks into five levels so players can match expectations before a game. Brackets 1 through 4 describe a range from theme-first decks to fully optimized ones. Bracket 5 is cEDH.

The important detail is that bracket 5 isn't defined by a stricter card pool. A bracket 4 deck and a bracket 5 deck can use the same cards. The difference is **intent**: at a cEDH table, everyone agrees that the aim is to win, that nobody is holding back, and that decks are tuned with the competitive environment in mind. The full breakdown of each bracket is in [Commander brackets explained](/blog/commander-brackets-explained).

That makes "is this cEDH?" a conversation, not a checklist. A very strong casual deck is still a bracket 4 deck if its owner built it to have powerful, fun games rather than to win against other competitive decks.

## How cEDH differs from casual Commander

Casual Commander is about the experience: big plays, memorable turns, and a game that lasts long enough for everyone to do something. cEDH keeps the format's rules but changes almost every assumption around them.

| | Casual Commander | cEDH |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | A fun, interactive game | Win as efficiently as possible |
| Typical game length | Many turns, often an hour or more | Often decided in a handful of turns |
| Usual win | Combat damage, big board states | Combos and efficient engines |
| Interaction | Some removal, a few counterspells | Dense, cheap and often free |
| Mana | Lands plus some ramp | Lots of fast mana and low curves |
| Role of the commander | Often the deck's theme | Usually an engine, a combo piece or a reliable value source |

### Speed

In casual Commander, a turn-four play is early. In cEDH, players regularly expect someone to attempt a winning play within the first few turns, and decks are built to do that or to stop it. That changes deckbuilding: expensive cards that are great at a casual table are often too slow to matter.

### Compact win conditions

Casual decks often win by building a large board and attacking. cEDH decks usually win with a small number of cards that end the game outright, typically a two- or three-card combo. One widely known example is Thassa's Oracle with Demonic Consultation: Demonic Consultation can exile your entire library if you name a card that isn't in it, and Thassa's Oracle wins the game when it enters if your devotion to blue is at least the number of cards left in your library. It's mentioned here only to show what a compact, two-card win looks like; cEDH decks use many different combos. [Combo decks explained](/blog/mtg-combo-decks-explained) covers how combo strategies work in general.

### Tutors and consistency

Because a cEDH deck can only run one copy of each card, it relies heavily on **tutors**, cards that search your library. Demonic Tutor, Vampiric Tutor and Mystical Tutor are classic examples. Tutors let a 100-card singleton deck find its win condition or the exact answer it needs with surprising reliability.

## Fast mana

**Fast mana** means mana sources that produce more mana than they cost, or produce it immediately, so you can cast spells earlier than the normal one-land-per-turn pace allows.

Typical examples:

- **Sol Ring** costs `{1}` and taps for `{C}{C}`, so it pays for itself immediately and is a net gain of one mana every turn after.
- **Mana rocks that cost zero or one.** Artifacts like Mox Diamond and Chrome Mox enter for no mana by having you discard or exile a card from your hand, then tap for mana right away.
- **One-shot rituals.** Dark Ritual costs `{B}` and adds `{B}{B}{B}`, a net gain of two black mana for that turn. Lotus Petal costs `{0}` and can be sacrificed for one mana of any color.
- **Cheap mana creatures.** One-mana creatures that tap for mana, like Llanowar Elves and Birds of Paradise, accelerate every turn after they survive.

Fast mana matters because cEDH is a race to be the first player able to win while also holding up protection. A player who starts with two or three extra mana on their first turn can either deploy a powerful engine immediately or threaten a win while still keeping interaction ready. That's why cEDH decks often have very few expensive cards and a lower land count than casual decks: the mana comes from elsewhere.

The cost of fast mana is card disadvantage. Chrome Mox and Mox Diamond each cost you a card, and rituals are gone after one use. cEDH players accept that because tempo, being first, usually matters more than raw card count in such fast games.

## Interaction density

The other half of cEDH is interaction. Since any player might win on any turn, every deck needs enough cheap answers to stop the others. Casual decks might run a few counterspells; cEDH decks often run many, plus efficient removal and stax effects.

### Cheap and free counterspells

Counterspells are central because most winning plays involve casting spells. Free ones are especially valuable because you can use them on the same turn you spend your mana on your own plan.

- **Force of Will** can be cast by exiling a blue card from your hand and paying 1 life instead of paying its mana cost.
- **Pact of Negation** costs `{0}` and counters a spell, but at the start of your next upkeep you must pay `{3}{U}{U}` or lose the game.
- **Swan Song** costs `{U}` and counters an enchantment, instant or sorcery spell, giving that spell's controller a 2/2 flying Bird token in return.

### Efficient removal and stax

Removal that costs one or two mana, such as Swords to Plowshares, lets players stop creature-based engines without falling behind. **Stax** pieces are permanents that restrict what players can do, often taxing or limiting spells. Thalia, Guardian of Thraben makes noncreature spells cost `{1}` more, and Rule of Law stops each player from casting more than one spell each turn. These pieces slow the whole table so the stax player has time to win.

### What this means for play

With so much interaction around, cEDH is less about deploying your plan and more about **timing**. Players hold their counterspells, watch for the turn when the table is tapped out or out of answers, and try to make their winning play through as little resistance as possible. Knowing which opponent is closest to winning, and who has to stop them, is a skill shared with casual games, and [Commander threat assessment](/blog/commander-threat-assessment) covers it in more depth. In cEDH it just happens much faster.

## Table etiquette

Competitive Commander has expectations that differ from a casual pod. Groups and events vary, so treat these as common norms rather than rules.

**Agree on intent before the game.** Saying "this is a cEDH game" before shuffling sets expectations. If one player brought a casual deck, the game won't be fun for anyone. This is the bracket conversation, and it matters most at bracket 5.

**Communicate clearly.** With so much instant-speed interaction, announce your spells and their targets, say when you pass priority, and give other players a chance to respond. Ask "any responses?" before resolving something important. Clear communication prevents arguments about whether someone had a chance to counter a spell.

**Play at a reasonable pace.** cEDH turns can be complex, but long pauses for every decision drag the game out for three other people. Think during other players' turns so you're ready on your own.

**Respect deals, avoid kingmaking.** Political deals happen in cEDH, but players generally expect decisions to be made in pursuit of your own win. Deliberately handing the game to one opponent when you can't win yourself, often called kingmaking, is widely frowned upon.

**Know event rules.** Community cEDH events set their own policies on things like proxies, time limits and how drawn games are handled. Read them before you show up.

**Be gracious.** Games can end abruptly on turn two or three. That's the format. Shuffle up and play the next one.

## Is cEDH for you?

cEDH suits players who enjoy tight, high-stakes decisions, deep knowledge of interactions and the stack, and games where everyone is playing their best. It's less suited to players who enjoy long games, big board states or decks built around a theme. Neither is better; they're different experiences that share a rules set.

If you're curious, a good first step is to watch or join a cEDH game with an open mind, or bring a strong, focused bracket 4 deck to a group that welcomes newcomers. You'll learn quickly which cards and habits the format rewards.

## Takeaway

cEDH is Commander at bracket 5: the same rules and cards, played with full competitive intent. Expect fast mana, low curves, compact combo wins and dense, cheap interaction, and expect players to communicate clearly and agree on intent before the game.
