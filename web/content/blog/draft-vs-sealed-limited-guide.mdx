Draft and Sealed are the two main ways to play Limited, where you build a deck on the spot from cards you open instead of bringing one from home. In Booster Draft, players open packs, take one card, and pass the rest around the table, so you choose your pool one pick at a time. In Sealed, you open a set number of packs yourself and build from exactly what you get. Both end with a 40-card deck. The difference is how much control you have over what's in your pool.

This guide explains how each format works, how to build a deck in either one, and which skills each format tests. For a broader look at every way to play, see [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).

## What is Limited?

Limited is any format where your card pool is restricted to what you open at the event. Constructed formats, like Standard or Commander, let you build from any legal card you own. In Limited, everyone starts from scratch with new packs from the same set.

A few things make Limited different from Constructed:

- **Decks are smaller.** The minimum is 40 cards, not 60.
- **There's no four-copy limit.** You can play as many copies of a card as you open or draft.
- **Basic lands are free.** The event provides them, so you don't need to open them.
- **The unused cards in your pool are your sideboard.** In a best-of-three match, you can swap them in between games.
- **Everyone plays the same set.** That levels the field: nobody has a stronger collection, and knowing the set is part of the skill.

Because pools are random, Limited decks are less powerful and less consistent than Constructed decks. Games are slower, combat matters more, and ordinary creatures do a lot of the work.

## How Booster Draft works

A draft happens at a table, called a pod, of several players. Each player has a few booster packs, traditionally three. The steps repeat until all cards are taken:

1. Everyone opens their first pack at the same time.
2. You look through it, take one card, and place it face down in your pile.
3. You pass the rest of the pack to the player next to you, and receive a pack from the player on your other side.
4. You take one card from that pack, then pass it on. This continues until every card in the first round of packs is gone.
5. Everyone opens their next pack. The passing direction usually switches each round, so you pass to the other neighbor.
6. Once all packs are drafted, you build a deck of at least 40 cards from your picks plus as many basic lands as you need.

What makes draft interesting is that you choose what goes into your pool, but so does everyone else. The cards that reach you depend on what your neighbors take. If the player passing to you is taking blue cards, you'll see fewer good blue cards, and more of whatever they're skipping.

### In-person draft vs online draft

In person, you draft with real people at a store or a kitchen table, usually with a time limit for each pick. Your neighbors are the same players you'll often face in the games afterward.

Digital clients like MTG Arena also offer draft. The basic process is the same, pick a card and pass the rest, but depending on the mode, the other drafters may be real players or computer-controlled drafters. Picks are timed, the deck builder fills in basic lands with a click, and you play your matches against other players afterward. Different modes have different entry costs and rewards, so check the client before you enter.

The main practical difference is how much you can learn from your neighbors. Computer drafters tend to follow consistent preferences, while human drafters react to what they see, so reading what's being passed works a little differently between the two.

## How Sealed works

In Sealed, you open a set number of booster packs (the exact number depends on the event) and build a deck from that pool alone. Nobody else takes cards from you, and you take nothing from anyone. When the packs are open, your pool is fixed.

You then build a deck of at least 40 cards using your pool plus basic lands. Everything you don't play is your sideboard.

Sealed is common at prerelease events, where players try a new set for the first time, and it's a gentle introduction to Limited because you don't need to make fast decisions under a pick timer. You can spread everything out, sort by color, and think.

## Building a 40-card Limited deck

The building process is the same for Draft and Sealed. A reliable starting template:

| Part of the deck | Guideline |
| --- | --- |
| Lands | About 17 |
| Creatures | Roughly 15 to 17 |
| Other spells | The rest, mostly removal and a few combat tricks |
| Colors | Two is typical |

These are guidelines, not rules. A low-curve aggressive deck might run 16 lands. A deck full of expensive spells might want 18.

### Pick two colors

Most Limited decks are two colors. Sort your pool by color and look for the two with the most playable cards and the best removal. One color is rarely deep enough on its own, and three colors usually make your mana unreliable.

### Fill the curve

Your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) is how your spells are spread across mana costs. In Limited, you want plenty of plays on turns two, three and four, and only a few cards that cost five or more. A deck full of six-drops loses to a deck that plays a creature every turn.

A rough shape for the creatures: several two-drops, several three-drops, a few four-drops, and a couple of expensive finishers.

### Prioritize creatures and removal

Limited games are mostly decided in combat, so you need enough creatures to attack and block. Removal, cards that destroy, exile or otherwise neutralize an opposing creature, is the most valuable non-creature spell, because it answers the one card that would beat you. [How to evaluate cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) goes deeper on ranking these.

### Split your lands by color

With 17 lands and two colors, split the basics roughly in proportion to how many cards of each color you play, leaning toward the color with more early plays. A deck that's mostly green with a few blue cards might run ten Forests and seven Islands.

## Draft vs Sealed: what changes

The deck-building rules are identical. What changes is how you get your pool, and that changes which skills matter most.

| | Booster Draft | Sealed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How you get cards | Pick one, pass the rest | Open your packs and keep everything |
| Control over your pool | High, pick by pick | None after opening |
| Main skill | Reading signals and building as you go | Finding the best deck in a fixed pool |
| Deck consistency | Usually higher, since you pick for synergy | Usually lower, since the pool is random |
| What wins | Synergy and a focused plan | Raw card quality, bombs and removal |
| Time pressure | Pick timers | Usually a longer build period |

### Sealed rewards bombs and removal

In Sealed, your pool is fixed, so the best deck is often the one that plays your strongest cards. A **bomb**, a card that wins the game by itself if unanswered, is worth a lot, and so is cheap, reliable removal. Sealed decks also tend to be less synergistic, since you can't draft toward a theme, so raw card quality matters more.

Building in Sealed is a puzzle. Lay out every color, count the playables and removal in each, and check whether your bombs are in colors deep enough to support them. Sometimes the right deck leaves a strong card in the sideboard because its color is too shallow.

### Draft rewards reading signals

In draft, you're competing with the other drafters for cards. If you and the player passing to you both want the same color, you'll both end up with weaker decks. If you find a color nobody near you is drafting, you'll get great cards from it late in each pack.

That's what **reading signals** means: watching what's still in the packs you receive to figure out what your neighbors are and aren't taking. A strong card in a color, still there several picks into a pack, is a hint that color is open. Early in the draft, take the strongest cards. As the draft goes on, settle into the colors that keep flowing to you.

Draft also rewards planning. Because you build as you pick, you can take cards that work together, fill gaps in your curve, and pick up enough creatures, instead of hoping your packs give you a balanced pool.

## Which should you try first?

If you're new to Limited, start with Sealed. There's no pick timer, you see your whole pool at once, and the main task, building a sound two-color deck with a good curve, is the core skill of every Limited format.

Once you're comfortable building, try draft. It adds a layer of decision-making that many players find the most rewarding part of Magic.

## Limited glossary

- **Bomb:** a card so strong it can win the game by itself if your opponent can't answer it.
- **Pick order:** a ranking of which cards to take first in a draft. Experienced players build one for each set, usually with bombs and removal near the top.
- **Signals:** information you get from the packs passed to you. A strong card arriving late suggests its color is underdrafted near you.
- **Splash:** adding a small number of cards from a third color, supported by a few lands of that color, usually to play a bomb or a strong removal spell.
- **Playable:** a card good enough to make your final 40.
- **Curve:** the spread of your spells across mana costs.
- **Pool:** every card you have available to build with, whether opened or drafted.
- **Combat trick:** an instant that changes the outcome of a fight, like a temporary boost to a creature's power and toughness.

## The takeaway

Draft and Sealed share the same deck-building rules: about 17 lands, plenty of creatures, a smooth curve, two colors, and as much removal as you can fit. Sealed tests how well you build from what you're given. Draft tests how well you read the table and shape your pool as you go. Learn to build a clean 40-card deck first, and both formats get easier.
