A burn deck is a mono-red aggro deck that wins by dealing 20 damage to the opponent as fast as possible, mostly with cheap spells that hit the face directly. It plays a handful of hasty creatures to deal early damage, then uses spells like Lightning Bolt to finish the job, often before the opponent's best cards matter. Building one is about efficiency: as much damage per card and per mana as you can get. Playing one is about counting.

If you're new to how aggro fits among the other archetypes, the [deck archetypes overview](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) sets the scene. This guide is about burn specifically.

## The game plan

Burn treats your opponent's life total as the only resource that matters. Your opponent starts at 20. If most of your spells deal 3 damage, you need seven of them, or fewer if creatures chip in early. Everything in the deck serves that math.

That focus gives burn a few unusual properties:

- **It ignores the opponent's board when it needs to.** A deck that wins by blocking creatures can't block a Lava Spike.
- **It punishes slow starts.** An opponent who stumbles on mana or spends the first three turns setting up is often dead before their plan comes online.
- **It's resilient to removal.** Your opponent's removal only hits your creatures, and burn doesn't need those creatures to survive long.
- **It runs out of cards.** Every spell is a one-for-one at best, and many go to the opponent's face rather than removing a threat. If the game goes long, burn usually loses.

The plan, then, is a race. You turn cards into damage efficiently and hope the opponent can't stabilize before their life total reaches zero.

## The core cards

Burn is built from a short list of card roles. Exact lists depend on the format, but the shape stays the same.

### One-mana burn spells

This is the heart of the deck. The ideal burn spell costs one red mana and deals 3 damage.

- **Lightning Bolt** (`{R}`, instant, 3 damage to any target) is the benchmark every other burn spell is measured against.
- **Chain Lightning** is a sorcery version with the same cost and damage.
- **Lava Spike** (`{R}`, sorcery, 3 damage to target player or planeswalker) can't hit creatures, but it's another cheap three points to the face.
- **Rift Bolt** has suspend, so you can exile it for `{R}` and it gets cast on your next turn for 3 damage to any target.

### Two- and three-mana burn

Slightly more expensive spells are fine when they offer something extra. **Skewer the Critics** costs `{2}{R}` normally but only `{R}` if an opponent lost life earlier in the turn, which is easy after an attack. Some burn decks play **Fireblast**, which deals 4 damage and can be cast for free by sacrificing two Mountains, a perfect finisher on the last turn of a game.

### Hasty creatures

Creatures in burn need to deal damage the turn they arrive or keep doing damage after the opponent finds blockers.

- **Monastery Swiftspear** has haste and prowess (it gets +1/+1 until end of turn whenever you cast a noncreature spell), so every burn spell makes it hit harder.
- **Goblin Guide** is a 2/2 with haste for one mana. Its drawback can give the opponent extra lands, which matters less in a deck that plans to win quickly.
- **Eidolon of the Great Revel** deals 2 damage to any player who casts a spell with mana value 3 or less. That hurts you too, but burn usually wins the trade because it's ahead on damage.

## How to count damage

Counting is the most important skill in burn. Before every turn, ask: how much damage can I deal right now, and how much can I deal over the next two turns?

**Count your reach.** Reach is the damage you can deal without combat, mostly from burn in hand. If the opponent is at 9 and you hold three Lightning Bolts, you have exactly enough. If they're at 10, you need one more point from somewhere.

**Count expected creature damage.** A creature that will survive another attack is worth its power per turn, but only if the opponent can't block or remove it. Don't count damage that depends on the opponent making a mistake.

**Count your mana.** Four burn spells in hand with three lands is three spells this turn, not four.

**Count their life loss you didn't cause.** Opponents damage themselves with painlands, shocklands and fetchlands, and every point helps you. Pay attention when they pay life.

A simple rule of thumb: once the opponent's life total is within the damage you can deal with the burn in your hand, plus one draw, stop pointing spells at creatures and start aiming everything at the face.

### When to burn a creature

Burn spells that can hit creatures give you a choice every time. Point them at a creature when:

- it would block and kill your attackers for several turns, costing you more damage than the spell would deal;
- it gains the opponent life or blocks well enough to stop the race;
- it's an engine that will take over the game if left alone.

Otherwise, aim at the opponent. Many burn players lose because they spend half their spells on creatures and run out of damage one or two spells short.

## Building the deck

### Creature and spell split

A common shape is around 12 to 16 creatures and 20 to 24 burn spells in a 60-card deck. More creatures means more damage if the game goes unanswered, but creatures are easier to stop. More spells means more reach and fewer dead cards against blockers.

If the opponents in your area play lots of cheap removal and blockers, lean toward spells. If they play slow decks that don't interact early, more creatures will do more damage.

### Mana curve

Burn wants almost everything to cost one or two mana, with only a few three-mana cards. The goal is to cast two spells a turn by turn three or four, so a hand never sits idle waiting for mana.

### Land count

Burn usually plays about 18 to 20 lands in a 60-card deck, fewer than most decks because the curve is so low. Mono-red keeps the mana simple: mostly Mountains, with a few utility lands if the format offers them. Drawing your fourth land is nice; drawing your seventh is a wasted card, so don't overload on lands.

## Playing around lifegain

Lifegain is burn's natural enemy, because burn wins by math and lifegain changes the math. A card that gains 4 life undoes more than a full Lightning Bolt.

A few habits help:

- **Know the lifegain cards in your format.** Kitchen Finks, Lightning Helix and similar cards are common in decks that want to beat aggro.
- **Hold burn when lifegain is likely.** If you can't kill the opponent this turn and they might gain life next turn, it can be better to keep spells in hand and fire them all at once, especially instant-speed spells you can cast at the end of their turn.
- **Kill lifegain creatures.** A creature that gains life every turn is worth a burn spell almost every time.
- **Use effects that stop lifegain.** Skullcrack deals 3 damage to a player or planeswalker and stops players from gaining life that turn, which can blank a big lifegain spell if you cast it at the right moment.

## Common mistakes

1. **Burning creatures by reflex.** Every spell aimed at a creature is damage that didn't go to the face.
2. **Wasting damage early.** Throwing three burn spells at the opponent on turn two can be correct, but only if you're confident you'll find the rest. Instants are often better held until you see what the opponent does.
3. **Ignoring their clock.** Burn is a race. If the opponent will kill you in two turns, plan to win in two turns, even if that means using burn on a blocker.
4. **Forgetting prowess and Eidolon.** Order your spells so prowess creatures get pumped before attacks, and remember Eidolon of the Great Revel damages you too.
5. **Keeping slow hands.** A burn hand with no early plays is just a weaker deck. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) has worked aggro examples.

## Sideboarding ideas

Burn's sideboard usually covers three problems: lifegain, decks that block well, and permanents that shut burn down. A few long-standing options:

- **Against lifegain:** Skullcrack and similar "can't gain life" effects.
- **Against artifact-heavy decks:** Smash to Smithereens destroys an artifact and deals 3 damage to its controller, so it's removal and burn in one card.
- **Against creature-heavy aggro:** cheap creature removal that also hits players, or creatures with better blocking stats. Some burn players cut fragile creatures entirely and rely on more spells.
- **Against slow combo and control:** creatures that are hard to remove and more burn that hits the face. Against control, cards that can't be countered or that deal damage over time are valuable.

Also know what opponents bring in against you: lifegain creatures, cards like Kor Firewalker that protect from red, and effects that stop players from being targeted. Plan to answer the ones you can and race through the ones you can't. For a general framework on what to cut and add, see [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101).

## Burn on a budget

Burn is one of the cheapest ways into competitive Constructed in many formats, because many of its key burn spells are commons or uncommons and its mono-color mana base can be mostly basic Mountains. Our [budget decks guide](/blog/budget-mtg-decks-under-50) covers how to pick replacements when you can't get every card.

## The takeaway

Burn is simple to pilot and hard to master. Build it with as many efficient one-mana spells as you can, keep the curve low and the land count around 18 to 20, and always count damage before you act. Point spells at the face unless a creature truly threatens your race, and respect lifegain. Do those things and you'll win a lot of games before your opponent gets to play theirs.
