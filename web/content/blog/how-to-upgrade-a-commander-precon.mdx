To upgrade a Magic: The Gathering Commander precon (preconstructed deck), play it a few times as it comes, then cut its weakest cards first: the ones that are off theme, overcosted or slow to affect the board. Fix the mana base and ramp next, because that improves every game. Then add cards that push the deck's main theme, ten or so at a time, while keeping an eye on how strong the deck becomes compared with the rest of your table.

A preconstructed Commander deck is built to be playable straight out of the box, but it has to balance a lot of goals at once: show off new cards, stay affordable and work for players of different experience. That leaves plenty of room to improve it. This guide walks through the process in the order that gets you the most improvement for the least effort.

## Play it before you change it

The best first step is the least exciting one: play the deck as it is, three to five games if you can. You'll learn things a decklist can't tell you.

- Which cards you were happy to draw, and which ones sat in your hand.
- Whether you ran out of mana, out of cards, or out of ways to deal with threats.
- How the commander actually performs once opponents start targeting it.
- What the deck's real plan is, as opposed to what the box says it is.

Keep a quick note on your phone after each game. "Never cast the seven-drop," "flooded twice," "no answer to a flier." Those notes become your upgrade list. Without them, you're guessing, and most guesses lead to adding more big, flashy cards to a deck whose problem is actually mana.

If your group plays with house rules, a quick [rule zero conversation](/blog/commander-rule-zero-guide) before those games also tells you what kind of deck the table expects.

## Find the weakest cards

Every precon has a handful of cards that pull less weight than the rest. Look for these patterns:

- **Off-theme cards.** A deck built around tokens that also runs a random big creature with no token synergy. These are often included for flavor or to show off a mechanic.
- **Overcosted effects.** A card that does something for six mana that another card does for three. Commander games are long, but you still want to spend each turn efficiently.
- **Vanilla bodies.** Creatures that are just stats, with no ability that matters in a game of four players and 40 life each.
- **Narrow answers.** Removal that only hits one card type, or only works in a rare situation.
- **Win-more cards.** Cards that only do something when you're already ahead.
- **Lands that enter tapped** without a good reason, especially if the deck has many of them.

Your play notes help sort these out. A card that looks weak on paper but kept doing work is worth keeping for now; a card that looks fine but never got cast is a good cut.

## Fix the mana and the ramp first

Upgrading the mana is the change that improves the most games. A deck with smooth mana casts its spells on time, while a deck with clunky mana loses even with powerful cards in it.

### Lands

Most Commander decks want somewhere between 35 and 38 lands, adjusted for how many mana rocks and ramp spells they play. Then look at quality:

- **Too many tapped lands** slow you down. Replace a few with lands that enter untapped.
- **Command Tower** taps for any color in your commander's color identity and fits in nearly every multicolor deck.
- **Two-color decks** need fewer fixing lands than three-color decks. In a mono-colored deck, basics are often fine.
- **Utility lands** can add value, but too many colorless lands in a multicolor deck cause color problems.

### Ramp

Most precons already include Sol Ring and a few mana rocks, but many still run light on ramp. A common target is around ten pieces of ramp, counting cheap mana rocks like Arcane Signet and Mind Stone and green spells like Rampant Growth, Cultivate and Kodama's Reach.

Two- and three-mana ramp matters more than expensive ramp. A four-mana rock that taps for two isn't bad, but casting it on turn four usually means you didn't do much else that turn. The [ramp guide](/blog/mtg-ramp-deck-guide) covers the different kinds of acceleration in more detail.

## Check the deck's fundamentals

Once the mana is fixed, count the cards that do the jobs every Commander deck needs. These are rough guidelines, not rules:

| Category | Rough target | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ramp | about 10 | Gets you ahead on mana |
| Card draw | about 10 | Keeps your hand full in long games |
| Targeted removal | about 8 to 10 | Answers individual threats |
| Board wipes | 2 to 3 | Resets the board when you fall behind |
| Theme cards | the rest | Executes the deck's plan |

If any category is far below the target, it's probably the deck's biggest weakness. Running out of cards and having no answer to a big threat are the two most common complaints with unmodified precons.

Flexible removal is worth prioritizing. Swords to Plowshares in white, Beast Within in green and Chaos Warp in red can each deal with a wide range of problems. Card draw that keeps working over several turns is usually better than a single burst, because Commander games often go long.

## Focus the theme

A precon often has two or three ideas mixed together: say, tokens, a bit of lifegain and a few +1/+1 counter cards. Upgrading is easier when you pick one main plan and lean into it.

1. **Choose the plan** your commander supports best. Read the commander carefully and ask what kind of cards it rewards you for playing.
2. **Cut the cards that serve the secondary plans** unless they also support the main one.
3. **Add cards that reward or enable the main plan.** A token deck wants token makers and cards that make tokens stronger; a graveyard deck wants ways to fill the graveyard and ways to use it.
4. **Keep some redundancy.** Several cards that do similar things make the deck more consistent than one perfect card.

If you aren't sure which plan is strongest, your play notes usually point to it: the games you won show what the deck does well.

### Protect your commander

If your deck relies heavily on its commander, a few ways to protect it are worth including. Lightning Greaves and Swiftfoot Boots are long-standing options. Lightning Greaves gives the equipped creature haste and shroud, and Swiftfoot Boots gives haste and hexproof. Both have cheap equip costs, so you can attach them to your commander soon after it enters.

## Upgrade on a budget

You don't need to spend much to make a big difference. A few principles keep upgrades affordable:

- **Swap in pairs.** For every card you add, name the card it replaces. This keeps the deck at 100 cards and forces you to make decisions.
- **Upgrade in rounds of five to ten cards.** Play a few games after each round. Changing thirty cards at once makes it impossible to tell which changes helped.
- **Prioritize mana and fundamentals.** Basic lands, cheap mana rocks and common removal spells are often inexpensive and help more than a rare theme card.
- **Look for reprinted staples.** Cards that have been printed many times are usually easier to find.
- **Use cards you already own.** Older precons and bulk collections often hold exactly the role-players you need.
- **Proxy-test before buying** if your group allows it. Playing a card first tells you whether it's worth getting a real copy.

A builder can also help you plan. [MTG Deck Lab](/) can draft a Commander list around a commander and theme, which gives you a set of options to compare with your precon.

## Keep power level in mind

Upgrading always raises a deck's power, and that's the point, but it's possible to overshoot. A precon that wins every game by turn six is no longer fun for a table of other precons.

The official [Commander brackets](/blog/commander-brackets-explained) give groups a shared vocabulary for power level. Precons are generally designed around the lower-middle part of that scale. Adding fast mana, tutors, mass land destruction, extra turns or cards from the official Game Changers list can move your deck up a bracket, so check the current bracket guidelines when you add those kinds of cards.

A good habit is to tell your group when you've upgraded a deck significantly. That way nobody sits down expecting a precon matchup and gets something much stronger.

## A sample upgrade order

If you want a simple plan to follow, this order works for most precons:

1. Play three to five games and take notes.
2. Replace a few tapped lands and add Command Tower if it isn't already in the deck.
3. Bring ramp up to around ten pieces.
4. Add card draw and flexible removal until the fundamentals look healthy.
5. Cut off-theme cards and replace them with on-theme ones.
6. Play again, then repeat steps 4 and 5 as needed.

For a deeper look at building a list from scratch, including how to pick categories and a commander, see [how to build your first Commander deck](/blog/how-to-build-your-first-commander-deck).

## The takeaway

Start by playing the precon and noting what goes wrong. Cut the weakest cards, fix the lands and ramp, and fill gaps in card draw and removal before you chase theme upgrades. Then pick one main plan and lean into it, swapping in pairs and in small rounds. Keep your table's power level in mind as you go, and you'll end up with a deck that's stronger and more yours without losing the fun.
