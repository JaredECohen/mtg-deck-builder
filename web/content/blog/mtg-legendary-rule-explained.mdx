The legend rule says that if you control two or more legendary permanents with the same name, you choose one of them to keep and put the rest into their owners' graveyards. It only looks at the permanents a single player controls, so you and your opponent can each have a copy of the same legend without any problem. It doesn't care about card type: legendary creatures, artifacts, enchantments, lands and planeswalkers all follow it.

This guide covers what "legendary" means, exactly how the rule works, the edge cases that confuse new players, how the rule has changed over the years, and why it matters so much in Commander.

## What "legendary" means

Legendary is a **supertype**, a word that appears on the type line before the card type. A card's type line might read "Legendary Creature — Human Soldier" or "Legendary Artifact — Equipment." If you're not sure where to look, [how to read a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) walks through every part of the card frame. Newer legendary cards also have a decorative crown or frame treatment, but the type line is what counts.

Legendary cards represent unique people, places and objects in Magic's story. There's only one of each, so the game doesn't let you have two of the same one on your side.

Some long-standing examples:

- **Isamaru, Hound of Konda:** a legendary creature.
- **Umezawa's Jitte:** a legendary artifact (an Equipment).
- **Gaea's Cradle:** a legendary land.
- **Garruk Wildspeaker:** a planeswalker. Modern printings of planeswalkers are legendary too.

The supertype on its own does nothing. A legendary creature fights, blocks and dies exactly like any other creature. The only rule it brings with it is the legend rule. For the other card types these supertypes attach to, see [MTG card types explained](/blog/mtg-card-types-explained).

## How the legend rule works

The rule is a **state-based action**. That means the game checks for it automatically, every time a player would get priority, and fixes the situation right away. Nobody casts anything and nothing uses the stack.

Here's what happens step by step when you play a second copy of a legend you already control:

1. You cast the second copy. It goes on the stack and can be countered like any other spell.
2. It resolves and enters the battlefield. For a moment, you control two permanents with the same name.
3. Before anyone gets priority, the game checks state-based actions and sees two legendary permanents with the same name under your control.
4. You choose one to keep. The other goes to its owner's graveyard.

You get to choose which one stays. You can keep the new copy or the old one. That choice has real uses, which we'll cover below.

### Same name, not same character

The rule checks the **exact name**. Two different cards that depict the same character but have different names don't interact. If you control two legendary creatures with different names that both show the same character, you keep both.

### Your permanents only

The rule checks each player separately. If you control a legendary creature and your opponent controls a copy with the same name, both stay on the battlefield. The rule only kicks in when one player controls two or more.

It's about control, not ownership. If you steal your opponent's copy of a legend you already have, you now control two, and you have to choose one. If you keep yours, theirs goes to their graveyard, because cards always go to their owner's graveyard.

### It isn't sacrifice and it isn't destruction

The legend rule simply puts the extra permanents into the graveyard. That has a few consequences:

- **Indestructible doesn't help.** Indestructible stops "destroy" effects and lethal damage, not the legend rule.
- **It isn't sacrificing.** Abilities that trigger when you sacrifice a permanent don't trigger.
- **It does count as dying.** A creature going from the battlefield to the graveyard dies, so "when this creature dies" triggers happen normally.
- **Regeneration can't save it.** Regeneration only replaces destruction, and nothing is being destroyed.

## Using the rule on purpose

Since you choose which copy to keep, playing a second copy of a legend isn't always a waste.

- **Refreshing a permanent.** If your legendary creature is damaged or your opponent has put a negative effect on it, playing a fresh copy and keeping the new one can reset the situation. The new copy is a brand-new object with none of those effects.
- **Getting another enters-the-battlefield trigger.** A legendary permanent with an ability that triggers when it enters still triggers, even if you then choose to keep the old copy. The trigger was already set off when it entered.
- **Resetting a planeswalker.** A planeswalker low on loyalty can be replaced with a fresh copy at full starting loyalty. The [planeswalkers guide](/blog/mtg-planeswalkers-explained) covers loyalty and how this fits into planeswalker play.

When you're building a deck, though, the legend rule is a reason to play fewer copies of a legendary card. Drawing two of one at the same time means one is often a dead card. Many Constructed decks run fewer than four copies of a legend for exactly this reason.

## Copies, tokens and exceptions

Copy effects create the most legend rule questions.

- **Copying your own legend.** If you use a Clone to copy a legendary creature you control, the copy has the same name and is also legendary. You'll have to choose one and lose the other.
- **Copying an opponent's legend.** If you copy a legendary creature your opponent controls, you end up with one under your control and they keep theirs. Nobody has two, so the rule does nothing.
- **Copies that aren't legendary.** Some effects make copies that specifically aren't legendary. Helm of the Host, for example, makes a token copy of the equipped creature that isn't legendary. Those copies dodge the legend rule entirely.
- **Cards that switch it off.** A few cards say the legend rule doesn't apply. Mirror Gallery is the classic example. When a card says this, follow the card.

### Legendary spells

The legend rule only applies to permanents, but a few instants and sorceries are legendary too. A legendary instant or sorcery has its own restriction: you can only cast it if you control a legendary creature or a legendary planeswalker. Urza's Ruinous Blast is a legendary sorcery that works this way.

## How the rule has changed

The legend rule has been revised over Magic's history, so older articles and players may describe it differently. In general terms:

- **It used to check every player.** Older versions of the rule looked at all legendary permanents on the battlefield, not just one player's. Playing your copy of a legend could knock your opponent's copy off the battlefield as well. The current rule only checks within each player's own permanents.
- **Planeswalkers had a separate rule.** For years, planeswalkers weren't printed as legendary. Instead, a "planeswalker uniqueness rule" stopped you from controlling two planeswalkers of the same character type, even with different names. That rule was later retired, and planeswalkers became legendary, so they now follow the ordinary legend rule by name.

If you play with older cards, nothing about the cards themselves changes. The current Comprehensive Rules apply to every card, old or new.

## The legend rule in Commander

The legend rule matters more in [Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) than anywhere else, because the format is built around a legendary card.

- **Your commander is usually a legendary creature.** In general, your commander must be a legendary creature. A few other cards state on them that they can be your commander. The official Commander rules list the exact eligibility.
- **Singleton decks rarely have duplicates.** A Commander deck has only one copy of each card other than basic lands, so you won't draw two copies of a legend. The rule shows up through copies and tokens instead.
- **Copying your commander costs you something.** If you copy your own commander with a Clone effect, you'll have to choose one. If you keep the copy, your actual commander goes to your graveyard, and you can move it to the command zone if you choose. Copy effects that make nonlegendary copies are popular in Commander for exactly this reason.
- **Multiplayer doesn't change the rule.** Every opponent can control the same legend you do, and nothing happens. The rule still only checks one player's permanents at a time.

Many Commander decks include other legendary creatures alongside the commander, especially decks that care about legends. That's fine: they just can't share a name.

## The takeaway

The legend rule is short. If you control two or more legendary permanents with the same name, you keep one and the rest go to the graveyard. It only checks your own permanents, it goes by exact name, and indestructible won't save you from it. Use it on purpose to refresh a damaged legend or a low-loyalty planeswalker, watch out for it with copy effects, and keep the number of legendary duplicates in your deck low.
