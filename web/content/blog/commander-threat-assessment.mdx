Threat assessment in Magic: The Gathering Commander is the skill of figuring out which opponent is actually closest to winning, not just who has the scariest-looking board. In a four-player game you have limited removal and three people to worry about, so spending your answers on the right player at the right time often matters more than the cards in your deck.

This guide covers how to read the table, the archenemy problem, when to remove and when to hold, politics and deals, staying under the radar, using board wipes well, and the mistakes that cost multiplayer games.

## Why threat assessment matters more in Commander

In a one-on-one game, the only threat is your opponent. In multiplayer [Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh), every opponent is a threat, but not equally. If you use your only removal spell on a creature that wasn't going to win the game, and the player next to you goes off the following turn, you lost to a misjudgment, not to bad luck.

It also works the other way. Each opponent's removal and attacks are also limited. If the table agrees on who the real threat is, that player gets handled. If the table misreads it, the real threat often wins while everyone else fights each other.

## How to identify the real threat

Look past the biggest creature on the battlefield and ask who is closest to winning and who will be hardest to stop later. A few factors matter most.

### Board state

Count what each player could do on their next turn. How much damage could they deal, and to whom? Are their creatures evasive? A board of small fliers can be more dangerous than one large ground creature that everyone can chump block.

### Mana

Mana is potential. A player with twice as many lands and mana rocks as everyone else can cast more spells per turn, play their expensive threats earlier and respond with mana left open. A player who ramped heavily early is often a bigger long-term threat than one with a slightly bigger board right now.

### Cards in hand

A player with a full grip has options. A player who is empty-handed is limited to what they draw. Card counts are public information, so check them. A player who has quietly drawn several extra cards each turn is building toward something.

### Engines

An engine is a permanent that keeps producing value every turn. These are often more dangerous than any single creature, because they snowball. Rhystic Study and Smothering Tithe are well-known examples of must-answer value engines: each one taxes the whole table, and if nobody pays, its controller pulls further ahead every turn. Other engines might draw a card every upkeep, produce extra mana or make tokens.

The longer an engine stays on the battlefield, the more it costs to deal with it later. Removing an engine early is often better value than removing a big creature.

### Commander damage

A player who takes 21 or more combat damage from a single commander loses the game, regardless of their life total. A commander that hits hard can threaten to eliminate someone in two or three swings even when that player is at a high life total. Track it.

### Combo potential

Some decks can win suddenly by assembling a combination of cards. If a player is playing a deck that can combo, a quiet board doesn't mean they're safe. Watch for tutors (cards that search the library), unusual mana, and a player passing turns with lots of mana open. Know what each deck at your table is capable of, and the [Commander brackets](/blog/commander-brackets-explained) conversation before the game is a good time to ask.

## The archenemy problem

The archenemy is the player everyone sees as the main threat. Being the archenemy is often worse than being behind, because the whole table aims removal and attacks at you.

Two things make this tricky. First, players sometimes target whoever looks scariest, even when someone else is closer to winning. Second, the real threat often benefits when the table piles onto the archenemy, since nobody is watching them.

If you notice the table fixating on one player while someone else quietly builds an engine or assembles a combo, say so. Pointing out the real threat isn't rude; it's part of multiplayer strategy, and it helps the table make better decisions.

## When to remove and when to hold removal

Every removal spell you have is a decision. A few guidelines:

**Remove engines and win conditions early.** A card that will win the game or generate value every turn deserves an answer before it gets out of hand.

**Hold flexible removal for real threats.** Swords to Plowshares or Beast Within can answer almost anything. Using one on a creature that's only a minor annoyance can leave you with nothing when a game-winning threat lands.

**Let others go first when it's safe.** If a threat targets someone else and the table has several players with removal, you can sometimes wait. Be careful, though: everyone thinking this way is how threats go unanswered. If the threat will win the game before your next turn, deal with it.

**Remove at instant speed when you can.** Waiting until the end of an opponent's turn or until a creature attacks lets you see more information first.

**Remember the commander comes back.** Removing a commander is temporary. Its owner can move it to the command zone and cast it again, paying an extra `{2}` for each previous time it was cast from there. Exile and "put it into its library" effects don't get around this, since the owner can send the commander to the command zone instead. Commander removal is still often worth it, because the tax adds up, but treat it as buying time.

## Politics and deals

Multiplayer games involve negotiation. Players make deals, form temporary alliances and argue about who the threat is. A few principles:

- **Be honest about the board.** Pointing out the real threat is good politics, and it's more convincing when it's true.
- **Make deals you can keep.** "I won't attack you this turn if you don't attack me" is a simple deal both players can track.
- **Keep your word.** If you make a deal and break it, the table will remember, possibly for many games. Most groups value trust more than any single win.
- **Don't make deals that only benefit the strongest player.** Helping the leader often means you lose to them later.
- **Respect your group's norms.** Some playgroups enjoy heavy politics; others prefer to keep it light. Read the table and keep things friendly.

## Don't become the obvious threat too early

A strong start can make you the archenemy before you're actually threatening a win. If you deploy every threat and engine you have by turn four, the table's removal will be aimed at you while you still have a long way to go.

Some ways to stay under the radar:

- **Develop at a measured pace.** Play what you need to stay safe and progress, but hold some threats back.
- **Don't swing at everyone.** Attacking every player makes everyone want to retaliate.
- **Keep your engines low-profile.** A card-draw engine is less likely to draw fire if you aren't also leading on board.
- **Win quickly once you commit.** When you go for it, try to go for it all at once so the table has fewer chances to react.

This isn't about being dishonest. It's about not giving the table a reason to target you before you're ready.

## Using board wipes well

Board wipes like Wrath of God, Damnation or Blasphemous Act clear the battlefield of creatures. In multiplayer, they're among the most powerful tools for resetting a game, but they're also easy to waste.

**Wait until it matters.** A board wipe is best when an opponent is threatening a win, or when you'd lose more value than you'd keep by not using it. Casting one just because the board is full can leave you defenseless later.

**Count what you lose too.** A wipe that kills one opponent's big board and your small one is great. A wipe that kills your best creatures and only a little of theirs is not.

**Rebuild faster than the table.** The player who recovers first after a wipe often wins. If you cast a wipe, try to have a follow-up ready.

**Don't overcommit into a known wipe.** If an opponent is playing a deck known for sweepers and is holding cards with mana open, consider holding back a couple of threats.

## Tracking life totals and commander damage

Life totals start at 40 in Commander, and with several players damage comes from many sources. Keep accurate records:

- **Use a life tracker, app or pen and paper.** Agree as a table on how you'll track.
- **Track commander damage separately.** Note how much each commander has dealt to each player.
- **Announce big changes.** If someone takes a big hit, say the new total out loud so everyone stays on the same page.

Accurate tracking helps threat assessment, since you can see who is close to death and who is close to winning through commander damage.

## Common threat assessment mistakes

**Attacking the weakest player.** It's tempting to finish off the player with the lowest life total, but eliminating them often removes a player who would have helped deal with the real threat. Attack the player who is closest to winning, or whose blockers are least valuable to the table.

**Wasting removal on the first threat.** The first creature someone plays is rarely the most dangerous card they'll cast. Save premium removal for threats that actually matter.

**Ignoring engines.** A small creature that draws a card every turn is often more dangerous than a big creature with no abilities.

**Forgetting about combo.** A player with a quiet board and a full hand of mana can be the biggest threat at the table.

**Holding a grudge.** Targeting a player because of something that happened earlier, rather than the current board, leads to poor decisions.

Look past the scariest board. Weigh mana, cards in hand, engines, commander damage and combo potential to find who is actually closest to winning. Save flexible removal for threats that matter, deal with engines early, keep your deals, and avoid becoming the archenemy before you're ready to win.
