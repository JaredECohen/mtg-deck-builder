A signal in Magic: The Gathering booster draft is information about what the players next to you are taking, read from the cards they pass. When a strong card in one color reaches you later than it should, it usually means the players passing to you aren't in that color, so it's likely to keep flowing. Reading signals well lets you settle into the colors nobody near you wants, which is how you end up with a deck full of good cards.

This guide assumes you know how a draft works. If you don't, [draft vs sealed](/blog/draft-vs-sealed-limited-guide) explains the passing and deck building. It also assumes you can tell a strong card from a weak one; [how to evaluate cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) covers that.

## What a signal is

Every pack you receive has already been picked through by the players upstream of you, the ones passing to you. What's missing tells you what they took. What's left tells you what they didn't want.

You can't see the missing cards, but you can learn a lot from what remains:

- **A strong card in a color, late in the pack,** suggests the players upstream passed it because they aren't in that color.
- **Several playable cards in one color, pick after pick,** suggests that color is underdrafted upstream.
- **A color that dries up,** with only weak cards reaching you, suggests someone upstream is taking it.

None of these are certain on their own. Signals are evidence, and good drafters build a picture over many picks rather than jumping on one card.

## Upstream, downstream and pack direction

The players who pass to you are upstream. The players you pass to are downstream. Their positions matter because packs traditionally change direction each round:

| Pack | Passing direction | Who feeds you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pack 1 | Left | Your right-hand neighbor and those beyond them |
| Pack 2 | Right | Your left-hand neighbor and those beyond them |
| Pack 3 | Left | Your right-hand neighbor again |

Your closest neighbor in each direction matters most. The player directly passing to you sees each pack one pick before you do, so their choices shape your pack more than anyone else's.

Because pack 3 passes the same way as pack 1, the signals you read in pack 1 are the most useful for pack 3. Pack 2 comes from the other side, so your read can change.

## What a late good card means

Picks early in a pack tell you very little. The first few cards gone from a pack were taken by players choosing from the best available, often before they've settled on colors. A strong card in the first two or three picks may just mean the pack had several good cards.

As the pack goes on, a strong card is more meaningful. If a premium removal spell or a strong creature reaches you after several players have passed it, each of them chose something else over it. The likeliest reason is that it's in a color none of them are drafting.

Ask a few questions before reading too much into it:

- **How strong is it really?** A card you think is great might be one others reasonably rate lower. Be honest about where it sits.
- **What else was in the pack?** If the pack was full of strong cards, a good one coming late is less surprising.
- **Is it a pattern?** One late card is a hint. Three late cards in the same color over several picks is a signal.

### Wheeling

In an eight-player pod, the pack you open comes back to you after the other seven players have each taken a card. Seeing which cards wheel, meaning which make it all the way around, is one of the clearest signals in draft. If a playable card in a color wheels, nobody in the pod took it over seven chances. That's a sign the color is open across the table, not just next to you.

Remember the cards in your first pack. Noting what you passed lets you check which ones came back.

## Staying open early

The biggest signal-reading mistake is deciding your colors too early. If you take a strong card first and then force that color no matter what, you'll often fight a neighbor over the same cards and end up with a thin deck.

In the early part of pack 1:

- **Take the best card,** usually measured by power and how often it'll make your deck.
- **Lean toward flexible cards** like colorless artifacts or cards that fit several archetypes, when the power is close.
- **Keep two or three colors in mind** without committing to them.
- **Pay attention to what keeps coming.** By the middle of pack 1, a pattern often starts to show.

Staying open has a cost. Every pick spent in a color you don't end up playing is a wasted pick. The goal isn't to stay open forever. It's to wait until you have enough information to choose well.

## When to commit to colors

Most drafters settle into one color around the middle of pack 1 and a second color by the end of pack 1 or the start of pack 2. The exact timing depends on the signals.

Signs it's time to commit:

- **One color keeps flowing,** with good cards arriving mid-pack.
- **You have a solid core** of playables in a color, enough that switching would cost you many picks.
- **Your other options have dried up,** meaning someone upstream is clearly in them.

Commit to your main color first, and keep your second color a little more flexible. Two-color decks are the norm in Limited, so it's fine to keep a few picks in reserve for a second color until pack 2 shows you which one is open from the other side.

### Switching colors

Sometimes you commit, and then the color dries up. If pack 2 shows that the color you picked is heavily drafted from the other side, it can be worth moving into a more open one, especially if you have a few playables there already. The longer you wait, the more it costs, so switch early or not at all.

## How pack 2 changes the read

In pack 2, the passing direction flips. The neighbor you were feeding in pack 1 is now feeding you.

This creates a useful effect: if you took a color heavily in pack 1, your downstream neighbor likely saw little of it and avoided it. In pack 2, that neighbor is passing to you, and they're likely to pass that color along. Strong pack 1 drafting in a color can pay off in pack 2.

If pack 2 shows something different, such as a third color flowing freely, consider it as your second color. The signals you read from the other side are just as real as those from pack 1.

## Sending signals

Signals go both ways. The cards you pass tell your downstream neighbor what you aren't taking.

- **Cutting a color** means taking its good cards so they don't reach your downstream neighbor. If you do this consistently in pack 1, your neighbor is likely to avoid that color, and in pack 2 they'll pass it to you.
- **Passing a color freely** tells your neighbor it's open. If you're not in it, letting it go can encourage them into it and away from your colors.

You don't need to play mind games. Simply drafting your colors consistently sends clear signals, and clear signals help you more than confusing ones.

### Hate drafting

Hate drafting means taking a card you won't play just to keep it from an opponent. It's usually not worth it, because a playable card for your own deck matters more. Late in a pack, when nothing playable for you remains, taking the strongest card that would help your neighbor is reasonable.

## Common signal mistakes

- **Reading one card as a signal.** Wait for a pattern.
- **Ignoring signals entirely.** Forcing a color you like can work, but it means accepting worse cards when that color isn't open.
- **Switching too often.** Flipping colors every few picks leaves you with a pile of unplayable cards.
- **Forgetting your curve.** Even in the open colors, you need playables at every mana value. The [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) helps you check.
- **Undervaluing the second color.** A strong main color with a weak second color leads to a thin deck.

## Online vs in person

In person, drafting next to human players means signals reflect real choices. Online, depending on the mode, some drafters may be computer-controlled, and their preferences can be more consistent than a person's. The principles are the same, but how much a late card means can differ.

## The takeaway

Signals come from what the players upstream pass you. A strong card that arrives late, or a color that keeps flowing, points to an open lane. Take the best cards early, watch for patterns through the middle of pack 1, commit to a main color once the evidence is clear and keep your second color flexible until pack 2. Draft your colors consistently and your neighbors will read your signals too.
