Limited Edition Alpha, Limited Edition Beta and Unlimited Edition are the first three printings of Magic: The Gathering, all released in 1993. They contain essentially the same cards, designed by Richard Garfield, and they launched the whole game: the five colors, the basic lands, the creature, instant and sorcery types, and the most famous cards in Magic's history, from Black Lotus and the Moxen to Lightning Bolt and Serra Angel. This guide covers all three together, since most of what matters about one applies to the others.

## The setting and flavor

There is no story in the first Magic sets. The game's premise is that each player is a planeswalker, a powerful wizard who can travel between worlds and summon creatures and spells from them. The worlds were collectively called Dominia, and the cards hint at many different places without tying them into a plot.

Flavor came from the art and from flavor text, much of which quoted poetry, folklore and classic literature rather than an invented history. The cards are a mix of classic fantasy archetypes, like Serra Angel, Shivan Dragon, Lord of the Pit and Mahamoti Djinn, and stranger ideas that only made sense for a brand-new game, like Two-Headed Giant of Foriys and Raging River. Names such as Sengir Vampire and Royal Assassin would later get fuller backstories, but in 1993 they were simply evocative cards.

## The three printings

**Alpha** was the first print run. It is recognizable by its noticeably rounder corners and a handful of errors. Two cards were left out entirely by mistake: Circle of Protection: Black and Volcanic Island. Alpha sold out almost immediately.

**Beta** followed in October 1993. It corrected many Alpha errors, added the two missing cards, used less rounded corners, and included extra basic land artwork. Alpha and Beta both have black borders.

**Unlimited** arrived in December 1993 with white borders, which signaled a reprint and became the core set look for more than a decade. It had a much larger print run than Alpha and Beta combined. Revised Edition, in 1994, followed Unlimited and dropped some of the most powerful cards.

For gameplay purposes, the three printings are the same cards. Collectors care deeply about which printing a card is from, but every rules-legal version plays identically.

## Mechanics

These sets defined the game's foundations. Many of their mechanics are still around, while a few have been retired:

- **Card types**: lands, creatures, instants, sorceries, enchantments and artifacts. Creature spells were originally labeled "Summon," and Auras were labeled "Enchant Creature." Interrupts, a faster kind of instant, existed until Sixth Edition folded them into instants.
- **Keywords**: flying, first strike, trample, regeneration, banding, protection and landwalk (like islandwalk on Lord of Atlantis's Merfolk) all appear. Banding has since been retired.
- **The color pie**: blue draws cards and counters spells (Ancestral Recall, Counterspell), black kills creatures and bends the rules for power (Dark Ritual, Demonic Tutor), red burns (Lightning Bolt, Fireball), green ramps and makes big creatures (Llanowar Elves, Birds of Paradise), and white protects and controls the board (Swords to Plowshares, Wrath of God).
- **Ante**: the original rules included playing for ante, where each player risked a random card from their deck. A few cards, such as Contract from Below, Darkpact and Demonic Attorney, only work with ante. Ante is no longer part of sanctioned Magic, and these cards are banned in every format.

The "Power Nine" is the name for Black Lotus, Ancestral Recall, Time Walk, Timetwister and the five Moxen (Pearl, Sapphire, Jet, Ruby and Emerald). They are the most powerful cards from Magic's early years and are on the Reserved List.

## Limited

The first sets weren't drafted the way Limited is played today. In 1993 most people bought a 60-card starter deck and some 15-card boosters, then played casually or for ante. Booster draft as a format came later, and these sets were never balanced for it. Rares like the Power Nine sat on the same sheet as very weak cards, and color balance was rough.

If you play a nostalgia "cube" of these cards today, the usual rules apply: value removal like Lightning Bolt and Swords to Plowshares, and evasive creatures like Serra Angel and Shivan Dragon. Our guide to [evaluating cards for Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) explains why.

## The meta: how it played in Constructed

### Before formats

In 1993 and most of 1994, there were no formats at all. Players built decks from any cards they owned, and decks with multiple Black Lotuses or several copies of Ancestral Recall were possible. In January 1994 the first banned and restricted list arrived, restricting cards like Ancestral Recall, Black Lotus and Braingeyser to one copy per deck and banning the ante cards. In January 1995, Type II (the format now called Standard) was announced, using the current core set, then Revised, and the most recent expansions. The original card pool lived on as Type I, which became Vintage.

Because Revised left out the Power Nine and other powerful cards, those cards never appeared in Type II. Many Alpha cards did keep playing in Type II through reprints in Revised, Fourth Edition and later core sets, which is part of why cards like Wrath of God, Lightning Bolt and Counterspell became so familiar.

### Vintage and Legacy

As of October 2026, these sets are most relevant in Eternal formats:

- **Vintage** allows almost every card, but restricts the strongest to one copy. The Power Nine, Sol Ring, Mana Vault, Demonic Tutor, Channel, Balance, Wheel of Fortune and Time Vault are all restricted there.
- **Legacy** bans the Power Nine and other fast mana and card draw, including Sol Ring, Mana Vault, Demonic Tutor, Mind Twist, Balance, Channel, Wheel of Fortune and Time Vault. But many of these sets' cards are pillars of Legacy: the original dual lands, Swords to Plowshares, Lightning Bolt, Dark Ritual, Counterspell and Animate Dead.
- **Banned everywhere**: the ante cards, Chaos Orb (a dexterity card you flip onto the table) and Crusade, which Wizards banned in all formats in 2020 among a group of cards with racist names or imagery.

### Pauper, Commander and Modern

In Pauper, Lightning Bolt, Counterspell and Dark Ritual are legal through common printings, while Sinkhole is banned there. In Commander, Sol Ring is in almost every deck, and Demonic Tutor, Timetwister, Wheel of Fortune and Mana Vault are legal, but Black Lotus, the Moxen, Ancestral Recall, Time Walk, Balance, Channel and Fastbond are banned. None of these printings are legal in Modern, though reprinted cards like Lightning Bolt and Birds of Paradise are. Our [formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) guide covers how these formats are defined.

### The Reserved List

In 1996, Wizards created the Reserved List, a promise never to reprint certain cards. Forty-seven of Alpha's unique cards are on it, including the Power Nine, the nine dual lands in Alpha (plus Volcanic Island from Beta), Time Vault, Fastbond and Lich. That's why the original dual lands and the Power Nine haven't been reprinted in tournament-legal form since the list was created, while non-Reserved cards like Sol Ring, Lightning Bolt and Counterspell have been printed many times.

## Cards from Alpha, Beta and Unlimited worth owning

These cards are worth knowing even if you never own an original copy, since most have playable reprints:

- **Lightning Bolt**: the benchmark burn spell, legal from Modern to Pauper.
- **Swords to Plowshares**: the most efficient creature removal in Legacy and Commander.
- **Sol Ring**: the most-played Commander card.
- **Counterspell**: the original two-mana counter.
- **Dark Ritual** and **Animate Dead**: the core of Legacy Reanimator and storm-style combo.
- **The original dual lands**: the backbone of Legacy and Vintage mana bases.

## The takeaway

Alpha, Beta and Unlimited are the foundation of everything in Magic. Most of their cards were simple by today's standards, but a few of them were so strong that the game built formats, restricted lists and even a reprint policy around them. If you want to play with their best ideas today, Legacy is the most natural home, using reprints of cards like Animate Dead, Dark Ritual and Swords to Plowshares.
