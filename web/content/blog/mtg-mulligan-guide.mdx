In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), keep a seven-card hand when it has enough lands to cast its spells, the right colors, and a realistic path to doing what your deck is built to do. Mulligan when it doesn't. Under the London mulligan you always draw a fresh seven and then put one card on the bottom for each mulligan you've taken, so a mulligan costs you one card of quantity but lets you pick the best six from seven.

That's the whole decision in two sentences. The rest of this guide is about applying it: how to read a hand quickly, how your deck and your position change the answer, and the mistakes that cost the most games.

## How the London mulligan works

At the start of a game, each player draws seven cards. Starting with the player who goes first, each player decides whether to keep or mulligan.

If you mulligan, you shuffle your hand back into your library and draw a new seven. You can repeat this as many times as you like. Once you decide to keep, you put a number of cards from your hand on the bottom of your library equal to the number of times you mulliganed, in any order you choose.

So:

| Mulligans taken | You draw | You put on the bottom | You keep |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 7 | 0 | 7 |
| 1 | 7 | 1 | 6 |
| 2 | 7 | 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 7 | 3 | 4 |

The key detail is that you always see seven cards. A mulligan to six isn't a random six-card hand. It's the best six cards out of seven, which is much stronger than older mulligan rules allowed. That makes mulliganing a bad seven less painful than many players assume.

### Commander's free mulligan

In multiplayer games, including most Commander games, your first mulligan is free. You shuffle, draw a new seven, and if you keep, you don't put any cards on the bottom for that first mulligan. Your second mulligan costs one card, your third costs two, and so on.

The free mulligan exists because a Commander deck is 100 cards with lots of one-of effects, and a hand that does nothing in a long multiplayer game is miserable. Use it. A hand with one land or no plan is worth throwing back when the redraw is free. Check with your table if you're playing two-player Commander or a house variant, since groups sometimes use different mulligan rules.

## How to evaluate a seven-card hand

Go through these questions in order. Most hands are decided by the first two.

### 1. Land count

In a typical 60-card deck with around 22 to 26 lands, a hand with 2 to 5 lands is in the rough keepable range. Fewer than two and you need to draw lands immediately to function. More than five and you have too few spells to do anything with your mana.

- **0 or 1 land:** almost always a mulligan. The rare exception is a deck built around very cheap spells with a one-lander full of one-drops, and even then it's a gamble.
- **2 lands:** keepable if your hand has cheap spells you can cast with those two lands. Stronger on the draw, since you see an extra card before you need a third land.
- **3 or 4 lands:** the sweet spot for most decks.
- **5 lands:** keepable if the two spells are good and your deck has expensive cards that want the mana. Weak in a fast deck.
- **6 or 7 lands:** mulligan.

These ranges shift with your deck. A low-curve aggro deck with fewer lands is happy with two. A control deck full of five-mana spells wants three or four. Your [mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) tells you how much mana your hand actually needs.

### 2. Colors

Count lands by color, not just in total. Three lands that all make red mana won't cast the blue spells in your hand. Ask whether you can cast your early spells on curve with the lands you have. A hand that can cast everything except one late-game card is fine. A hand that can't cast any of its spells until it draws a specific land type is close to a one-lander.

### 3. Curve

Look at what you can do on turns one through three. A hand whose cheapest spell costs four mana spends the early game doing nothing, which is fine against some decks and fatal against others. A good hand usually has a play to make on turn two or three.

### 4. Does the hand do what your deck does?

This is the question beginners skip. Every deck has a job. An aggro deck needs to pressure the opponent early. A control deck needs answers and lands. A combo deck needs its pieces or ways to find them. A ramp deck needs ramp and something to ramp into.

A hand with three lands and four random spells can be technically functional and still lose because it doesn't advance your plan. Ask: if I draw nothing useful for three turns, does this hand still do its job?

## On the play vs on the draw

In a two-player game, the player who goes first skips their first draw. That makes a real difference to which hands you keep.

**On the play** you're a card behind but a turn ahead. Your hand has to function with exactly the seven cards you see, plus one draw per turn starting on turn two. Speed matters more, so hands with early plays get better and slow hands get worse.

**On the draw** you see one more card before your first land drop matters, which makes risky two-land hands more keepable. You're also reacting to your opponent's start, so interaction like removal becomes more valuable.

Rule of thumb: be a little stricter about land count on the play, and a little more willing to keep a two-lander with cheap spells on the draw.

## Worked examples: aggro

Picture a mono-red aggro deck with 20 lands, plenty of one- and two-drop creatures, and burn spells.

**Hand A:** Mountain, Mountain, Monastery Swiftspear, Goblin Guide, Lightning Bolt, Lava Spike, Rift Bolt.

**Keep.** Two lands is enough for a deck where almost everything costs one mana. You have creatures on turn one, burn to finish, and a fast clock. This is exactly what the deck wants to do.

**Hand B:** Mountain, Mountain, Mountain, Mountain, Mountain, Lightning Bolt, Goblin Guide.

**Mulligan.** Five lands in a deck that tops out at two or three mana means two spells of action. Aggro wins by deploying threats fast, and this hand runs out of gas on turn two. A six-card hand built from a fresh seven is likely to be better.

**Hand C:** Mountain, Monastery Swiftspear, Goblin Guide, Lightning Bolt, Lava Spike, Rift Bolt, Skewer the Critics.

**Mulligan, usually.** One land with six one- and two-mana spells is tempting because everything is cheap. But you can cast one spell per turn until you draw more lands, and in a 20-land deck that's not guaranteed. On the draw against a slow deck some players keep this, but the safer choice is to send it back.

## Worked examples: control

Now picture a blue-white control deck with 26 lands, counterspells, removal, board wipes and a few expensive finishers.

**Hand D:** Island, Island, Plains, Glacial Fortress, Counterspell, Supreme Verdict, Opt.

**Keep.** Four lands with both colors, cheap interaction, a board wipe and a cantrip to dig. This hand can answer early threats, wipe the board on turn four and keep hitting land drops. It does what control does.

**Hand E:** Island, Plains, Supreme Verdict, Wrath of God, Sphinx's Revelation, Teferi, Hero of Dominaria, Dig Through Time.

**Mulligan.** Two lands and nothing that costs less than four mana. Control can afford a slow start, but this hand needs to hit two more land drops in a row just to cast its first spell, and it has no early answers if the opponent starts fast.

**Hand F:** Island, Island, Plains, Plains, Glacial Fortress, Counterspell, Sphinx's Revelation.

**Keep, especially against a slow deck.** Five lands is usually too many, but control is the archetype that wants to hit every land drop and cast expensive spells. With a counterspell for the early game and a powerful card draw spell for the late game, this hand is fine. The same hand in the aggro deck above would be a clear mulligan, which is the point: the right answer depends on the deck.

## Mulligans in Limited

In Limited (draft and sealed), decks are 40 cards with around 17 lands. A few differences from Constructed:

- **Hands are less consistent.** Your deck has fewer redundant cards, so you'll see more awkward hands. Don't hold every hand to Constructed standards, but don't keep bad hands just because the deck is weak, either.
- **Lands matter most.** Most Limited games are decided by who uses their mana better. Three or four lands is ideal. Two-landers are keepable on the draw with cheap spells. One-landers and six-landers are mulligans.
- **Colors are fragile.** Many Limited decks run two colors with roughly even land splits. A hand with only one color of land and spells of the other is close to unplayable.
- **Bombs aren't a reason to keep.** A hand with your best rare and one land is still a one-lander.
- **Know your format's speed.** In a fast Limited environment, a hand with no plays before turn four is risky. In a slower one, it might be fine.

## What to put on the bottom

After you keep a mulligan, you choose which cards to bottom. The principle is simple: bottom the cards that are least useful in this hand, and keep a balanced mix of lands and spells.

- **Fix the land count first.** If you have too many lands, bottom a land. If you have just enough, bottom a spell.
- **Bottom the most expensive card you can't cast soon.** A seven-mana spell in a three-land hand is often the first to go.
- **Keep what your deck needs to function.** In aggro, keep the cheap threats. In control, keep the interaction. In combo, keep the pieces or the ways to find them.
- **Bottom redundant or situational cards.** A second copy of a legendary card, or a sideboard-style answer that doesn't match the opponent's deck, is a good candidate.
- **Remember what you bottomed.** Those cards are at the bottom of your library, and you probably won't draw them this game. If you bottomed your only copy of a key card, plan around not having it.

## Common mulligan mistakes

These are the errors that cost the most games, roughly in order of how often they show up. (For more general habits, see [common MTG beginner mistakes](/blog/mtg-beginner-mistakes).)

1. **Keeping one-landers because the spells are good.** Spells you can't cast are blank cards. A great hand with one land is a bad hand.
2. **Keeping seven lands' worth of nothing.** Five or six lands with weak spells feels safe because you won't miss land drops, but you won't do anything either.
3. **Fearing the mulligan too much.** Under the London mulligan, a six is the best six of seven. It's a real cost, but a functional six usually beats a non-functional seven.
4. **Ignoring play/draw.** The same two-lander can be a keep on the draw and a mulligan on the play.
5. **Ignoring the matchup.** In game two and three, you know what your opponent is playing. A hand full of answers for creatures is great against aggro and weak against a deck with no creatures. After sideboarding, evaluate hands against the deck in front of you. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers how to plan those games.
6. **Keeping hands that can't do the deck's job.** Functional isn't enough. Ask whether the hand advances your plan.
7. **Rushing the decision.** Take a moment to imagine your first three turns. If you can't see a reasonable sequence of plays, mulligan.

## The short version

Count your lands, check your colors, picture your first three turns, and ask whether the hand does what your deck is built to do. If the answer is no, mulligan with confidence. The London mulligan rewards players who throw back bad sevens, and Commander's free mulligan makes it even easier. Build a deck with a sound mana base and you'll face these decisions less often.
