You win a game of Magic: The Gathering when every opponent has lost or left the game. Most games end with an opponent's life total reaching 0, but a player also loses by having to draw from an empty library or by getting 10 poison counters, and in Commander by taking 21 combat damage from a single commander. A handful of cards add their own ways to win, and any player can concede at any time.

This guide covers each way to win and lose, with real card examples. If you're brand new, the [beginner's guide to playing Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) explains the basics of a turn first.

## How winning and losing work

Magic is built mostly around losing rather than winning. The rules list a few conditions that make a player lose. You win when all of your opponents have lost.

Most of these losses happen through **state-based actions**: checks the game makes automatically whenever a player would get priority, which is the chance to cast a spell or use an ability. Nobody has to announce them. If a condition is true when the game checks, that player loses right away, without using the stack.

That timing matters. If you are at 0 life when the game checks, you lose, even if you were about to gain life on your next turn. You can't respond to a state-based action, because it doesn't use the [stack](/blog/how-the-stack-works-mtg).

## 1. Reduce an opponent to 0 life

Each player starts at 20 life in most formats (40 in Commander). If a player's life total is 0 or less, they lose the next time state-based actions are checked.

Life can go below zero. A player at 2 life who takes 5 damage goes to -3 and loses. If an effect lets them stay in the game somehow, they'd need to gain enough life to climb back above zero.

Ways to deal damage or cause life loss:

- **Combat damage.** Attacking with creatures is how most games are won. [MTG combat explained](/blog/mtg-combat-explained) covers attacking and blocking step by step.
- **Direct damage.** Spells like Lightning Bolt deal damage to a player or other target.
- **Life loss.** Some effects make a player lose life rather than deal damage. The difference matters because damage prevention stops damage but not life loss.

## 2. Drawing from an empty library

If a player is required to draw a card while their library has no cards in it, they lose the next time state-based actions are checked. This is often called **decking** or **milling out**.

Two details catch people out:

- **Having an empty library isn't enough.** You only lose when you have to draw and can't. A player with zero cards in their library is still in the game until their next draw.
- **Every draw counts.** It isn't only the draw step. If a card makes you draw three and you have two cards left, you'll lose when the game next checks.

**Mill** is the strategy of putting cards from an opponent's library into their graveyard to run them out. A card like Glimpse the Unthinkable mills ten cards at once. Milling doesn't make the opponent lose directly. It sets up the draw from an empty library.

## 3. Ten poison counters

A player with 10 or more poison counters loses the game. Poison counters are separate from life: you can be at full life and still lose to poison.

Ways to give poison counters:

- **Infect.** A creature with infect deals damage to players as poison counters and to creatures as -1/-1 counters. Damage from infect doesn't reduce life at all.
- **Toxic.** A creature with toxic gives poison counters in addition to its normal combat damage when it deals combat damage to a player. Toxic 2 means two poison counters.
- **Poisonous.** An older keyword: when the creature deals combat damage to a player, that player also gets the listed number of poison counters.
- **Proliferate.** Proliferate lets you add one more of each kind of counter already on any number of players and permanents you choose. It's a common way to push a poisoned opponent closer to 10.

Poison works the same in Commander: 10 counters, even though players start at 40 life. That makes poison a faster route there than in most other formats.

## 4. 21 commander damage

In Commander, a player who has been dealt 21 or more combat damage by the same commander over the course of a game loses. This is tracked separately for each commander.

- Damage from two different commanders doesn't add together.
- Only combat damage counts. A commander that deals damage with an ability isn't adding to the total.
- It's tracked per commander for the whole game, even if that commander leaves and comes back.

Commander damage is why a big, evasive commander can be a threat even when everyone is at 40 life. [What is Commander?](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) covers the rest of the format's rules.

## 5. Alternate win cards

Some cards say "you win the game" when a condition is met. When one resolves, or its condition is met as the card describes, you win immediately. In a multiplayer game, every opponent loses.

| Card | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Laboratory Maniac | If you would draw a card while your library is empty, you win the game instead. |
| Thassa's Oracle | When it enters, you look at the top cards of your library equal to your devotion to blue. If that number is at least the number of cards in your library, you win the game. |
| Felidar Sovereign | At the beginning of your upkeep, if you have 40 or more life, you win the game. |
| Test of Endurance | At the beginning of your upkeep, if you have 50 or more life, you win the game. |
| Approach of the Second Sun | If you cast it from your hand and have already cast another one this game, you win. Otherwise it goes back into your library seventh from the top and you gain 7 life. |
| Coalition Victory | You win if you control a land of each basic land type and a creature of each color. |
| Maze's End | Its ability searches your library for a Gate. If you then control ten or more Gates with different names, you win the game. |
| Battle of Wits | At the beginning of your upkeep, if you have 200 or more cards in your library, you win the game. |

### Laboratory Maniac and Thassa's Oracle

These two turn your own empty library into a win instead of a loss. Laboratory Maniac replaces the draw that would make you lose with a win. Thassa's Oracle checks once, as its enters ability resolves, whether your devotion to blue (the number of blue mana symbols in the mana costs of permanents you control) is at least the number of cards left in your library. With an empty library, the Oracle alone is enough, because its own two blue symbols count.

Both are popular in [combo decks](/blog/mtg-combo-decks-explained) that pair them with a card that empties your library quickly.

### Felidar Sovereign and life totals

Felidar Sovereign needs 40 or more life at the beginning of your upkeep, and still needs it when the trigger resolves. In a 20-life format, that takes a lot of lifegain. In Commander, where you start at 40, the bar is very different: you only need to stay there.

### The upkeep check

Many alternate win cards trigger at the beginning of your upkeep. That gives opponents a full turn cycle to answer the card or knock you below the threshold. Plan to protect the card, or to meet the condition right before your upkeep.

## Cards that stop wins and losses

A few cards change these rules. Platinum Angel says you can't lose the game and your opponents can't win the game. While it's on the battlefield, you can be at 0 life or past 10 poison counters and stay in. Once it leaves, the next state-based action check ends the game if you still meet a loss condition.

Other cards make a player lose directly. Door to Nothingness can be activated, at a large mana cost, to make a target player lose the game.

## 6. Concession

Any player can concede at any time, which means they lose the game immediately. Conceding can't be stopped or responded to, and you don't need to wait for priority.

In a two-player game, the opponent wins. In a multiplayer game, the conceding player leaves and the rest keep playing. When a player leaves a multiplayer game, the cards they own leave with them, so the board can change a lot.

Etiquette varies. Conceding a hopeless game to save time is normal. Conceding to hurt someone, such as quitting to deny another player a win in a multiplayer game, is usually frowned upon.

## Draws

If every remaining player loses at the same time, the game is a draw. This can happen, for example, when an effect deals damage to every player and they all hit 0 together. In tournaments, draws can also happen when a match runs out of time, depending on the event's rules.

## Examples

A few short scenarios show how these rules play out:

- **The decking timing.** Your opponent's library is empty after a mill spell on your turn. They don't lose yet. They lose when they have to draw at the start of their turn.
- **Poison at full life.** In Commander, a player at 40 life who has taken 10 infect damage loses, even though their life total hasn't changed.
- **Two commanders.** A player has taken 15 combat damage from one commander and 10 from another. They haven't lost to commander damage, because neither commander has reached 21.
- **Laboratory Maniac.** You control Laboratory Maniac with an empty library and draw a card. Instead of losing, you win.

## The takeaway

Most games end at 0 life, but a player also loses by drawing from an empty library or by having 10 poison counters, and Commander adds 21 combat damage from a single commander. Alternate win cards like Thassa's Oracle and Felidar Sovereign give you a different finish line, and any player can concede at any time. Knowing every route helps you spot when you're closer to winning, or losing, than the life totals suggest.
