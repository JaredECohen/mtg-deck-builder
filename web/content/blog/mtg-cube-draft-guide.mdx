A cube is a curated collection of Magic: The Gathering cards, usually one copy of each, that a group drafts from instead of opening booster packs. Players sit around a table, open packs made from the cube, draft cards one at a time, and build decks from what they picked, exactly like a normal booster draft. The difference is that every card in the pool was chosen by the cube's owner, so the format can be as powerful, nostalgic or themed as they like.

This guide explains what a cube is, how a cube draft runs, how to build your own, and why it's one of Magic's most beloved ways to play.

## What a cube is

A cube is a set of cards built for drafting. A few things define most cubes:

- **It's usually singleton.** Each card appears once. That makes every pick feel unique and keeps the pool varied.
- **It's curated.** The owner picks every card. There are no filler commons unless the owner wants them.
- **It's reusable.** After a draft, the cards go back into the cube. You can draft the same cube many times, and each draft plays out differently.
- **It's balanced around archetypes.** A good cube supports several strategies in each color and color pair, so players can draft many different decks.

Today the word means any curated draft pool, whether it lives in a deck box, a binder or an online client.

### How big is a cube?

Cube size depends on how many people will draft. The math is simple: if each player opens a set number of packs with a set number of cards each, the cube needs at least that many cards per player. A common setup is three packs of 15 cards for each of eight players, which takes 360 cards. Many cubes are built at that size or larger, so the owner can rotate cards in and out and every draft sees a different subset.

## How a cube draft works

If you've played booster draft, you already know how to draft a cube. If not, [draft vs sealed](/blog/draft-vs-sealed-limited-guide) explains how Limited formats work. Here's the cube version in short.

1. **Make packs.** Shuffle the cube and deal it into packs, usually 15 cards each, three per player.
2. **Open and pick.** Each player opens a pack, takes one card, and passes the rest to the next player.
3. **Keep passing.** Pick one card from each pack you're passed until it's empty. The direction alternates between packs, usually left, then right, then left.
4. **Build a deck.** Once all packs are drafted, build a deck from your picks plus as many basic lands as you need. Most cubes use Limited deck rules: a minimum of 40 cards.
5. **Play.** Players are paired off and play matches, typically best of three.

### How cube drafting differs from set drafts

Cube drafts look like normal drafts, but they play differently:

- **Every pick is strong.** A set's booster has a rare, a few uncommons and many commons. A cube pack might be full of cards that would be first picks in a normal draft. You're often choosing between several excellent cards.
- **Power matters more than in most sets.** Cubes often include famously strong cards from Magic's history, so games can swing fast.
- **Archetypes are deliberate.** The cube designer chose the cards to support specific decks, like blue-white control, red aggro or green ramp. Finding an open archetype is a big part of drafting well.
- **Mana fixing is curated.** Dual lands and other fixing cards appear in carefully chosen numbers, so playing more than two colors is a decision, not an accident.

Card evaluation still matters, though. The [guide to evaluating cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) covers the core skills, and they carry over to cube with a twist: in cube, removal and efficient threats are plentiful, so cards need to be truly strong to make your deck.

### Cube drafting tips

- **Pick strong, flexible cards early.** Colorless and mono-colored cards keep your options open.
- **Watch what's coming.** If the strong cards of one color keep arriving late, that color is probably open.
- **Commit to an archetype.** Cubes reward focused decks more than piles of good cards.
- **Value mana fixing.** In a multicolor deck, good dual lands are often worth an early pick.

## Cube power levels

One of the best things about cubes is that you can tune them to any power level. A few common styles:

### High-power cubes

These cubes include many of the most powerful cards ever printed, often including the Power Nine (the famous, extremely rare cards like Black Lotus and Ancestral Recall). Games are fast and explosive. Magic Online has long offered a high-power cube, and many players' first cube experience is something in that style. Physical high-power cubes are often built with playtest cards or proxies for casual use, because the real cards are rare and expensive. Groups decide that for themselves.

### Legacy or unpowered cubes

These cubes include strong cards but leave out the most extreme ones like the Power Nine. They still feel powerful, but games last a bit longer.

### Pauper and peasant cubes

A pauper cube uses only cards that have been printed at common, much like the [Pauper format](/blog/what-is-pauper-mtg). A peasant cube allows commons and uncommons. These cubes are cheaper to build and play more like a traditional set draft, with more focus on board battles and combat.

### Themed cubes

Some cubes are built around a theme: a single set or block, a favorite era of Magic, a tribal theme, or only cards from a specific plane. There are cubes built for Commander-style multiplayer, too.

The right power level depends on your group. A high-power cube can feel chaotic to newer players. A pauper cube can feel slow to players who love big plays. Many cube owners keep more than one cube for different crowds.

## Building your own cube

Building a cube is a project many players enjoy as much as the drafts themselves. Here's a process that works.

### Choose a size and power level

Decide how many players you'll usually have and how strong you want the cube to be. A 360-card cube supports eight players with three 15-card packs. Smaller groups can use smaller cubes or fewer packs per player.

### Balance the colors

Most cubes give each of the five colors roughly the same number of cards, then add sections for multicolor cards, colorless cards (mostly artifacts) and lands. Balance keeps every color draftable and prevents one strategy from dominating.

### Design archetypes

Think about the decks you want people to be able to draft, and make sure the cube supports them. A typical approach is to give each two-color pair a theme, like red-white aggro or blue-black control, and include enough cards for each. Cards that work in more than one archetype are especially valuable, because they keep drafters flexible.

### Plan your curve and removal

Like a normal Limited format, a cube needs plenty of cheap creatures, a healthy amount of removal, and enough card draw. Check that every color has early plays and answers, or some decks will be unplayable.

### Include fixing

If you want multicolor decks to be possible, include dual lands and other mana fixing. If you want most decks to be two colors, include less.

### Test and update

After a few drafts, look at which cards nobody picks and which ones always go first. Replace dead cards with ones that do more, and consider swapping out cards that warp every draft around them. A cube is never finished.

Many cube designers use free online tools to manage their lists, generate packs and draft remotely. They're handy, but a spreadsheet and a box of cards work just as well.

## Why players love cube

Cube has a devoted following, and it's easy to see why:

- **Every draft is different.** The same cube rarely produces the same decks twice.
- **It's full of great cards.** You get to play with cards you might never use otherwise.
- **It's a creative outlet.** Building and tuning a cube is a design challenge.
- **It's free to replay.** Once the cube is built, you can draft it again and again without buying packs.
- **It's social.** A cube night brings a group together for a whole evening of drafting and playing.

If you want to see how cube fits in with every other way to play, [the formats overview](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) compares them.

## The takeaway

A cube is a curated, usually singleton pool of cards that a group drafts like a booster set, then reuses forever. Pick a power level that fits your group, balance the colors, design archetypes for each color pair, and update the list after every few drafts. When you draft, take strong flexible cards early, read what's open, and commit to a focused deck.
