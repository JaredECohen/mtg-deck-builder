In Magic: The Gathering (MTG), the metagame is the mix of decks that people are actually playing in a given format at a given place and time. It is "the game around the game": before a single card is drawn, your choice of deck, sideboard and play style is already a bet on what you expect to face. Reading the metagame well means knowing which strategies are popular, why they are popular, and how to position your own deck against them.

This guide explains what a metagame is, how to read one without relying on hype, how to choose a deck and sideboard for the field you expect, and why the room at your local store rarely looks like the lists you see online. It doesn't make any claims about what is good right now. Metagames change constantly, and the method here works in any format.

## What "metagame" actually means

Every format has a card pool and a set of rules. Within those limits, players build decks, and over time certain decks become common because they win, because they're cheap, because they're fun, or simply because a well-known player posted a list. The total of those choices is the metagame.

A few related terms come up constantly:

- **Archetype:** a family of decks with the same core plan, such as burn, control or reanimator. Two players can run different lists of the same archetype. If the vocabulary is new, [the deck archetypes guide](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) covers the main families.
- **Meta share:** the rough percentage of the field playing a given archetype.
- **Tier:** an informal grouping of decks by how often they appear and how well they do. "Tier one" usually means the decks you should expect to face most.
- **Rogue deck:** a deck outside the established archetypes, often built specifically to beat what everyone else is playing.
- **Metagaming:** deliberately choosing cards or decks because of what you expect opponents to play, rather than only on raw card quality.

The key idea is that no deck is good in a vacuum. A deck is good against a particular field. A slow, powerful control deck may be excellent when everyone plays midrange and terrible when half the room plays fast aggro.

## Why metagames shift

Metagames move in a rough cycle, and understanding that cycle is more useful than memorizing any single snapshot.

1. **A strategy wins.** A deck posts good results and gets attention.
2. **Players copy it.** Its share of the field rises.
3. **Players adapt.** Opponents add sideboard cards that punish it, or switch to decks that naturally beat it.
4. **The counter-deck rises.** Now the deck that beats the old favourite is the one to watch.
5. **Something beats the counter-deck,** and the cycle continues.

New sets, banned and restricted announcements, and rotation in formats like Standard reset parts of this cycle. Between those events, the field still moves every week as players react to each other. That's why "what's the best deck?" is often the wrong question. "What will people be playing, and what beats that?" is the right one.

## How to read a metagame

Reading the metagame is gathering evidence and weighing it honestly. There are several sources, and each has blind spots.

### Tournament results

Published decklists from events show what performed well. They're useful, but they describe what happened, not what will happen next. A deck that won last weekend will be more popular this weekend, and players will arrive ready for it. Results also over-represent decks that are good at winning large events, which isn't the same as what your local players bring.

### Your own match history

Write down what you play against. After a dozen events or a few dozen online matches you'll have a small, honest sample of the field you actually face. Note the archetype, whether you won, and what decided the game. This is the most underrated data source in Magic because it reflects your real opponents.

### Talking to players

At a local store, conversation is metagame data. If three regulars mention they've been building the same deck, that matters more than any online list. People also tend to stick with decks they own, especially in formats where cards are expensive, so last month's field is a strong predictor of this month's.

### Questions to ask

When you look at any source, ask:

- **Which archetypes are most common?** These are the matchups you must not lose badly.
- **What speed is the format?** If many decks can win quickly, slow plans and expensive cards get worse.
- **What kind of interaction is popular?** A field full of counterspells punishes expensive single threats. A field full of creature removal punishes decks with few creatures.
- **What is everyone ignoring?** If no one plays graveyard hate, graveyard decks get better. If no one plays artifact removal, artifact strategies have room.

## Choosing a deck for the metagame

Once you have a picture of the field, there are three broad ways to approach deck choice.

### Play the strongest known deck

The safest choice is usually a deck that is proven and that you know well. Its weaknesses are known too, so expect opponents to be ready for it, but a familiar deck played well beats an unfamiliar "perfect" deck played badly. If you've only just picked up a list, practice matters more than positioning.

### Play the deck that beats the strongest deck

If one archetype dominates, choosing something with a natural edge against it can pay off. The risk is that you lose to everything else. This works best when the dominant deck really is a large share of the field, and when your choice isn't hopeless against the second and third most common decks.

### Go rogue

Building something new to exploit a gap in the metagame can be rewarding, especially at a small local event where you know the regulars. Rogue decks often win because opponents don't know how to play against them. They also tend to be less tested, so they can have holes you haven't found yet.

### Know your role in each matchup

Whatever you pick, think through each common matchup in advance. Who is the aggressor and who is the defender? The [beatdown guide](/blog/whos-the-beatdown-mtg) explains why getting this wrong loses games. Knowing your role against each major deck before the event saves you from working it out mid-game.

## Adjusting your sideboard

In best-of-three play, your sideboard is the most direct tool for adapting to the metagame. The main deck is built for the field as a whole, and the sideboard handles specific problem matchups. The mechanics of swapping cards between games are covered in [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101). Here we focus on choosing what goes in.

### Weight sideboard slots by frequency

Spend your slots on the matchups you'll actually face. Three cards for the most common deck in the room are worth more than three cards for a deck you might see once a month. A simple method: list the top four or five archetypes you expect, then decide how many slots each deserves based on how often you'll play it and how bad the matchup is without help.

### Prefer flexible cards

A sideboard card that's good against two or three archetypes is worth more than a narrow one that only answers a single deck. Broad graveyard hate, artifact or enchantment removal that hits several types of threat, and cheap counterspells that stop both combo pieces and big spells all earn their slots more often.

### Watch what others sideboard

If the field is packed with cards aimed at a certain strategy, consider whether your deck is the target. If everyone is running graveyard hate, your graveyard deck may need a backup plan for games two and three, or you may want a different deck.

### Main-deck adjustments

When a deck is common enough, it can be right to move answers to it into the main deck. If most opponents play creatures with a particular weakness, a removal spell that exploits that weakness can earn a main-deck slot. In best-of-one, where there's no sideboarding between games, this kind of main-deck tuning matters even more. The [best-of-one vs best-of-three guide](/blog/best-of-one-vs-best-of-three) explains how deck building differs between the two.

## Local vs online metagames

The metagame isn't one thing. It's different in every room and on every platform.

### Local metagames

A local store event is a small, stable group of players. That makes it predictable. The same people bring the same decks, often for months. Budget plays a bigger role, because people play what they own, and personal taste matters, because regulars play decks they enjoy. A local field might be full of decks that rarely appear in large tournaments.

That predictability is an advantage. If you know four of the eight regulars play creature-heavy decks, you can tune your list for them in a way that would be risky at a large event.

### Online metagames

Online play has a far larger, more varied population, and it shifts faster. Players can build any deck without owning paper cards, so popular lists spread quickly. Ladder or queue play also pairs you with opponents of varying skill and deck choice, so a single snapshot of the field can be misleading. Your own recorded match history is the best guide here.

### Large tournaments

Big events tend to look like the published results, since many players arrive with the decks that have been winning. Expect the established archetypes and expect opponents to be prepared for them.

### Casual and Commander tables

Even casual play has a metagame. In Commander, your pod's metagame is the decks your friends bring and how strong they are. Reading it means noticing what keeps winning, adjusting your removal and interaction, and talking about power level before the game. The point isn't to out-tune your friends, but to bring a deck that leads to a good game for the table.

## Common metagame mistakes

- **Overreacting to one result.** A single win doesn't make a deck dominant. Look for a pattern across several sources.
- **Chasing the last metagame.** By the time a deck is widely known as the best, many players are prepared for it.
- **Ignoring your own skill.** Switching to a well-positioned deck you've never played often costs more than it gains.
- **Building a sideboard for everything.** Fifteen cards can't beat every deck. Prioritize the matchups you'll face.
- **Forgetting the field is people.** At a local event, the metagame is your friends' preferences and budgets, not a spreadsheet.

## The takeaway

The metagame is the field you expect to face, and every deck-building decision is a bet on it. Gather evidence from results, your own match history and the players around you, then choose a deck you can play well that is positioned for that field. Spend sideboard slots on the matchups you'll actually see, and expect the field to move as soon as anything gets popular.
