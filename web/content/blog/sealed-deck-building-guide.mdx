To build a Magic: The Gathering Sealed deck, sort your pool by color, find your bombs and removal, and pick the two colors that give you the most strong, playable cards. Then choose about 23 spells with a sensible mana curve, lean on creatures, and add 17 lands split by your color balance. Splash a third color only for a few powerful cards, and only when you have the fixing to support it.

This guide assumes you know how Sealed works. If you don't, [Draft vs Sealed](/blog/draft-vs-sealed-limited-guide) explains the format first. Here, the focus is on the decisions: how to read a pool, how to choose between close color pairs, and how to finish the deck so it plays smoothly.

## Step 1: Sort the pool

Before you judge anything, organize it. Lay out your cards in groups:

- One pile for each of the five colors
- One for multicolored cards
- One for artifacts and other colorless cards
- One for nonbasic lands, especially any that make more than one color

Within each color, sort roughly by mana value. Now you can see at a glance which colors are deep and which are thin, and where each color's curve sits.

Take a minute to read anything unfamiliar. Sealed pools are usually from a new or recent set, and mechanics you haven't seen can change how good a card is.

## Step 2: Find your bombs and removal

In Sealed, you can't draft around your pool's weaknesses. You have what you opened. That makes the two strongest card categories even more important than in Draft.

### Bombs

A **bomb** is a card that wins the game on its own if your opponent doesn't answer it quickly. Big flyers, creatures that generate value every turn, and planeswalkers often qualify. Sealed games tend to be slower than Draft games, so bombs get more time to take over.

Pull out every card you think might be a bomb and note its color. A color with two bombs has a strong claim on your deck.

### Removal

**Removal** is anything that kills or neutralizes an opposing creature: destroy effects, damage spells, fight cards, and Auras that stop a creature from attacking or blocking. Removal is how you answer your opponent's bombs, so it's nearly as valuable as having your own.

Rank your removal. Unconditional removal that handles any creature is best. Cheap, conditional removal comes next. Removal that only hits small creatures, or costs a lot of mana, is still playable but lower priority. [How to evaluate cards in Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) has a full framework for ranking removal and creatures.

## Step 3: Pick your colors

Most Sealed decks are two colors. Two colors give you a reliable mana base, and with an average pool, that's where most of your good cards will be.

Score each color by asking:

1. **How many bombs and good removal spells does it have?**
2. **How many playable cards does it have in total?** A color with a bomb but only five other playables often can't support a deck.
3. **Does it have creatures across the curve?** Colors with plenty of two-, three- and four-drops are easier to build around.

Then look at pairs. Sometimes two colors that look average on their own combine well, because their multicolored cards are good or their creatures fit the same plan. Check the multicolored pile for gold cards that point you to a specific pair.

### When two pairs are close

If two color pairs look roughly equal, pick the one that:

- Has more removal
- Has a better curve, with fewer clunky expensive cards
- Has creatures that block well, since Sealed games often stall

Don't throw out the other pair. It's your plan B between games.

## Step 4: Build the curve

Your **mana curve** is how many cards you have at each mana value. Sealed decks are often slower than Draft decks, but you still need early plays, or a faster opponent will run you over. For the theory, [the mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) goes into depth. For a 40-card Sealed deck, a rough target looks like this:

| Mana value | Rough count |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 0-2 |
| 2 | 4-6 |
| 3 | 4-6 |
| 4 | 3-5 |
| 5 | 2-4 |
| 6 or more | 1-3 |

These are guidelines, not rules. A deck with several strong expensive bombs can afford a heavier top end if it has enough removal and blockers to get there.

### Creatures and spells

Aim for around 15 to 17 creatures and 6 to 8 noncreature spells, mostly removal. Creatures are how you win and how you defend, and Limited decks without enough of them often lose to a board they can't block. If you're short on creatures, you can go a little lower, but try to cut weaker noncreature spells first.

### Cards to cut

When you have more playables than slots, cut in roughly this order:

- **Narrow cards** that only work in specific situations
- **Combat tricks** beyond the best one or two
- **Vanilla creatures** with poor stats for their cost
- **The most expensive cards** if your curve is top-heavy

Keep the cuts handy. Many of them are useful sideboard cards.

## Step 5: Play 17 lands

The standard for a 40-card Limited deck is **17 lands and 23 spells**. That ratio gives you a good chance of hitting your land drops through the early and middle turns without flooding too often.

Adjust slightly when your deck pushes you to:

- **16 lands** if your deck is low to the ground, with very few cards above four mana.
- **18 lands** if you have several expensive bombs and plenty of ways to use extra mana.

Most decks should stay at 17.

### Splitting your basics

Split your lands roughly in proportion to your colored cards, weighted toward the color with more early plays and heavier colored costs. If your main color has most of the two-drops and cards with two colored symbols, give it more lands. A 9-8 or 10-7 split is common for an even two-color deck.

Count any nonbasic lands that make your colors as sources for both.

## Step 6: Decide on a splash

A **splash** means adding a small number of cards from a third color, supported by a few lands or fixing cards. Splashing can add power to a Sealed deck, because Sealed is slower and you have more time to find the right mana.

### When to splash

Splash when:

- The card is a bomb or premium removal that your main colors can't replace
- The splash cards have only one colored symbol of the third color, not two
- You have fixing: dual lands, mana creatures or artifacts that make any color, or cards that find basic lands

Don't splash for an average card. The cost of an inconsistent mana base usually outweighs a small upgrade.

### How to splash

Keep the splash to one to three cards. For mana, you'll typically want three or four sources of the splash color, counting fixing lands and cards that find it. Replace basics from your main colors carefully, and don't splash early drops, since you'll rarely have the splash color in the first few turns.

## Step 7: Plan your sideboard

In Sealed, your whole pool is your sideboard. Between games, look at what your opponent is doing:

- **Lots of flyers?** Bring in creatures with reach or flying, or removal that hits flyers.
- **Lots of artifacts or enchantments?** Bring in cards that destroy them.
- **A faster opponent?** Swap expensive cards for cheaper creatures.
- **Your deck isn't working?** Switch to your plan B color pair between matches.

If you're building at an event for the first time, the [prerelease guide](/blog/mtg-sealed-prerelease-guide) covers what to expect around the build itself: timing, what to bring and how rounds run.

## Common Sealed mistakes

- **Building around one bomb in a shallow color.** One great card doesn't make up for a color with too few playables.
- **Too few creatures.** Removal is great, but you need bodies on the battlefield.
- **Ignoring the curve.** Seven five-drops and no two-drops loses to anything fast.
- **Splashing too much.** Three colors with no fixing is a recipe for stuck hands.
- **Playing the wrong land count.** Stay at 17 unless the deck clearly calls for a change.

## The takeaway

Sort your pool, find your bombs and removal, and choose the two colors with the most strong playables, not the flashiest single card. Build a curve with real early plays, aim for 15 to 17 creatures, and play 17 lands. Splash only for powerful cards, and only with the fixing to back it up. Keep your second-best color pair in mind as a plan B.
