MTG deck archetypes are the broad game plans that almost every Magic deck falls into: **aggro** wins fast with cheap creatures, **control** answers everything and wins late, **midrange** trades efficiently and outclasses opponents in the middle turns, **combo** assembles a specific set of cards that wins on the spot, and **tempo** spends a little mana to stay one step ahead. Knowing which archetype you're playing, and which one you're facing, tells you how to build, how to mulligan and how to play every turn.

Below, each archetype gets its plan, curve, land count, defining cards, strengths, weaknesses and how to beat it. Then we cover how archetypes match up and the most useful question in any game: who's the beatdown?

## What an archetype actually is

An archetype isn't a decklist. It's a description of *how a deck intends to win* and *what it's willing to give up* to do so. A red deck full of hasty creatures and a white deck full of small fliers look nothing alike, but both are aggro: they want to deal 20 damage before the opponent's more powerful cards come online.

Every archetype is a trade-off between three resources:

- **Speed**: how early the deck affects the game.
- **Power**: how much each individual card does.
- **Interaction**: how well the deck stops the opponent's plan.

No deck maxes out all three. Aggro buys speed with low card power, control buys interaction and late-game power by doing little early, and the others strike different balances.

Two numbers come up constantly below. **Mana value** is the total mana in a card's cost (Lightning Bolt is 1, Wrath of God is 4). The **mana curve** is how many cards a deck has at each mana value. If those are new to you, the [mana curve guide](/blog/mana-curve-explained) covers them in depth. Land counts here assume a 60-card constructed deck.

## Aggro: win before they can stabilize

**Game plan.** Play cheap creatures from turn one, attack every turn, and finish the opponent with burn or a last push of damage before their bigger spells matter. Aggro treats the opponent's life total as the only resource that counts.

**How it wins.** Usually by turns four to six in a 60-card format. A good aggro draw deals a chunk of damage on each of the first three or four turns, then uses direct damage to close the last few points.

**Typical build.**

- Curve: heavily weighted to 1 and 2 mana, a handful of 3s, very few or no cards above 3.
- Lands: roughly 16 to 20. The low curve means you need fewer lands, and drawing too many is how aggro loses.
- Creatures: often 20 or more.

**Signature cards.** One-mana creatures that hit hard or fast, like **Goblin Guide** (a 2/2 with haste for one red mana) and **Monastery Swiftspear** (a one-drop with haste and prowess, which grows whenever you cast a noncreature spell). Efficient burn like **Lightning Bolt**, which can remove a blocker early or go to the face to finish the game.

**Strengths.**

- Punishes slow starts, clunky draws and greedy mana bases.
- Consistent at doing its one thing, and forces answers early.

**Weaknesses.**

- Runs out of cards. Small creatures don't keep up with bigger ones once the opponent stabilizes.
- Cheap board wipes and lifegain can undo several turns of work.
- Flooding on lands is especially damaging.

**Playing against aggro.** Your life total is now a resource you have to protect. Trade early, even if a trade looks slightly unfavorable on card value, because every turn a creature survives costs you damage. Prioritize cheap removal and blockers, keep hands that do something on turns one to three, and don't take unnecessary damage from your own lands. Once you stabilize, aggro rarely comes back.

## Control: answer everything, then win

**Game plan.** Survive the early game with removal, counterspells and card draw, trade one-for-one or better, and eventually win with a small number of powerful threats once the opponent has nothing left.

**How it wins.** Late. Control is happy for the game to go ten or more turns. It wins through **card advantage** (having more useful cards than the opponent over the long game) and **inevitability** (the opponent can't win a long game against it).

**Typical build.**

- Curve: flatter and higher than aggro, with lots of 2, 3 and 4 mana interaction and a few expensive finishers.
- Lands: roughly 24 to 27. Control must hit every land drop to cast its answers and its big spells.
- Creatures: few, sometimes fewer than five.

**Signature cards.** **Counterspell** (two blue mana: counter target spell) is the archetype's namesake. **Wrath of God** destroys all creatures and represents the board wipe, which lets control trade one card for several. Card draw like **Fact or Fiction** keeps the hand full, and the win conditions are usually a few resilient threats that take over once they stick.

**Strengths.**

- Excellent against decks that rely on a handful of powerful cards.
- Grinds out long games through card advantage.
- Flexible: good answers deal with almost anything.

**Weaknesses.**

- Can be too slow against fast aggro or a combo deck that wins before control's answers line up.
- Draws "the wrong answer" sometimes: a counterspell when a creature is already on the battlefield.
- Games take long, and small mistakes compound.

**Playing against control.** Don't overextend into a board wipe. Commit just enough threats to pressure the control player and keep the rest in hand. Make them act on your turn, cast spells when they're tapped out, and favor threats that come from unusual angles (lands that become creatures, cards that work from the graveyard, abilities that can't be countered). Discard spells and card advantage of your own are strong here, because control lives and dies on having the right answer.

## Midrange: the efficient middle

**Game plan.** Play the most efficient threats and answers available at every point on the curve. Against aggro, midrange acts like a control deck with bigger creatures. Against control, it acts like an aggro deck with better individual cards.

**How it wins.** By trading resources efficiently and landing threats that are simply better than the opponent's. Midrange games often turn on one or two key cards resolving or being answered.

**Typical build.**

- Curve: peaks at 2 and 3 mana, with a meaningful number of 4s and a few 5s.
- Lands: roughly 22 to 25.
- A roughly even split of threats and interaction.

**Signature cards.** Efficient, hard-to-answer creatures in the mold of **Tarmogoyf**, a two-mana creature whose size often outgrows its cost. Cheap removal such as **Lightning Bolt**, and disruption such as **Thoughtseize**, which looks at the opponent's hand and takes their best card. The common thread is that each card is powerful on its own, not dependent on synergy.

**Strengths.**

- Rarely has a terrible matchup, since it can shift between aggressor and defender.
- Strong individual cards mean fewer dead draws.

**Weaknesses.**

- Rarely has a dominant matchup either. It can be a step slower than aggro and a step weaker in the late game than control.
- Vulnerable to combo decks that ignore its fair interaction.
- Choosing the right role each game takes judgment.

**Playing against midrange.** Figure out which role you can play better than they can. A faster deck should go under midrange before its premium threats line up. A slower deck should trade carefully and aim to have the more powerful late game. Card-for-card trades usually favor midrange, so look for cards that generate two-for-ones.

## Combo: assemble the pieces, win on the spot

**Game plan.** Gather a specific combination of cards that wins the game outright, or creates such an overwhelming advantage that the game is effectively over. Everything else in the deck exists to find the pieces, protect them, or survive long enough to use them.

**How it wins.** Through an interaction between two or more cards. A long-standing example from Modern history is **Splinter Twin** with **Deceiver Exarch**. The enchantment lets the creature tap to create a hasty copy of itself, and each copy's enters ability untaps the original, which can then tap to create another copy. The loop produces as many hasty attackers as you like in a single turn. Other combo decks work differently. Some cast a long chain of cheap spells in one turn, others build infinite mana, and others cheat a huge creature onto the battlefield early. But the shape is the same: a few specific cards, together, end the game.

**Typical build.**

- Curve: low, packed with cheap card selection (cards that dig deeper into your deck) and the combo pieces themselves.
- Lands: varies widely, roughly 18 to 24 depending on how much mana the combo needs.
- Redundancy: multiple copies of each piece, or several cards that fill the same role.

**Signature cards.** Cheap card selection that helps find specific cards, like **Brainstorm** or **Ponder**. Tutors that search the library for a card. Protection that keeps the combo from being disrupted, such as a counterspell held up on the turn you go off.

**Strengths.**

- Can win from a position that looks hopeless, ignoring the board.
- Forces opponents to hold up interaction, which slows their own plan.

**Weaknesses.**

- Fragile. Targeted disruption, such as discard or a well-timed removal spell, can strand the remaining pieces.
- Inconsistent when it fails to draw the right cards.
- Often sacrifices interaction, so it can lose to a fast aggro start.

**Playing against combo.** Learn exactly what the pieces are and when the deck is about to go off. Save your interaction for the cards that matter: removing the creature in response to the enchantment is far more valuable than spending it on an incidental blocker. Pressure them early so they have to try the combo before they can protect it. Sideboard hate cards that stop the combo's specific mechanism; the [sideboarding guide](/blog/sideboarding-101) covers how to plan that.

## Tempo: cheap threats, cheaper answers

**Game plan.** Deploy a few efficient threats early, then use cheap interaction to stop the opponent from catching up until those threats finish the job. **Tempo** in Magic means the efficiency of your mana use: spending one mana to undo the opponent's three-mana play is a tempo gain.

**How it wins.** By building a small lead and protecting it. A tempo deck doesn't need a big board. One evasive creature backed by counters and bounce can win by itself.

**Typical build.**

- Curve: very low, with lots of 1 and 2 mana cards.
- Lands: roughly 18 to 21, often supported by cheap card selection that smooths draws.
- A mix of a few evasive or hard-to-block threats and plenty of cheap instant-speed interaction.

**Signature cards.** **Delver of Secrets**, a one-mana 1/1 that transforms into a 3/2 flier when you reveal an instant or sorcery from the top of your library at the start of your upkeep. **Brainstorm** and **Ponder**, which make a low land count work and help set up Delver. Cheap counters like **Spell Pierce** and **Mana Leak** that slow the opponent's key turns.

**Strengths.**

- Efficient on mana and plays at instant speed, so it's hard to read.
- Pressures control and slow decks while still interacting.

**Weaknesses.**

- Thin on raw power. If the opponent removes the first few threats, the deck can run out of steam.
- Cheap counterspells get worse as the game goes long and the opponent has more mana.
- Mistakes are costly, since the deck's edge is small and built on precision.

**Playing against tempo.** Make their cheap answers awkward. Cast two spells in one turn, play around soft counters like Mana Leak by waiting a turn to have extra mana, and remove their first threats aggressively. A tempo deck that loses its early creatures is usually far behind.

## Other archetypes you'll hear about

The five above are the main families, but you'll also see:

- **Ramp**: accelerates mana with cards like **Llanowar Elves** to cast big threats ahead of schedule. It sits between midrange and control.
- **Tokens**: makes many small creatures and pumps them all at once.
- **Aristocrats/Sacrifice**: turns creatures dying into damage or cards.
- **Reanimator**: puts a huge creature in the graveyard and returns it to the battlefield cheaply.

Most of these are sub-types or blends of the core five: a tokens deck can play like aggro or midrange, and Reanimator plays like combo.

## How the archetypes match up

There's a classic triangle that describes how the three "fair" archetypes tend to interact:

- **Aggro tends to beat control.** Control's answers are a little too slow and too expensive to keep up with a fast, wide start.
- **Control tends to beat midrange.** Midrange relies on individual threats, and control has answers for each one plus a stronger late game.
- **Midrange tends to beat aggro.** Midrange's bigger creatures and efficient removal blunt aggro's early pressure.

Combo usually does well against slow decks with little interaction and worse against fast aggro or heavy disruption. Tempo often pressures control and combo but can struggle against midrange's bigger threats.

Treat all of this as a tendency, not a law. Specific card choices, a well-built sideboard and play skill all swing matchups. A control deck with cheap sweepers and lifegain can beat aggro, and an aggro deck with a lot of burn can beat midrange. The triangle tells you which side is favored *on average* and what each side is trying to do.

## Who's the beatdown?

Every game of Magic has an aggressor and a defender, and figuring out which one you are is one of the most important decisions you make. The idea was popularized by a well-known strategy essay from the late 1990s, and the core of it is simple.

- **The beatdown** is the player whose deck is worse in a long game. They need to apply pressure and end the game before their opponent's stronger late game takes over.
- **The control player** in that matchup is the one with the better long game. Their job is to survive, trade and let card quality win.

The key insight: your role depends on the matchup, not just your deck. A midrange deck is the control player against aggro and the beatdown against dedicated control. Misassigning your role is a common way to lose: sitting back against control gives them time to draw answers, and racing aggro instead of blocking lets the faster deck win on its terms.

Ask yourself before the game, and again during it: *if this game goes long, who wins?* If the answer is "them", you're the beatdown. Be aggressive, take reasonable risks and use your removal to clear the way. If the answer is "me", play defensively, trade off threats and don't take risks you don't need.

| Archetype | Wins by | Typical curve | Lands (60 cards) | Example cards | Main weakness |

| Aggro | Early damage, turns 4-6 | Mostly 1-2 mana | 16-20 | Goblin Guide, Monastery Swiftspear, Lightning Bolt | Runs out of cards, board wipes |
| Control | Card advantage, late finishers | Flat, 2-4 mana answers plus a few big cards | 24-27 | Counterspell, Wrath of God, Fact or Fiction | Too slow against fast starts |

The land ranges are starting points, not rules. Your exact count depends on the curve and how much card selection you play. The [formats guide](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) explains how deck size and card pool change these choices across Standard, Modern, Commander and Limited.

## Choosing an archetype for yourself

Aggro is the easiest place to start: its plan is clear and it teaches the value of the mana curve quickly. Midrange teaches fundamentals, since it forces you to choose a role every game. Control, tempo and combo reward patience and practice.

The practical takeaway: before you build or play, name your archetype in one sentence ("I win with cheap creatures and burn by turn five"), then ask in every matchup whether you're the beatdown. Those two habits fix more games than any single card choice.
