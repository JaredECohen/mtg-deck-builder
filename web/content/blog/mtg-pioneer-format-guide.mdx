Pioneer is a non-rotating Constructed format for Magic: The Gathering. Its card pool starts with the Return to Ravnica expansion and includes every expansion and core set released into Standard since then. Once a card is legal in Pioneer, it stays legal unless it's banned, so the format grows with each new Standard set instead of losing old ones.

The [MTG formats overview](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) gives a quick summary of Pioneer alongside every other format. This guide goes deeper: what's legal, why the format was created, how it feels to play, and how to build or buy your first deck.

## What Pioneer is

Pioneer is a 60-card Constructed format. You build your deck ahead of time from cards you own, rather than drafting or opening packs at the event.

### Deck rules

- **Main deck:** at least 60 cards. There's no maximum, but almost every competitive deck plays exactly 60 to keep draws consistent.
- **Copies:** no more than four copies of any card with the same name, except basic lands, which are unlimited.
- **Sideboard:** up to 15 cards you can swap in between games of a match.
- **Banned list:** Pioneer has its own banned list, separate from other formats. It changes over time, so check the official list from Wizards of the Coast before building or buying a deck.

These are the same deck-building rules Standard and Modern use, which makes moving decks and cards between them simple.

### Which cards are legal

A card is legal in Pioneer if it has been printed in Return to Ravnica or any later expansion or core set that was released into Standard. A few points that trip people up:

- **Reprints count.** If an older card was reprinted in a Pioneer-legal Standard set, every copy of it is legal, including the original printing. Thoughtseize, for example, was first printed well before Return to Ravnica, but its reprint in Theros made it Pioneer-legal.
- **Supplemental sets don't count.** Cards that were printed only in products that never entered Standard, like Masters sets, Commander products or Modern Horizons, aren't legal in Pioneer.
- **Some iconic cards are missing.** Lightning Bolt, Counterspell, Path to Exile and Swords to Plowshares have never been printed in a Standard set from the Return to Ravnica era onward, so none of them are legal. That absence shapes the whole format.

When in doubt, any major card database lets you check a card's legality by format. That's more reliable than trying to memorize the set list.

## Why Pioneer exists

Wizards of the Coast introduced Pioneer in 2019 to fill a gap between Standard and Modern.

**Standard rotates.** Every so often, the oldest sets leave Standard. Players who invested in a Standard deck lose the ability to play it there, even if the cards are still good.

**Modern is big and old.** Modern's card pool reaches back to Eighth Edition and Mirrodin, includes cards from supplemental sets designed for it, and has a long history of powerful cards and deep, established decks. That depth is great for veterans, but it can feel like a lot to take on for a new player.

Pioneer offered a middle ground: a format where cards from recent Standard sets stay playable forever, with a starting point recent enough to leave out many of the oldest and most powerful cards. It also gave players a home for decks and cards that had just rotated out of Standard.

### Pioneer versus Standard

Pioneer plays a lot like [Standard](/blog/mtg-standard-format-guide) with more options. Because the pool is larger, decks tend to be more refined, synergies go deeper and the best cards from many years of Standard are all available at once. The format is also more stable, because nothing leaves it except through bans.

The trade-off is power. A Pioneer deck has access to the strongest cards from every Pioneer-legal set, so a pile of Standard cards may not compete without upgrades.

### Pioneer versus Modern

Modern is faster and more powerful. Its card pool includes efficient cards Pioneer lacks, like Lightning Bolt and the cheap interaction mentioned above, plus cards from sets designed specifically for Modern. Games in Modern are often decided earlier.

Pioneer games tend to last a bit longer and reward board interaction and careful play. Without one-mana answers like Lightning Bolt and Path to Exile, cheap creatures are harder to remove efficiently, and decks lean more on two-mana interaction and value.

## What Pioneer feels like to play

Pioneer supports every major archetype, including aggro, midrange, control and combo. The specific top decks change with each new set and each banned-list update, but a few traits are fairly stable.

- **The mana is good but not perfect.** Pioneer has strong dual lands, including the shocklands like Steam Vents and Overgrown Tomb from the Ravnica sets. Mana bases are solid, but three-color decks still face real costs.
- **Interaction is efficient.** Cards like Fatal Push and Thoughtseize give black decks cheap answers. Blue decks have counterspells and card selection like Opt.
- **Midrange and control are viable.** Without as many cheap free spells as older formats, there's more room for decks that trade resources. Our guides to [midrange](/blog/mtg-midrange-deck-guide) and [control](/blog/mtg-control-deck-guide) describe strategies you'll see often.
- **New sets matter.** Every new Standard set adds cards to Pioneer, so the format keeps shifting. That's good for brewers, who regularly find new interactions.

## How to get started in Pioneer

### 1. Pick a strategy you enjoy

Start with the play style you like rather than whatever looks strongest this week. A deck you understand will win more than a strong deck you don't. If you're unsure, look at the [deck archetypes overview](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) and pick the description that sounds most fun.

### 2. Start with a budget version

Many Pioneer decks have a cheaper version that keeps the core plan and swaps the most expensive cards for close substitutes. Mono-colored aggro decks are usually the most affordable entry point, because they need fewer expensive dual lands. Our guide to [budget MTG decks under $50](/blog/budget-mtg-decks-under-50) has tips for keeping costs down without gutting the deck.

### 3. Use cards you already own

If you've played Standard, check your collection. Cards you thought were retired after rotation may still be legal and useful in Pioneer.

### 4. Build a sideboard

Pioneer events are typically best-of-three matches, so a sideboard matters. Even 15 cards that cover your worst matchups can swing results. [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) explains how to build one.

### 5. Find where to play

Pioneer is supported in paper at many local game stores, and on Magic Online. Ask your local store whether it runs Pioneer events, and if you play digitally, check which formats your platform supports.

### 6. Practice before the event

Play your deck enough to know its key decisions: what hands to keep, which threats to save removal for, and how to sideboard against the decks you expect.

### Mistakes to avoid at your first event

- **Copying a list you can't pilot.** A complex combo deck you've played twice will lose to a simple deck you know well.
- **Skipping the legality check.** Confirm every card, including older printings, against the current banned list and format legality before you register.
- **Ignoring your sideboard plan.** Decide in advance what comes in and out for the matchups you expect, so you're not guessing between games.
- **Playing too slowly.** Pioneer games can run long. Make routine decisions quickly to avoid unfinished matches.

## The takeaway

Pioneer is the non-rotating format built from Return to Ravnica forward. It's bigger and more stable than Standard, less powerful and less expensive to approach than Modern, and it keeps growing with every new Standard set. Pick a strategy you like, start with a budget version, check your old Standard cards and build a sideboard, and you'll be ready for your first Pioneer event.
