In best-of-one, a match is a single game: whoever wins that game wins the match. In best-of-three, players keep playing until one of them has won two games, and between games each player can swap cards with their sideboard. That one difference changes how decks are built, how much luck matters, and which strategies are strongest.

This guide compares the two structures, explains what happens between games in best-of-three, and covers how to build for each. For the details of building and using a sideboard, [Sideboarding 101](/blog/sideboarding-101) is the deep dive. Here, the focus is on the match structure itself.

## How each match structure works

### Best-of-one

Best-of-one is the simplest structure. You and your opponent play one game, and the result is the match result. There's no second game, so there's nothing to adjust between games, and decks are played exactly as they're built.

Best-of-one is fast. A single game is usually much shorter than a full three-game match, which makes it appealing for casual play, quick online queues and events with lots of rounds.

### Best-of-three

In best-of-three, the first player to win two games wins the match. A match can end 2-0 or go to a third game at 1-1.

Here's how a typical best-of-three match flows:

1. **Game one.** Both players use their main deck as built. Who plays first is decided randomly, such as by a die roll or coin flip.
2. **Sideboarding.** After game one, both players can swap cards between their main deck and sideboard.
3. **Game two.** Under standard tournament rules, the player who lost the previous game chooses whether to play first or draw first.
4. **Game three, if needed.** Players sideboard again, and the loser of game two chooses play or draw.

Tournament matches also have a time limit. If time runs out before a player wins two games, the event's rules decide what happens, which can include a drawn match. Check the rules of the specific event if you're playing in one.

## Sideboarding: the big difference

A **sideboard** is a set of extra cards you can bring in between games. In Constructed formats, a sideboard is up to 15 cards. In Limited, such as Sealed or Draft, every card in your pool that isn't in your deck works as your sideboard.

Sideboarding only exists in best-of-three, and it changes the shape of the match:

- **Game one is a blind matchup.** Neither player knows exactly what the other is playing, so main decks are built for the field in general.
- **Games two and three are tailored.** Once you've seen your opponent's deck, you can bring in cards that answer it directly, like graveyard hate against a graveyard deck or extra removal against a fast creature deck.
- **Both players adapt.** Your opponent sideboards too, so the games after the first are a contest of who adjusted better.

That's why best-of-three is generally considered the more skill-testing format. You have more decisions to make, and a single unlucky draw is less likely to decide the whole match.

## Deckbuilding for best-of-one

Without a sideboard, every card you'd want against a specific opponent has to fit in the main deck or not be there at all. Best-of-one decks usually look different as a result.

### Main-deck flexible answers

In best-of-one, you want answers that are rarely dead. A removal spell that can hit creatures, artifacts or enchantments is better than one that only hits a narrow target, because you don't know what you'll face. Very narrow cards, like graveyard hate or cards that only matter against one strategy, are harder to justify.

### Proactive decks get stronger

Decks that focus on their own plan, like fast aggro, are especially good in best-of-one. They don't need to answer anything specific, and opponents can't bring in dedicated hate against them for a second game. A deck that wins quickly before the opponent's answers line up can take a lot of matches.

### Focused strategies avoid the counterpunch

Strategies that are strong but easy to beat with the right sideboard cards, such as some combo or graveyard decks, are also attractive in best-of-one. In best-of-three, opponents bring in targeted hate after game one. In best-of-one, there's no second game, so that hate rarely appears unless the opponent was already playing it.

### Consistency matters more

A single game means a mana problem or a bad opening hand can decide the match. Best-of-one players often favor smooth mana bases and fewer colors, and make careful decisions about which hands to keep. The [mulligan guide](/blog/mtg-mulligan-guide) covers how to judge an opening hand.

## Deckbuilding for best-of-three

Best-of-three gives you a second line of defense, which lets your main deck stay focused.

### Keep the main deck streamlined

Because you can bring in narrow answers after game one, your main deck can be built to do its own thing well. Cards that are only good against a few decks can wait in the sideboard.

### Plan for what the opponent will bring in

If your deck has an obvious weakness, expect your opponent to target it after game one. An artifact deck should plan for artifact removal, and a graveyard deck should plan for graveyard hate. Your sideboard can include cards to fight through that, such as threats that dodge the hate or ways to protect your key cards.

### Have a plan for play and draw

Since the loser of each game chooses who goes first in the next, you'll often switch between playing first and drawing first. Some players sideboard slightly differently on the play and on the draw, such as bringing in cheaper interaction when on the draw against a fast deck.

### Limited decks still sideboard

In Sealed and Draft, best-of-three lets you react to your opponent's deck with the cards from your pool. That can be as small as swapping in a creature with reach against flyers, or as big as switching colors entirely. The [guide to building a Sealed deck](/blog/sealed-deck-building-guide) covers how to keep a plan B ready.

## Where each structure is played

### MTG Arena

MTG Arena offers both best-of-one and best-of-three queues for Constructed formats and for Limited events. Best-of-one is popular there because games are quick and easy to fit into a short session. Players who want the full sideboarding experience can choose best-of-three queues. Because the structure differs, players often build separate versions of a deck for each.

### In-person tournaments

Paper tournaments, from store events to larger competitive events, are traditionally run as best-of-three. Prereleases and other Limited events at local stores are usually best-of-three as well. Event organizers set the structure, so check the event details if you're unsure.

### Casual play and Commander

Casual games between friends usually don't use a formal structure. You play as many games as you want. Commander is typically played as single multiplayer games rather than matches, and there's no traditional sideboarding between games. For an overview of the formats people play, see [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).

## Best-of-one vs best-of-three at a glance

| | Best-of-one | Best-of-three |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Games per match | 1 | Up to 3 |
| Sideboarding | None between games | After games one and two |
| Who plays first | Random | Random for game one, then the previous loser chooses |
| Time | Short | Longer |
| Role of luck | Larger | Smaller |
| Favored strategies | Proactive, consistent, hard-to-hate decks | Decks with good sideboard plans and flexible matchups |

## The takeaway

Best-of-one is a single game with no sideboarding, which rewards consistent, proactive decks and flexible main-deck answers. Best-of-three lets both players adjust after game one, which reduces the impact of luck and rewards good sideboard planning. Build for the structure you're actually playing, and if you play both, keep separate versions of your deck.
