Magic 2012, better known as M12, is the Magic: The Gathering core set released in July 2011. It was the third of the yearly "Magic 20XX" core sets, it turned hexproof into an official keyword, brought back bloodthirst for a Vampire-flavored aggro theme, and reprinted all five Titans alongside five planeswalkers. Its cards fed Standard for more than a year and several, from Grim Lavamancer to Phantasmal Image, still turn up in Modern and Commander.

## What the set was for

From Magic 2010 onward, core sets stopped being pure reprint collections. Each one mixed classic reprints with new cards and a light flavor theme, and each one was meant to be both a starting point for new players and a real Standard set. M12 followed that model closely. It mixed new cards with reprints, it was sold in regular boosters, Intro Packs, Event Decks and a fat pack, and it came with ten 30-card sample decks for teaching the game.

There's no story to follow in Magic 2012. The set's "characters" are its five mythic planeswalkers: Gideon Jura and Sorin Markov returned as reprints, while Jace, Memory Adept, Chandra, the Firebrand and Garruk, Primal Hunter were brand new. It was the first time a core set got three new planeswalker cards. Each planeswalker came with a rare and a common that referenced them by name, such as Chandra's Phoenix and Chandra's Outrage, or Garruk's Horde and Garruk's Companion.

### The rules change: "dies"

M12's lasting rules contribution was a new shortcut word. Before M12, cards said a creature "is put into a graveyard from the battlefield." From M12 on, they simply say it "dies." The change is purely about wording: nothing about how the game works changed, and older cards were updated in the Oracle text to use the new term. If you've ever read a card like Archon of Justice ("When this creature dies, exile target permanent"), you're reading M12's legacy. Our guide to [reading a Magic card](/blog/how-to-read-a-magic-card) covers how to parse templating like this.

## Mechanics

### Hexproof

A permanent with hexproof can't be the target of spells or abilities your opponents control, but you can still target it yourself. That's the key difference from shroud, which stops everyone, including you. Players had informally called this "troll shroud" for years after Troll Ascetic; M12 gave it a name and printed it on a handful of creatures.

Examples in the set include Gladecover Scout (a one-mana 1/1), Sacred Wolf (a three-mana 3/1), Aven Fleetwing (a flying 2/2 for four) and Dungrove Elder, a Treefolk whose power and toughness equal the number of Forests you control. Hexproof creatures pair naturally with Auras, since an opponent can't answer an Aura-loaded hexproof creature with a targeted removal spell. M12 leaned into that with cards like Trollhide, Divine Favor and Spirit Mantle at low rarities and Angelic Destiny at mythic. Hexproof is now an evergreen keyword, covered in our [keywords glossary](/blog/mtg-keywords-glossary).

### Bloodthirst

Bloodthirst first appeared in Guildpact. A creature with bloodthirst N enters the battlefield with N +1/+1 counters if an opponent was dealt damage earlier in the turn. That makes the order of your turn matter: attack first, or ping your opponent, then cast the creature after combat to get the bonus.

In M12 bloodthirst lived mostly in black and red. The commons were Bloodrage Vampire, Duskhunter Bat, Blood Ogre, Gorehorn Minotaurs and Lurking Crocodile; the uncommons included Stormblood Berserker and Vampire Outcasts; and the mythics were Bloodlord of Vaasgoth and Furyborn Hellkite. Cheap enablers like Taste of Blood and evasive creatures like Tormented Soul (which can't block and can't be blocked) made it easy to switch bloodthirst on.

### Planeswalkers and the Titans

Five mythic planeswalkers and five mythic Titans were the set's power ceiling. The Titans were reprints from Magic 2011: Sun Titan, Frost Titan, Grave Titan, Inferno Titan and Primeval Titan, each a 6/6 for six with a big effect when it enters or attacks. Having all five available again for another Standard year shaped the format's top end.

## Limited

M12 was a classic core set draft format: no multicolored cards, no complicated set mechanic, just creatures, removal and a few themes layered on top. Because there are no gold cards, archetypes came from combining two colors' themes rather than from signpost uncommons.

The themes you could draft toward were:

- **Bloodthirst aggro (black-red especially).** Cheap creatures and pingers turned on the bloodthirst creatures, and Gorehorn Minotaurs or Vampire Outcasts arriving as big bodies on turn four or five could end games fast.
- **Hexproof and Auras (green or white).** Gladecover Scout, Sacred Wolf or Aven Fleetwing wearing Trollhide or Divine Favor was hard for an opponent to remove. It was a real plan, but a fragile one if you drew Auras without creatures.
- **White fliers and tempo.** Stormfront Pegasus, Peregrine Griffin and Gideon's Lawkeeper gave white a way to win in the air while tapping down blockers.
- **Blue control.** Frost Breath, counterspells and fliers like Chasm Drake supported slower decks.

Removal at common was the clearest strength of the format: Doom Blade, Sorin's Thirst, Chandra's Outrage, Incinerate, Shock, Pacifism and Plummet all appeared at common. If you're new to evaluating cards like these, our guide to [the mana curve](/blog/mana-curve-explained) covers how to build a deck that uses them on time. The format's speed was moderate: bloodthirst decks could punish slow starts, but there was enough cheap removal and reach for midrange decks to keep up.

## The meta: how it played in Constructed

### Standard (July 2011 to October 2012)

M12 joined a Standard that had just been shaken up. On July 1, 2011, Jace, the Mind Sculptor and Stoneforge Mystic were banned to end the reign of Caw-Blade. M12 landed two weeks later and supplied pieces for most of the decks that followed.

Through the rest of 2011 and into 2012, its cards showed up across the format:

- **Wolf Run Ramp** used Primeval Titan, Solemn Simulacrum and often Garruk, Primal Hunter or Inferno Titan to ramp into Kessig Wolf Run from Innistrad.
- **Delver and other blue tempo decks** leaned on M12's Ponder and Mana Leak after Innistrad brought Delver of Secrets in the fall.
- **Control decks** such as Solar Flare and UB Control played Sun Titan, Grave Titan, Day of Judgment and Mana Leak.
- **Red aggro** used Stormblood Berserker, Chandra's Phoenix, Shock and Incinerate.
- **Illusions** decks built around Phantasmal Image and Lord of the Unreal were a smaller but real archetype.

No card from M12 was banned in Standard. The set rotated out in October 2012 when Return to Ravnica arrived, together with the Scars of Mirrodin block. For how rotation works in general, see our [Standard rotation explainer](/blog/mtg-rotation-explained).

### Modern, Legacy, Vintage and Commander (as of October 2026)

M12 is one of the sets that defined early Modern, since Modern begins with Eighth Edition and M12 was among the newest sets when the format launched later in 2011. Its biggest long-term contributions:

- **Grim Lavamancer** gives red decks in Modern and Legacy a repeatable damage source that feeds on spent burn spells and fetchlands.
- **Primeval Titan** remains the payoff in Modern Amulet Titan. It's banned in Commander.
- **Phantasmal Image** has filled the copy-a-creature slot in Modern creature combo decks.
- **Ponder** is banned in Modern but legal in Legacy, Vintage and Pauper, where it's a common staple.
- **Mana Leak** and **Doom Blade** were widely played commons in Modern's early years.

In Commander, Grand Abolisher, Solemn Simulacrum, Swiftfoot Boots, Rites of Flourishing and Sundial of the Infinite are long-time favorites. The set has no Reserved List cards. Our [Modern format guide](/blog/mtg-modern-format-guide) covers where these cards fit in today's format.

## Cards from Magic 2012 worth owning

- **Grim Lavamancer**: a one-drop that keeps dealing damage in Modern and Legacy red decks.
- **Primeval Titan**: the engine of Amulet Titan in Modern.
- **Phantasmal Image**: a two-mana Clone that slots into creature-based combo and value decks.
- **Grand Abolisher**: shuts down interaction on your turn in Commander.
- **Solemn Simulacrum**: one of the most-played colorless creatures in Commander.
- **Swiftfoot Boots**: cheap protection and haste for Commander's most important creatures.
- **Ponder**: a Legacy, Vintage and Pauper cantrip staple.

M12 is a good example of a core set that mattered. It looked simple, but it named hexproof, introduced "dies," and handed Modern a handful of cards that stuck around for over a decade.
