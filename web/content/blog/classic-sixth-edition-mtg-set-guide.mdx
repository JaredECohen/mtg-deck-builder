Classic Sixth Edition, usually just called Sixth Edition or 6th, is the Magic: The Gathering core set released in April 1999. Its cards were mostly familiar reprints, but it arrived with the Sixth Edition rules, the biggest rewrite of how the game works until Magic 2010: it introduced the stack as we know it, turned interrupts into instants and modernized combat. It also carried a strong group of staples, from Counterspell and Wrath of God to Vampiric Tutor, Birds of Paradise and City of Brass.

## What the set was for

By 1999, Magic had been running for six years on rules that had grown by accretion. There were "interrupts" that worked differently from instants, a separate damage prevention step, "batches" of effects that resolved in odd ways, and creature spells that were still called "summon" spells. The game made sense to veterans but confused almost everyone else. Sixth Edition's job was to clean that up and give the game a foundation it could grow on, and a core set was the natural place to launch it.

The set itself also took a few small steps toward modern Magic. It was the first core set to have an expansion symbol, a Roman numeral VI, and the first with collector numbers. Its cards were still white-bordered, the core set look that lasted until Tenth Edition. Packaging used the "Classic" name, which is why Scryfall and many collectors call it Classic Sixth Edition.

### The Sixth Edition rules

The rules change was the headline. Among the most important changes:

- **The stack.** Spells and abilities now went onto a single "stack" and resolved one at a time, last in, first out. Each player could respond to anything. This replaced the older system of batches and is still how the game works.
- **Interrupts became instants.** Counterspells and other interrupts were turned into ordinary instants that use the stack.
- **No more damage prevention step.** Damage prevention moved to the stack like everything else.
- **Combat damage used the stack.** After damage was assigned, it went on the stack, and players could respond before it was dealt. This "damage on the stack" rule enabled tricks like sacrificing a creature after its damage was locked in. It lasted until Magic 2010 removed it.
- **Summon became creature.** "Summon Elf" became "Creature — Elf," the type line format still used today.
- **Losing at zero life became immediate.** A player at 0 or less life loses as soon as the game checks, rather than at the end of a phase.
- **Banding was retired from the core set.**

Many longtime players disliked the changes at first, but they made the rules much easier to teach and to reason about, and every later rules update builds on them. Our [how to play Magic](/blog/how-to-play-magic-the-gathering) guide explains the stack in today's terms.

## Mechanics

Sixth Edition had no new keyword mechanics. Its cards used the classic abilities of the time: flying, first strike, trample, regeneration, and the "landwalk" abilities like islandwalk and swampwalk. Some notable themes in the card pool:

- **Tutors.** A cycle of one-mana instant tutors: Vampiric Tutor (any card, for 2 life), Mystical Tutor (instant or sorcery), Enlightened Tutor (artifact or enchantment) and Worldly Tutor (creature). Each puts the card on top of your library.
- **Lords.** Goblin King, Lord of Atlantis and Zombie Master give their creature types bonuses and landwalk.
- **Mass effects.** Wrath of God, Armageddon, Earthquake, Hurricane, Pestilence, Inferno and Jokulhaups gave control decks plenty of ways to reset the board.
- **Painlands and City of Brass.** Five painlands (like Adarkar Wastes) plus City of Brass gave multicolor decks fixing.

## Limited

Like most core sets of its time, Sixth Edition wasn't designed with draft archetypes in mind, and it wasn't one of the formats Pro Tour Limited events centered on. When it was drafted or played as Sealed, it worked like other early core sets: simple creatures, a little removal (Pacifism, Terror, Shock, Lightning Blast and Pyrotechnics at common), and games decided by whoever had more answers and better fliers.

Prodigal Sorcerer, the original "Tim," was a strong common because it pinged x/1 creatures endlessly. Sabretooth Tiger and Feral Shadow were the kind of solid bodies that filled out decks. If you replay it today as a nostalgia draft, a sensible two-color deck built on removal and evasive creatures is the right approach. Our guide to [evaluating cards for Limited](/blog/how-to-evaluate-cards-limited) covers the fundamentals.

## The meta: how it played in Constructed

### Standard, 1999-2001

Sixth Edition joined a Standard format shaped by Tempest and Urza's blocks and later Mercadian Masques and Invasion. Its reprints were the backbone of many of the era's decks:

- **Counterspell** was the core of blue "Draw-Go" control, which countered everything and won with a few threats or creature lands.
- **Wrath of God** and **Armageddon** were the tools of white control and white aggro. By late 2000, Kai Budde won Pro Tour Chicago with a Rebels deck that could bring in Wrath effects and Armageddon from the sideboard.
- **Earthquake** was a common threat in red decks, and players built around surviving it.
- **Birds of Paradise** and **Llanowar Elves** accelerated green decks, and **River Boa** gave them a sturdy two-drop.
- **Pestilence** gave black decks a card that doubled as repeatable removal and a win condition.

Sixth Edition left Standard when Seventh Edition replaced it in April 2001.

### Legacy and Vintage

Many of Sixth Edition's cards are Legacy and Vintage staples, though several are now restricted or banned:

- **Vampiric Tutor** and **Mystical Tutor** are restricted in Vintage and banned in Legacy.
- **Flash** is restricted in Vintage and banned in Legacy and Commander, after years as the engine of a turn-one Protean Hulk combo.
- **Goblin Recruiter** is banned in Legacy.
- **Doomsday** is the namesake of a Legacy combo deck.
- **Counterspell**, **Enlightened Tutor** and **Worldly Tutor** remain legal in Legacy.

Crusade and Pradesh Gypsies, both reprinted here, are banned in every format. Wizards removed them in 2020, along with other cards with racist names or imagery.

### Pauper, Commander and Modern

In Pauper, Counterspell, Pestilence and River Boa are all legal thanks to common printings. In Commander, Vampiric Tutor, Mystical Tutor, Enlightened Tutor, Worldly Tutor and Ashnod's Altar are staples. Sixth Edition itself predates Modern, but cards later reprinted in Modern-legal sets, like Wrath of God, Birds of Paradise, Llanowar Elves and City of Brass, can be played there. Our [formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) guide describes how each format's card pool works.

## Cards from Classic Sixth Edition worth owning

- **Counterspell**: the original two-mana hard counter, still played in Legacy and Pauper.
- **Vampiric Tutor**: a Commander staple that finds anything.
- **Wrath of God**: the classic board wipe.
- **Birds of Paradise**: the best-known mana creature.
- **Enlightened Tutor** and **Worldly Tutor**: flexible tutors for Legacy and Commander.
- **City of Brass**: five-color fixing on a land.

## The takeaway

Sixth Edition looks like an ordinary white-bordered core set, but every game of Magic you play today runs on the rules it introduced. Its card pool is also a good snapshot of what "classic Magic" felt like, with counterspells, board wipes and land destruction. Legacy is the best place to play those cards now.
