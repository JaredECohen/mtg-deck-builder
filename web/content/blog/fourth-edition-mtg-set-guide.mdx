Fourth Edition is the Magic: The Gathering core set released in April 1995, a white-bordered collection of 378 cards that replaced Revised Edition as the foundation of the game. It removed the original dual lands and powerful cards like Demonic Tutor and Regrowth, added favorites from Arabian Nights, Antiquities, Legends and The Dark, and was printed in enormous numbers. For about two years it was the base set of Type II, the format now called Standard.

## The setting and flavor

Fourth Edition has no storyline. Like other early core sets, it was a collection of cards from Magic's first years, held together by classic fantasy flavor: Serra Angel, Shivan Dragon, Sengir Vampire, Mahamoti Djinn and Lord of the Pit. The only story tie-in was a comic from Armada that celebrated Serra Angel's reprint.

What gives the set its character is where its new cards came from. Fourth Edition pulled in cards from the early expansions, so a booster could contain a Mishra's Factory from Antiquities, a Sylvan Library from Legends, a Sindbad from Arabian Nights or an Uncle Istvan from The Dark, all with white borders.

## What the set was for

### Replacing Revised

Fourth Edition's job was to refresh the core of the game. Wizards removed 50 cards from Revised and added 122 from Arabian Nights, Antiquities, Legends and The Dark. The cuts were significant:

- **The original dual lands**, such as Bayou, Savannah, Scrubland and Plateau, left the core set and never returned to it.
- **Powerful spells** like Demonic Tutor, Regrowth, Fork, Fastbond, Clone, Copy Artifact, Braingeyser and Kird Ape were cut.
- **Black's demonic contract cards**, including Contract from Below, Darkpact and Demonic Attorney, were removed.

The additions included cards that went on to matter for decades: Strip Mine, Mishra's Factory, Sylvan Library, Land Tax, Ball Lightning, Triskelion and Spirit Link. Some ante cards came along too, such as Bronze Tablet and Rebirth, which are banned in every sanctioned format today.

### Clearer rules

Magic's rules were still evolving in 1995, and many situations were settled by rulings rather than rulebook text. Fourth Edition's Pocket Players' Guide gathered the most important rulings into one place. It clarified how "fast effects" (instants and interrupts) and batches of effects resolved, and spelled out rules for phases, activation costs, targets and damage prevention. Those rules were later replaced by the Sixth Edition overhaul in 1999, which introduced the stack, but Fourth Edition was a big step toward a consistent game.

### A new look and production changes

The set restored the beveled card frame that Revised had lost, used more saturated colors, and introduced a redesigned white mana symbol and a new tap symbol. It was the first set with basic lands printed on their own sheet, the first with equal numbers of commons, uncommons and rares, and the first with foil booster wrappers and artwork on them. It was also printed in huge quantities, estimated at around 500 million cards. A separate "Alternate Fourth Edition" printing was made by another manufacturer but never officially released. ## Limited

Fourth Edition predates organized booster draft as players know it today. In 1995, most people played Magic with their own collections, starter decks or sealed product, and there was no Pro Tour Limited circuit yet. So there are no draft archetypes to describe honestly.

What can be said is that, like any core set of that era, Fourth Edition sealed games were decided by fliers, removal and big creatures. Cards like Lightning Bolt, Fireball, Terror, Swords to Plowshares, Serra Angel and Sengir Vampire were the cards everyone hoped to open. If you're new to the game, our [deck archetypes guide](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) explains how the aggro, control and midrange plans of that era still apply.

## The meta: how it played in Constructed

### Type II

Type II, the format that became Standard, began in January 1995 with Revised as its base set. Later that year the rules were updated to use the most recent core set, Fourth Edition, along with the recent expansions and Chronicles. Type II at the time had a restricted list, and several Fourth Edition cards ended up on it or banned outright:

- **Balance** was restricted in Type II in April 1995.
- **Channel** was banned in November 1995.
- **Mind Twist** was banned and **Black Vise** restricted in February 1996.
- **Strip Mine** was restricted in October 1996.
- In January 1997, every restricted card in Type II was moved to the banned list.

The decks of 1996 used Fourth Edition cards alongside Ice Age and the other recent sets. Necropotence decks, built on Ice Age's Necropotence plus fast mana like Fourth Edition's Dark Ritual, dominated that summer, a stretch remembered as "Black Summer". By the 1996 World Championship, Necro was the most-played deck in the room. Green-white Erhnamgeddon decks played mana creatures, Erhnam Djinn and Fourth Edition's Armageddon, Swords to Plowshares, Land Tax, Sylvan Library and Balance. Turbo Stasis decks locked players out with Stasis. The 1996 title went to Tom Chanpheng with a white weenie deck, beating Mark Justice's Necropotence deck in the final. Fifth Edition replaced Fourth Edition in 1997.

### Eternal formats

Fourth Edition isn't legal in Modern, but many of its cards, like Lightning Bolt, Counterspell, Birds of Paradise and Wrath of God, are Modern-legal through later reprints. As of October 2026, Scryfall lists Balance, Channel, Mind Twist, Mana Vault and Strip Mine as banned in Legacy, and Balance, Channel, Mana Vault and Strip Mine as restricted in Vintage. Balance and Channel are also banned in Commander.

Swords to Plowshares, Sylvan Library, Dark Ritual and Counterspell remain Legacy staples, and Mishra's Factory still appears in Legacy artifact and white decks. In Commander, Sylvan Library, Land Tax and Mana Vault are well-known staples. For an overview of these formats, see [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained) and our [Legacy format guide](/blog/mtg-legacy-format-guide).

Two Fourth Edition cards, **Crusade** and **Pradesh Gypsies**, are banned in every sanctioned format: Wizards removed them in 2020 as part of a group of cards with racist imagery or names.

## Cards from Fourth Edition worth owning

- **Swords to Plowshares**: the most efficient white removal spell ever printed.
- **Lightning Bolt**: the benchmark for red burn.
- **Sylvan Library**: card selection that's strong in Legacy and Commander.
- **Counterspell**: the standard two-mana counter.
- **Mishra's Factory**: a land that attacks and blocks.
- **Land Tax**: a white card-advantage engine for Commander.
- **Dark Ritual**: fast mana for Legacy combo and black Commander decks.

Fourth Edition is less glamorous than the black-bordered sets before it, but it shows how Magic matured: cleaner rules, a curated core and a sense of what was too strong to keep reprinting.
