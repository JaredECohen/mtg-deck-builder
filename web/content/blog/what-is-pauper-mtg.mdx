Pauper is a Constructed Magic: The Gathering format where every card in your deck must have been printed at common rarity. You build a 60-card deck with up to four copies of each card and a 15-card sideboard, just like most other Constructed formats. The only difference is the rarity rule, and that one rule makes Pauper one of the cheapest competitive formats in the game.

This guide covers how Pauper's rules work, how card legality is decided, what the main kinds of decks look like, and how the format differs from casual "commons only" games at the kitchen table. For a wider view of where Pauper sits among other formats, see [MTG formats explained](/blog/mtg-formats-explained).

## Pauper's deck-building rules

If you've built a deck for any 60-card format, you already know most of Pauper:

| Rule | Pauper |
| --- | --- |
| Minimum main deck size | 60 cards, no maximum |
| Copies of a card | Up to 4, except basic lands (any number) |
| Sideboard | Up to 15 cards |
| Starting life | 20 |
| Card rarity | Every card must have been printed at common |
| Banned cards | Pauper has its own banned list |

The sideboard follows the usual rules for best-of-three matches: between games, you can swap cards between your main deck and sideboard to prepare for your opponent's deck.

Pauper also keeps its own banned list, separate from other formats. A card being common doesn't guarantee it's legal, so check the official list before you build. It changes over time, which is why this guide doesn't name specific cards on it.

## How card legality works

The core idea is simple: a card is legal in Pauper if it has been printed at common rarity. What matters is the card's history, not the copy in your hand.

That has a useful consequence. Many cards have been reprinted at different rarities over the years. A card might first appear as an uncommon or rare, then later show up as a common in another set, or the other way around. If a qualifying printing exists at common, the card counts as a common for Pauper, and every copy of it is legal, including your copy with a different rarity symbol.

The details of which printings count, such as which products and digital releases qualify, are set by the format's official rules and have changed over the years. If you're unsure about a specific card, look it up in an official legality source or a card database that lists format legality. Don't rely on the rarity symbol on your card alone.

## Why Pauper is cheap

Commons are printed in large numbers. Every booster pack contains many more commons than rares, so supply is high and most commons cost very little. A full Pauper deck usually costs a fraction of a competitive deck in a format built around rares and mythics.

A few commons do become pricey, usually old cards that saw a small print run or cards that are in high demand. Even so, the rarity rule caps the cost of the format in a way no other rule can. For more ways to keep any deck cheap, see [budget MTG decks under $50](/blog/budget-mtg-decks-under-50).

## Why Pauper is still deep

Cheap doesn't mean simple. Magic has a huge pool of commons spanning its entire history, and many of them are strong, efficient cards. Lightning Bolt, Counterspell and Llanowar Elves have all been printed at common, and they're among the most famous cards in the game.

Because powerful rares are absent, the cards that would usually dominate are missing, and smaller interactions matter more. Combat is often decided by a single trick. Card advantage comes from creatures that draw a card when they enter, like Mulldrifter, rather than from expensive planeswalkers. Removal is plentiful but often conditional, so choosing the right answer for each threat is a real skill.

The result is a format where tight play and good deck-building are rewarded, and where a well-tuned deck of cheap cards can be every bit as hard to pilot as a deck in a more expensive format.

## Typical Pauper archetypes

Pauper decks fall into the same broad families as decks in other formats. If you're new to these labels, [MTG deck archetypes explained](/blog/mtg-deck-archetypes-explained) covers them in depth. The examples below are long-standing commons that illustrate each style. They aren't a list of "the best decks," and you should confirm current legality before you build around any of them.

### Aggro

Aggro decks play cheap creatures and try to reduce the opponent's life to 0 before the game drags on. In Pauper, aggro often pairs efficient one- and two-mana creatures with burn spells like Lightning Bolt and Rift Bolt to finish the job. White-based aggro can use creatures like Kor Skyfisher, which comes with flying and can return a permanent to your hand for extra value.

Aggro is a good starting point for new Pauper players. The decks are cheap, straightforward to play, and they punish slow opponents.

### Control

Control decks answer everything the opponent does, then win late with a few powerful cards. Pauper control leans on counterspells like Counterspell, removal like Doom Blade and Journey to Nowhere, and card-draw creatures like Mulldrifter to stay ahead on cards. Some control decks use sweeper effects such as Pestilence to clear several small creatures at once, which is especially strong in a format full of small creatures.

### Tempo

Tempo decks play a cheap threat early, then use cheap interaction to keep the opponent off balance while that threat attacks. A classic tempo creature is Delver of Secrets, a one-mana creature that can transform into a bigger flier. Tempo decks back it up with counterspells and spells that return an opposing creature to its owner's hand, buying time while their own small creatures deal damage.

Tempo is one of the hardest styles to play well, because every turn is a choice between developing your board and holding up interaction.

### Ramp-style decks

Ramp decks spend their early turns gaining extra mana, then cast big spells sooner than they normally could. In Pauper this can look like green decks using Llanowar Elves, Rampant Growth or Cultivate to accelerate into large creatures or expensive card-draw effects. Other ramp-style decks use special lands or artifacts that produce extra mana. The goal is the same: reach a game state the opponent's cheap cards can't keep up with.

## Pauper vs kitchen-table "commons only"

Plenty of playgroups run casual commons-only games, and some people call those Pauper too. They're related ideas, but they aren't the same format.

| | Pauper (the official format) | Kitchen-table commons only |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What counts as common | A card printed at common in a qualifying printing | Usually whatever your group agrees, often the rarity symbol on your own copy |
| Banned list | Yes, its own official list | Whatever your group decides |
| Deck size and copies | 60-card minimum, up to 4 copies, 15-card sideboard | Varies by group |
| Where it's played | Organized play, online, and at local stores | Wherever your friends play |

The most common source of confusion is the rarity rule. At a kitchen table, a group might say "only cards with a common symbol." Under official Pauper rules, a card whose copy shows a rare or uncommon symbol can still be legal if it was printed at common somewhere else. If you're building for a casual group, agree on which rule you're using before anyone shuffles up.

## Pauper Commander

There's also a separate commons-focused variant of Commander, often called Pauper Commander or PDH. It takes the multiplayer, singleton structure of [Commander](/blog/what-is-commander-edh) and applies a rarity restriction, with its own rules about which card can lead the deck. It has its own community and its own rules documents, so treat it as its own format rather than a version of 60-card Pauper. If it interests you, start with its official rules before building.

## Getting started with Pauper

1. **Pick an archetype you enjoy.** Aggro is the simplest to learn. Control and tempo reward practice.
2. **Check legality card by card.** Use an official legality source, and check the banned list.
3. **Start from a known shell.** Build around a few cards you know are strong, then fill in with cheap, efficient commons.
4. **Build a sideboard.** Competitive Pauper is usually played best-of-three, and the right sideboard cards win a lot of games.
5. **Play lots of games.** In a format where most cards are cheap, the skill of the player matters more than the cost of the deck.
